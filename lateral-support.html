Lateral Support

Part of: Shoulder is-subtopic-of
Lateral Support is a topic under Shoulder.
lateral-support
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Lateral support is a structural provision used in pavement and back‑fill design to counteract edge loads and side pressures, thereby lowering edge stresses that would otherwise cause edge cracking, lateral spreading, or excessive corner stresses and loss of load‑transfer efficiency[1]. In rigid‑pavement rehabilitation it is typically supplied by concrete shoulders whose joint spacing does not exceed 20 ft and whose width and extension into the pavement are governed by traffic levels, while in flowable‑backfill applications the supporting formwork must resist lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the fresh material with a minimum factor of safety of 1.3 and be placed in lifts no greater than four feet[1][2]. The effectiveness of this side restraint is quantified in the AASHTO rigid‑pavement design procedures by the load‑transfer coefficient J, which varies with observed joint performance, subgrade modulus and truck traffic to reflect the degree of lateral support provided[1].

Purpose and design objectives

Lateral support is needed to counteract edge loads on a pavement, thereby lowering the edge stresses that would otherwise develop and preventing damage such as edge cracking or lateral spreading. In rigid pavement rehabilitation, concrete shoulders may be provided so that the sides of the existing pavement receive lateral support, addressing the need to keep the pavement edges stable and prevent edge distress; for those concrete shoulders the joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft, ensuring a continuous supportive element along the pavement side. Lateral support also prevents excessive corner stresses and loss of load‑transfer efficiency at slab corners, because without adequate side restraint transverse joints cannot effectively share loads, leading to higher load‑transfer coefficients and potential performance degradation; the design therefore incorporates a load‑transfer coefficient J that increases when lateral support is poor. In the context of flowable backfill, lateral support is required to control the side pressure generated by unhardened material so that retaining walls, formwork and other structures remain stable during placement. The design must provide forms capable of sustaining fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the fresh fill with a minimum factor of safety of 1.3, and backfill shall be placed in lifts no greater than four feet unless otherwise approved, thereby keeping lateral pressures within the resisting capacity of the structure. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 8 (p. 163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

Lateral support supplied by pavement shoulders must function to carry edge loads so that edge stresses are significantly reduced compared with an unsupported condition, limiting damage in proportion to the applied stress level and helping preserve the structural capacity and serviceability of the pavement. For rigid pavements this functional requirement is achieved by providing concrete shoulders whose joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft. When only the first few feet adjacent to a distressed high‑type paved shoulder require repair, a minimum width of 3 ft of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulder may be placed and the remaining existing shoulder resurfaced with the same material. For widening projects the structure must extend into the shoulder by 2 ft when the current ADT is greater than 5 000 or the ADTT exceeds 500, while a 1‑ft extension is permitted when those values are 5 000 or less and 500 or less respectively, thereby preserving edge stability. Recycled‑asphalt bases may be used for shoulders, but they must have a minimum depth of 5 in on arterials and 4 in on collectors and local roads to ensure durability. The lateral support provided by the formwork and back‑fill placement must ensure that the forms can sustain the lateral fluid pressure generated by the total weight of unhardened flowable backfill with at least a 1.3 factor of safety. Backfill lifts are limited to four feet unless an exception is granted so that imposed lateral pressures never exceed the resisting capacity of the adjacent structure. Protection of foundation drains and other structural elements through a Class 4, Type A geotextile membrane further supports durability and serviceability objectives. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

Scope and applicability

In the design of Portland Cement Concrete (PCC) pavements, lateral support is appropriate when edge loads are present and the design calls for improving or providing shoulder structure; in that situation shoulders supply lateral support, which reduces edge stresses compared with an unsupported condition (Section 2.1). For rehabilitation of rigid pavements, lateral support may be provided by adding concrete shoulders, provided the joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft (Section 2.6). On Interstates, other limited‑access freeways and arterials where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is required, Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 6‑SP shoulders may be used, and a minimum width of three feet may be applied when only the first few feet of a high‑type paved shoulder are distressed. Where the existing paved shoulder merely needs cleaning and patching, a Type 7 shoulder is appropriate. For flexible pavements on collectors and local roads, shoulders may be constructed from the same surface material as the pavement structure or may be Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S, or 1‑SP shoulders (Section 2.6). In widening projects, if the current average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 or the average daily truck traffic exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder by two feet; otherwise it shall be extended by one foot (Section 2.6). Lateral support is also accounted for in rigid‑pavement design through the load transfer coefficient J, which reflects side support at slab corners and is appropriate when transverse joints are dowelled or tied concrete shoulders are present because these features reduce corner stress (Section 8.9). The selected J value shall be lower when the subgrade modulus k is high or truck percentages are low, and higher when k is low or truck percentages are high, as indicated by past deflection testing of load‑transfer efficiency; this guidance applies to all Department designs that use the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures (Section 8.9). [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 8 (p. 163)

Concrete shoulders that supply lateral support are only required for rigid‑pavement rehabilitation when desired, but when the existing shoulder is already high‑type paved and distress is limited to the first few feet next to the pavement, a minimum three‑foot width of a Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulder may be installed and the remaining shoulder resurfaced, making this narrower shoulder approach preferable to full concrete lateral support. Likewise, if only cleaning and patching of an existing paved shoulder is needed, a Type 7 shoulder is specified instead of providing concrete lateral support. For widening projects with current ADT of 5 000 or less (or ADTT of 500 or less), the required extension of the new pavement into the shoulder may be reduced to one foot and can even be omitted on arterials with less than 5 000 ADT if an optional shoulder type such as those in Section 1.3.B.1.d is used, providing another design alternative when traffic levels are low. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62)

Design inputs and requirements

The Pavement Policy Manual states that shoulders shall be provided and evaluated for Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements, and they must be designed to supply lateral support when edge loads are applied, thereby reducing edge stresses, in accordance with the relevant Publication 408 specifications and Federal‑aid Highway Program policy on pavement management. For rigid‑pavement rehabilitation projects, concrete shoulders may be provided to supply lateral support, and the joint spacing of those concrete shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft. The manual requires Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 6‑SP shoulders on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways and arterials where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is necessary, with a minimum width of three feet when only the first couple feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed; Type 7 shoulders are specified where only cleaning and patching of the existing paved shoulder is needed. When widening the pavement, if the current average daily traffic (ADT) exceeds 5 000 vehicles or the average daily truck traffic (ADTT) exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder area; otherwise it shall be extended one foot, and this extension may be eliminated on arterials with less than 5 000 ADT or on collectors and local roads if an optional shoulder type is used or a Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP, 7, or recycled shoulder is provided for the full width. Reclaimed asphalt concrete material may be used in the base courses of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 7 shoulders, and a cold‑mixed recycled base course shall have a minimum depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads. Lateral support for rigid pavements is governed by the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures through the load‑transfer coefficient J, which represents the support at slab corners; the applicable J values are taken from Table 8.2 that defines ranges for all Department designs and differ from those in the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures. The selection of a J value within the prescribed range depends on observed load‑transfer efficiencies from past deflection testing, with values toward the lower end used when efficiencies are good to normal and values toward the upper end used when efficiencies are low; higher J values should be used for lower subgrade modulus (k) values or higher truck percentages, while lower J values apply for higher k values or lower truck percentages. Under Publication 408 specifications, formwork for flowable backfill must be designed to sustain lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the unhardened material with a minimum factor of safety of 1.3. Additionally, flowable‑back‑fill lifts are limited to four feet unless a greater height is approved by the Representative, to prevent lateral pressures from exceeding the resisting capacity of adjacent structures. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 8 (p. 163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

Design methods and criteria

Lateral support in rigid pavement design is quantified by the load‑transfer coefficient J, which is incorporated into the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures to represent the support provided at slab corners [1]. The appropriate J value is chosen from the ranges listed in Table 8.2; a lower‑end selection is made when past deflection testing indicates good to normal transverse joint load‑transfer efficiency, whereas an upper‑end selection is required when that testing shows low efficiency, and poor estimated lateral support also calls for a higher J [1]. In addition, the selected J should be increased for lower subgrade modulus (k) values or higher percentages of truck traffic and decreased for higher k values or lower truck percentages [1]. For flowable‑backfill applications, the lateral‑support requirement is satisfied by designing the formwork to resist a lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the unhardened backfill with a minimum factor of safety of 1.3, as stipulated in Section 220 of the specifications [2]. The backfill must be placed in lifts that do not exceed four feet unless a Representative approves a greater lift height, thereby ensuring that lateral pressures remain within the resisting capacity of the structure [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

In the design of concrete shoulders that provide lateral support for a rehabilitated rigid pavement, the joint spacing shall be limited so that it does not exceed 20 ft, which directly governs the effectiveness of the lateral‑support function [1]. When a widening is contemplated, the required extension into the shoulder area depends on traffic levels: if the current ADT is greater than 5 000 or the ADTT is greater than 500, the widening structure must extend two feet into the shoulder area, whereas if the current ADT is 5 000 or less or the ADTT is 500 or less, the extension shall be one foot [1]. For rigid‑pavement design, the load‑transfer coefficient J, which accounts for the lateral support provided to the pavement at slab corners, must be selected from the departmental ranges given in Table 8.2, and the appropriate J value is guided by observed joint performance—lower‑end values are used when past deflection testing shows good to normal transfer efficiencies and higher‑end values are used when efficiencies are low [1]. Higher J‑values should be used for lower k‑values or higher truck percentages, while lower J‑values should be used for higher k‑values or lower truck percentages [1]. When flowable backfill is required, the formwork shall be designed to resist lateral fluid pressure that equals the total weight of the unhardened material and must incorporate at least a 1.3 factor of safety [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 8 (p. 163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

Structural section and dimensions

Concrete shoulders that serve as lateral support may be provided, but their joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft. When only the first few feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed, a minimum width of 3 feet for Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulders may be used, with the remainder resurfaced in kind. For pavement widening, the structure shall extend into the shoulder by 1 foot when ADT is 5000 or less (or ADTT is 500 or less) and by 2 feet when ADT is greater than 5000 (or ADTT is greater than 500). Recycled‑material base courses on shoulders require a minimum depth of 5 inches on arterials and 4 inches on collectors and local roads. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62)

For lateral support provided by shoulders, the joint spacing of concrete shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft; when a rigid‑pavement rehabilitation calls for concrete shoulders a minimum width of three feet of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP may be used and the remaining shoulder is resurfaced with the same material. Recycled asphalt base courses are permitted on shoulders, but must have a minimum depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads. The choice of shoulder type (e.g., Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP or Type 7) follows the project’s need for excavation, scarifying, or simple cleaning and patching. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62)

Materials and mixture design

Within the rehabilitation of rigid pavements, lateral support may be provided by concrete shoulders whose joints shall not exceed 20 ft in spacing, and when only part of a high‑type paved shoulder is distressed a minimum width of 3 ft of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulder may be installed while the remaining portion is resurfaced with the same surfacing as the new shoulder [1]. Reclaimed asphalt concrete material is permitted for recycled base courses on any shoulder type and may be placed either as an asphalt recycled base course for Type 6, 6‑F, or 6‑S shoulders or as an asphalt surface course for Type 7 shoulders; when a cold‑mixed recycled base course is used the minimum depth shall be 5 in on arterials and 4 in on collectors and local roads [1]. Lateral support specifications for flowable backfill must require the use of the type identified in Table A and prohibit any contact with aluminized materials or compounds; if the mix is adjusted on‑site it still must satisfy all Table A criteria [2]. The material properties that shall be verified include a slump meeting AASHTO T 119, air content measured by AASHTO T 152 (or T 196) without applying an aggregate correction factor, yield determined in accordance with AASHTO T 121/ASTM C136, and compressive strength tested according to PTM No. 604 using neoprene caps [2]. Placement temperature shall not be below 50 °F and the in‑place material must be maintained at no less than 50 °F for a minimum of 24 hours; for dikes the mix shall use reduced water to obtain at least a 3‑inch slump as directed by the Representative [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

For concrete shoulders used as lateral support on rigid pavements the joint spacing shall never exceed 20 ft [1]. When only the first couple feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed a minimum 3‑foot width of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulder may be provided and the remaining existing shoulder resurfaced with the same surfacing used on the new shoulder [1]. On Interstate and other limited‑access freeways where excavation or scarifying is necessary, Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulders shall be provided; Type 7 shoulders may be used when only cleaning and patching are required; flexible‑pavement shoulders may be provided with the same surface material as the pavement structure, or a Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulder may be provided [1]. When widening a roadway, if the current ADT is greater than 5 000 or the ADTT is greater than 500 the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder area by 2 feet; otherwise it shall be extended by 1 foot, and full‑width paving may be used in areas of heavy turning [1]. Reclaimed asphalt concrete material may be used in recycled base courses for Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or Type 7 shoulders, with a minimum depth of 5 inches on arterials and 4 inches on collectors and local roads [1]. Under Section 220.3 the flowable backfill used as lateral support must be the type indicated in Table A and may be adjusted on‑site only if it still meets the specified slump, air‑content, yield and compressive‑strength limits established by the QC plan [2]. Forms that retain the backfill are required to resist the lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the unhardened material with at least a factor of safety of 1.3 [2]. Lifts shall not exceed four feet unless the Representative approves otherwise [2]. Placement temperature is limited to a minimum of 50 °F for the material and must be maintained for at least 24 hours after placement, ensuring proper curing before the next lift or foot traffic [2]. Daily slump and yield testing (or at least every 200 cubic yards) together with compressive‑strength cylinders verify that the proportioned mix continues to satisfy structural, durability, constructability and performance requirements [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

Structural details and interfaces

In the 3R resurfacing, restoration, and rehabilitation procedures described in section 2.6 (Shoulders), concrete shoulders that are used to provide lateral support shall be laid with joint spacing that does not exceed 20 ft [1]. A minimum shoulder width of 3 ft may be provided for Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 6‑SP configurations [1]. This section does not prescribe any reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, or other connection details for those shoulders [1]. For the slab‑corner region addressed in section 8.9 (Load Transfer Coefficient, J), lateral support is provided primarily by dowelled transverse joints and, when present, tied concrete shoulders, both of which improve load transfer and keep the load‑transfer coefficient J low [1]. If dowelling or tied shoulders are lacking, indicating poor lateral support, the J value will increase to reflect higher corner stresses, with higher J values used for lower k values or higher truck percentages and lower J values applied when k values are high or truck percentages are low [1]. For flowable backfill, the formwork must be designed to sustain lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the unhardened material with a minimum factor of safety of 1.3 [2]. Lifts of flowable backfill shall not exceed 4 feet unless approved by the Representative [2]. A separation membrane of Class 4, Type A geotextile is required between foundation drains and the backfill to provide a transition that isolates the drainage system from the lateral pressures of the fill [2]. A pre‑formed drain no more than 2 inches thick shall be placed between the wall and the flowable fill, with outlets spaced at not more than 15 feet through the wall, thereby furnishing a load‑transfer connection that relieves pressure on the retaining structure [2]. Where needed, dikes constructed from the mix may be used as an integral part of the backfill to contain lateral pressures, serving as additional structural detail for lateral support [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 8 (p. 163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

In sections dealing with shoulders, concrete shoulders are provided to supply lateral support when rehabilitating rigid pavement, and the joint spacing of those shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft. For flexible pavements on collectors and local roads the shoulder should be of the same surface material as the pavement structure or a Type 1 series shoulder may be used; when widening, the new structure shall be extended into the shoulder by 2 ft if the current ADT is greater than 5 000 or ADTT is greater than 500, otherwise by 1 ft, and the entire shoulder may be paved with the same material at the District Executive’s discretion. Reclaimed asphalt concrete may be used in base courses for Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S shoulders or surface course for Type 7 shoulders, and a cold‑mixed recycled base on arterials must have a minimum depth of 5 inches (4 inches on collectors and local roads). The interfaces that provide lateral support—such as dowelled transverse joints and tied concrete shoulders—are accounted for by selecting an appropriate load transfer coefficient J in the AASHTO rigid‑pavement design procedures; when poor lateral support is estimated the J value will increase, so designers use a value toward the lower end of the prescribed ranges if past deflection testing shows good to normal load‑transfer efficiency and a value toward the upper end if testing indicates low efficiency. The chosen J should also reflect pavement stiffness and traffic composition, with higher J‑values used for lower k‑values or higher truck percentages and lower J‑values used for higher k‑values or lower truck percentages. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 8 (p. 163)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

When designing lateral support that involves flowable backfill, the forms must be sized to resist lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the unhardened material with at least a 1.3 factor of safety, and lifts should not exceed four feet unless specifically approved; drainage is protected by keeping base drain outlets clear of the backfill area, installing a pre‑formed drain no more than two inches thick between any retaining wall and the fill with outlet pipes spaced no more than fifteen feet apart, and using a Class 4, Type A geotextile membrane to shield foundation drains. Moisture control requires that flowable backfill never be placed through flowing water, that any material damaged by rain or runoff be removed and replaced, and that subsequent lifts be delayed until surface bleed water has dissipated. Climate considerations mandate avoiding placement when the material temperature is below 50°F or when air or surface temperatures are 40°F or lower, maintaining the in‑place backfill at no less than 50°F for a minimum of twenty‑four hours, and protecting the finished surface from frost, erosion, and damage with approved covers. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189)

In rehabilitating rigid pavements without widening, concrete shoulders may be used for lateral support but the joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft [1]. Where only the first couple feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed, a minimum width of 3 feet of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulder may be provided and the remainder resurfaced with the same material as the new shoulder [1]. When widening is required, the extension of the new structure into the existing shoulder depends on traffic; if the current average daily traffic (ADT) is greater than 5 000 or the average daily truck traffic (ADTT) exceeds 500, the structure shall be extended 2 feet into the shoulder, otherwise it shall be extended 1 foot [1]. Full out‑to‑out paving is strongly recommended in areas of heavy turning movement [1]. These geometric dimensions and traffic‑based thresholds constitute the accessibility, safety and user considerations that must be incorporated into any lateral support provision [1]. For flowable backfill, the formwork providing lateral support must be designed to sustain the lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of the unhardened material with a minimum factor of safety of 1.3 [2]. Backfill lifts shall not exceed four feet unless approved by the Representative [2]. A pre‑formed drain no more than two inches thick shall be placed between any wall and the fill, and outlet openings shall be spaced at intervals not more than fifteen feet through the wall [2]. The forms and backfill also have to protect foundation drains and other utilities from intrusion or mis‑alignment, and each lift must harden sufficiently to support foot traffic before the next lift is placed [2].
