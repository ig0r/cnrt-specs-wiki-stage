Stone Matrix Asphalt

Part of: Asphalt Concrete (HMA) is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) is a premium wearing‑course mixture designed to deliver a tough, stable surface that resists rutting and cracking on high‑speed, heavily trafficked highways such as Interstates. Its performance stems from a gap‑graded aggregate skeleton that creates stone‑to‑stone contact for structural strength, which is filled with a rich mortar binder—typically a fiber or polymer‑modified, high‑asphalt‑content matrix—that provides durability and moisture resistance while meeting minimum voids in the mineral aggregate and target air‑void levels. By carefully selecting durable aggregates, controlling binder content, and evaluating moisture susceptibility and draindown sensitivity during mix design, SMA achieves the longevity required for pavements carrying more than 30 million ESALs [1].

Material purpose and applicability

Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) is a tough, stable, rut‑resistant mixture that relies on stone‑to‑stone contact to provide strength and a rich mortar binder to provide durability. In the pavement system it serves as the wearing course, delivering a high‑durability surface capable of resisting rutting and cracking on heavily trafficked routes such as Interstates, Interstate look‑alike highways, and other high‑speed freeways. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Composition and characteristics

Stone Matrix Asphalt is defined by a gap‑graded aggregate skeleton that creates stone‑to‑stone contact for structural strength, which is then filled with a rich mortar binder composed of a high‑content, fiber or polymer‑modified asphalt matrix and mineral filler to give the mix its durability and rut resistance. The design calls for selecting durable aggregates, achieving a gradation that meets or exceeds minimum voids in the mineral aggregate (VMA), and choosing an asphalt content that yields the target air‑void level while also addressing moisture susceptibility and binder draindown. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Variations in the aggregate type, gradation, binder content, or stabilizer used in Stone Matrix Asphalt directly influence its stone‑to‑stone contact, void structure and moisture sensitivity, which together govern the mix’s strength, rut resistance and durability. For a wearing course (section 5.14), a gap‑graded aggregate paired with a fiber or polymer‑modified, high‑asphalt‑content matrix is required to achieve the intended performance, while meeting or exceeding the minimum VMA ensures that the selected binder amount can attain the desired air void level. Selecting durable aggregates, appropriate gradation, sufficient asphalt content and evaluating draindown sensitivity are essential steps to obtain a tough, stable SMA mixture. When reclaimed ground tire rubber is introduced into a gap‑graded asphalt mix (section 5.16), the crumb‑rubber modifier changes both the binder and the aggregate structure that are characteristic of Stone Mastic Asphalt. If the rubber particles make up at least 15 % by weight of the total blend, they swell during specialized mixing and heating, producing a binder with higher viscosity and permitting a higher binder content and thicker film thickness; this in turn enhances durability similarly to polymer‑modified binders. The gap‑graded aggregate, which lacks many fine fractions (minus No. 8 or finer) and therefore contains relatively larger air voids, promotes stone‑to‑stone contact that is central to SMA’s rutting resistance. Because of these compositional and processing differences, AR‑GG mixtures may be considered for the same applications as conventional SMA mixes. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–128)

Key material properties

In the wearing course defined in section 5.14, the performance of Stone Matrix Asphalt relies on a combination of physical, mechanical and chemical characteristics. The aggregate must be gap‑graded so that stone‑on‑stone contact is achieved and the required voids in the mineral aggregate are met or exceeded; this permits the selected asphalt content to reach the desired air‑void level. A high‑asphalt‑content matrix, which is often stabilized with fibers or modified with polymer, provides a rich mortar binder that contributes to durability, toughness, stability and rut resistance. In addition, the blend must be evaluated for moisture susceptibility and for asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity because these chemical interactions affect long‑term performance. According to section 5.16, the physical properties of the binder and mixture structure are also critical. A high‑viscosity rubberized binder—higher than that of conventional neat binders—is required, and it must satisfy the physical requirements set out in ASTM D6114, Table 1, Type II. The gap‑graded aggregate skeleton yields a relatively large air‑void content, which allows a thicker film thickness and greater binder content. The binder typically contains rubber particles up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm) in size, contributing to its viscosity and durability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–128)

In section 5.14 Stone Matrix Asphalt Wearing Course the properties of SMA are characterized through a five‑step mix‑design procedure. First, proper aggregate materials are selected and an aggregate gradation that yields stone‑on‑stone contact is verified; the chosen gradation must meet or exceed the minimum voids in mineral aggregate (VMA) requirement or otherwise permit use of the minimum binder content. Next, the asphalt content is adjusted so that the target air‑void level is achieved, after which moisture susceptibility and asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity are evaluated. In section 5.16 Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded Wearing Course the performance characteristics of a Stone Mastic Asphalt‑type mixture are classified by the specifications applied to its binder; for an asphalt rubber gap‑graded mix that may be used in place of SMA, the rubberized binder must satisfy the physical requirements set out in ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II. Compliance with this standard provides measurable criteria for viscosity, stiffness and other binder properties that define the mixture’s durability and resistance to rutting, and such AR‑GG mixtures are generally considered for the same applications as SMA mixtures. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–128)

Behavior and mechanisms

Stone Matrix Asphalt behaves as a tough, stable, rut‑resistant mixture; its stone‑to‑stone contact supplies the structural strength while the rich mortar binder adds durability. Its performance in wet conditions must be verified by evaluating moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity to ensure stability when exposed to water. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

In the wearing course defined in section 5.14, Stone Matrix Asphalt’s performance is governed by a physical mechanism of stone‑to‑stone contact that supplies load‑bearing strength, a chemical mechanism where a rich mortar binder ensures durability and adhesion, and a mechanical mechanism provided by a fiber or polymer‑modified high‑asphalt‑content matrix that resists deformation; the mix design must also consider moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity which can affect long‑term stability. The gap‑graded aggregate structure described in section 5.16 promotes stone‑to‑stone contact by creating a high void content that enables a thick, viscous binder film to surround the stones, and the rubberized binder, containing swollen crumb‑rubber particles up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm) and having a higher viscosity than conventional binders, provides a thicker coating and greater load‑distribution capacity, mechanisms that are similar to those of polymer‑modified binders; generally, AR‑GG mixtures with at least 15% rubber by weight may be considered for the same applications as SMA, so these physical (aggregate arrangement), chemical (rubber‑induced viscosity increase), and mechanical (thick film and higher binder content) effects together control SMA performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–128)

Durability and deterioration

Stone Matrix Asphalt’s long‑term performance can be limited by moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity, so these factors must be evaluated during mix design; achieving the required stone‑on‑stone contact, adequate voids in the mineral aggregate and proper air‑void level helps mitigate those durability concerns, and the mix should not be placed over existing pavement problems because SMA is not intended as a stopgap solution. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Stone Matrix Asphalt’s resistance to deterioration is enhanced when the mix uses durable, gap‑graded aggregates that achieve stone‑on‑stone contact, a high asphalt content rich mortar binder, and a fiber or polymer‑modified binder that together satisfy the required voids in the mineral aggregate and target air‑void level; these material choices also allow evaluation of moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity. Conversely, susceptibility to damage rises when the pavement is exposed to moisture without proper draindown control, when underlying pavements have problems, or when SMA must be placed in areas that demand extensive handwork or frequent paver adjustments such as intersections. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Material interactions and compatibility

Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) achieves its performance through a combination of durable aggregates that are arranged to create stone‑to‑stone contact for structural strength and a rich mortar binder that is often fiber‑ or polymer‑modified and contains a high asphalt content to provide durability. For the SMA wearing course described in section 5.14, the aggregate gradation must meet or exceed the minimum voids in the mineral aggregate (VMA) so that the higher binder demand can be satisfied, and the mix is evaluated for moisture susceptibility and asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity to verify adequate binder‑aggregate adhesion and resistance to stripping. In the context of the asphalt‑rubber gap‑graded wearing course described in section 5.16, SMA behaves similarly to an AR‑GG mixture: its gap‑graded, high‑void structure permits the use of a high‑viscosity binder, producing a thicker film around the aggregate and delivering durability comparable to polymer‑modified binders; when crumb rubber modifier is incorporated, the rubber content must be at least 15 % by weight of the total blend. Generally, AR‑GG mixtures may be considered for the same applications as SMA mixtures. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–128)

The guidance for Stone Matrix Asphalt notes that a key compatibility issue is its moisture susceptibility, requiring that the mixture’s moisture susceptibility and asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity be evaluated during design; it also cautions against placing SMA where there are any issues with the underlying pavements, implying potential debonding problems. No other risks such as incompatible binder modifiers or additives, RAP/RAS stiffness, deleterious aggregates, fuel or solvent contamination, dissimilar layers, or temperature susceptibility are mentioned in this section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Influence of production and placement

Long‑term performance of SMA hinges on controlling construction variations that affect the stone‑to‑stone framework and binder durability. The mixture is built from properly selected aggregates, a gap‑graded gradation that yields stone‑on‑stone contact, a VMA that meets or exceeds minimum requirements, and an asphalt content that provides the desired air‑void level while also passing moisture‑susceptibility and draindown tests; deviations in any of these steps can undermine strength and durability. In addition, the source advises avoiding SMA where a lot of handwork or many changes to the paver configuration are required—such as at intersections—and where there are any issues with the underlying pavements, because those conditions compromise the premium mix’s intended rut resistance and longevity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Selection, specification, and improvement

Specification of SMA is limited to projects where the paving area reaches at least 50,000 square yards, and quantities near 100,000 square yards or more make it a good option; SMA is considered for Interstates, Interstate look‑alike highways and high‑speed freeways, and for existing roadways with greater than 30 million ESALs. It should be avoided in locations that require extensive handwork or frequent paver adjustments such as intersections, and it should not be used where underlying pavement problems exist because SMA is a premium mix rather than a stop‑gap solution. Selection follows the five procedural steps of choosing proper aggregates, achieving a stone‑on‑stone gradation that meets or exceeds minimum VMA, using an asphalt content that provides the desired air void level, and confirming acceptable moisture susceptibility and binder draindown sensitivity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

In the Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing course (section 5.14), performance can be enhanced by using a fiber‑or polymer‑modified binder together with a high asphalt‑content matrix, selecting durable aggregates that achieve stone‑on‑stone contact, and adding the mineral filler in the prescribed manner while meeting or exceeding the minimum VMA requirement; the chosen gradation and asphalt content must then be adjusted to obtain the target air‑void level and the mixture should be evaluated for moisture susceptibility and draindown sensitivity. Quality control is reinforced by conducting a 100‑ton test strip, employing a fiber‑mixing machine, and limiting SMA specifications to projects of at least 50,000 square yards (preferably 100,000 sq yd or more) where the mix design can be fully implemented. Alternatively, in an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded wearing course (section 5.16), reclaimed ground tire rubber may be incorporated as a crumb‑rubber modifier into the binder at a level of at least 15 % by weight of the total blend; the resulting rubberized binder must satisfy the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II and contains rubber particles up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm) that give it higher viscosity than conventional neat binders. Because this high‑viscosity rubberized binder can be placed with the high void content of gap‑graded aggregates, a higher binder content and thicker film thickness are achievable, providing performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders and helping to mitigate rutting and cracking in SMA pavements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–128)

Performance evaluation and implications

Laboratory evaluation of Stone Matrix Asphalt includes testing its moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity, while field evidence is gathered through a Demonstration Placement (PDA) as specified in Item Number 0419-0000 and by constructing a 100‑ton test strip. Long‑term performance considerations are reflected in the recommendation to use SMA on existing roadways that will experience greater than 30 million equivalent single axle loads (ESALs). [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

Stone Matrix Asphalt’s toughness and rut resistance stem from its stone‑to‑stone contact framework combined with a rich mortar binder, which together give the pavement high strength and durability. Because these properties are achieved only with a gap‑graded aggregate and a fiber or polymer‑modified, high‑asphalt‑content matrix, the mix design must follow five steps that include selecting durable aggregates, attaining stone‑on‑stone contact, meeting or exceeding minimum VMA, choosing an asphalt content to reach the target air void level, and evaluating moisture susceptibility and draindown. Although SMA is generally more expensive than a dense‑graded Superpave due to its higher binder and specialized additives, it can be cost‑effective for large projects—specifically when at least 50,000 square yards are placed (with quantities near 100,000 or more square yards being especially suitable) or on high‑traffic routes exceeding 30 million ESALs. Construction also requires special equipment such as a fiber machine, a 100‑ton test strip, and distinct handling of mineral filler, so SMA should be avoided in areas that demand extensive handwork or where underlying pavement problems exist. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)
