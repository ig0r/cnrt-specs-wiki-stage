Pavement Condition Survey

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Pavement Condition Survey is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The Pavement Condition Survey is a systematic evaluation of an existing roadway segment that records International Roughness Index values and documents visible surface distresses—such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling or faulting, and drainage problems—while also noting premature failures and integrating the segment’s traffic history and structural capacity to inform design criteria[1][2]. Conducted through annual automated videologging (STAMPP) supplemented by manual inspections per Publication 336 (or 343 for concrete), the survey quantifies distress severity and extent against defined thresholds (for example, thirty percent of a segment with two or more low‑severity distresses triggers microsurfacing and combined deviations exceeding twenty percent mandate full replacement) to populate treatment matrices that direct routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction actions, including warranty‑driven requirements such as rut‑depth checks with a twelve‑foot straight edge[1][2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The Pavement Condition Survey evaluates the existing physical condition of a pavement segment by recording its surface and underlying characteristics at the time of inspection. It captures International Roughness Index values and documents visible distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting, while also noting drainage problems, shoulder conditions and geometric features. The survey incorporates the segment’s history and traffic data to assess structural capacity and uses Maintenance Functional Codes to guide design criteria. The investigation should answer what the measured IRI values are, which distress types are present and how severe each is, whether any distress appears premature, and what observations can be made about the level of each distress and its underlying causes. It also must determine how the condition interacts with surrounding site characteristics, how the pavement has deteriorated uniformly since the most recent resurfacing, rehabilitation or reconstruction, and what the rate and pattern of deterioration are at the segment level. Further questions include which treatment matrix or cycle‑chart outcome is appropriate—routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction—and which rehabilitation or replacement projects are required based on needs and the minimum treatment. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions, the Pavement Condition Survey evaluates the distress condition of asphalt‑concrete pavement segments by automatically identifying, measuring and rating each distress in accordance with Publication 336, focusing on flushing, potholes, cracking and rutting. The investigation must determine whether the surveyed segment meets the performance‑criteria limits set out in Table A, for example checking if thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, which would trigger microsurfacing. It also must verify whether combined deviations exceed a remedial‑action area greater than twenty percent of the segment surface, which would require removal and replacement. Additional questions include whether rut depth exceeds acceptable levels, as verified with a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width, and whether repair patches are spaced less than one hundred feet apart, in which case they are combined into one continuous repair. After an extended‑season paving operation, the survey evaluates the condition of the newly placed asphalt concrete pavement and its workmanship by looking for fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination. The investigation is intended to answer whether the material and construction meet the Department’s acceptance criteria or if remedial action is required. [1][2]

The field inspection component of a Pavement Condition Survey records International Roughness Index values, specific distress types and their severity, and whether any distress is premature; these data quantify existing deficiencies and estimate the percentage of the surface that will require patching. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions for asphalt concrete pavement (Section 496), annual automated and manual condition surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336 identify, measure, and report pavement distresses, allowing the Department to compare each segment’s distress levels against the limits in Table A. When a year‑5 survey determines that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the findings trigger microsurfacing or another treatment approved by the Department; if combined deviations beyond the Table A thresholds total more than twenty percent of the surface area of any segment, the results mandate full removal and replacement of that segment. The condition data are cross‑checked with the treatment strategy suggested in the annual State of the Interstate report and with the forecasted treatments from the Pavement Asset Management System, ensuring that the most cost‑effective rehabilitation or reconstruction option is chosen, safety considerations are addressed, and preservation guidelines are followed during design and life‑cycle cost analysis. The survey provides a detailed picture of existing distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems, which is essential for selecting an appropriate rehabilitation strategy, sizing layers, choosing stabilization methods, and ensuring each distress type is properly addressed in design and maintenance plans. By linking the condition information with traffic loading (ESAL) and structural data such as FWD results, planners can prioritize more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction when structural capacity is insufficient, allowing funding and work to be spread over multiple years. The Condition Survey together with any layer analysis yields the effective structural capacity (Deff or SNeff); engineers compare this measured capacity to the capacity required for future traffic (Df or SNf) to determine which overlay type is suitable, for example ruling out a bonded PCC overlay on a severely cracked concrete slab. For jointed plain concrete pavements the survey provides STAMPP condition data that feed the calculation of Effective Existing Thickness; accurate input values—sometimes verified through a field view by the District PME/PM—allow DARWin to compute the actual asphalt‑overlay thickness required rather than relying solely on a structural number, and documenting how these inputs were obtained is essential for the overlay design submission. Annual automated Pavement Condition Surveys collect condition data such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting; the resulting matrices match these distress levels to one of four treatment categories—routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction—and thereby dictate which actions should be scheduled. For routine maintenance the survey triggers cyclic crack sealing every 3 to 5 years for asphalt surfaces and joint sealing every 5 years for concrete pavements, while preservation projects are limited to situations where patching does not exceed ten percent of the pavement area because larger patches are generally not cost‑effective for preservation. When surveyed repair areas are less than one hundred feet apart, the data direct a single continuous repair, and rut‑depth measurements taken with a twelve‑foot straight edge further inform severity assessments, guiding timely planning, design choices, maintenance scheduling, and rehabilitation actions. A spring‑time manual survey required after extended‑season paving for Superpave mixture design examines fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination; the Department uses these findings to decide whether the work is accepted or remedial action is required, thereby directing maintenance and rehabilitation efforts to the specific distresses identified. Condition Survey results stored in the Roadway Management System give planners a snapshot of each segment’s distress level that can be matched to project priorities and the minimum treatment required; by combining those condition data with pavement history and traffic counts engineers evaluate the existing structural capacity before selecting rehabilitation or replacement options, and Maintenance Functional Codes linked to the survey information then guide the appropriate design criteria for any chosen treatment. The annual STAMPP pavement condition survey—covering one hundred percent of the National Highway System and fifty percent of non‑NHS roadways—feeds detailed distress data into PennDOT’s RMS, where recommended treatments are generated for each segment; because a surface‑treatment recommendation is normally a good indication that surfacing (typically an asphalt overlay) is needed, the survey results guide rehabilitation design decisions and must be attached to Federal oversight design submissions. The database also produces summaries of specific distresses such as shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking, which county maintenance staff use to pinpoint high‑risk items for timely repair, and condition inventories for guide rails and drainage features allow engineers to identify and schedule needed maintenance actions. Scope and applicability

The Pavement Condition Survey is applied to any existing roadway when a recent assessment of its surface condition is required, including asphalt‑concrete pavements evaluated according to Publication 336, continuously reinforced concrete and unpaved roads covered by Publication 343, or municipal streets for which alternative distress procedures such as MicroPaver™ are employed. It records the severity of typical distresses—including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies—and is considered essential for selecting Full‑Depth Reclamation projects across these pavement types and material conditions. When an existing Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) is being evaluated for an asphalt‑concrete overlay, the Condition Survey Method requires STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and may also require a field view by the District PME/PM to obtain accurate values for the existing pavement‑condition variables. Under the STAMPP program, Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) are applied annually to all Interstate roadway sections and record surface‑layer distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting on both asphalt‑surfaced and jointed concrete pavements. The STAMPP surveys cover 100 % of National Highway System roadways each year and 50 % of the non‑NHS network on a rotating two‑year cycle, also supporting condition inventories for shoulders, guide rails and drainage features. Conditions that are not reliably captured by the STAMPP automated surveys—premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate‑related issues such as Vanport Limestone or alkali‑silica reaction, and ramp pavements—require additional field verification. The Pavement Condition Survey prescribed in Section 496 of the specifications is intended for asphalt‑concrete (flexible) pavements that are covered by a 60‑month warranty. It uses automated surveys, per Publication 336, to identify and measure surface distresses such as low‑severity and medium‑severity cracks, flushing, potholes, rutting and fatigue cracking across the pavement layers, with manual surveys required for flushing and potholes and rut depth verified by a 12‑foot straight edge. The survey also evaluates site conditions that may affect performance, including base‑condition deficiencies, traffic loads exceeding design ESALs, routine departmental maintenance activities, destructive procedures, uncontrolled forces of nature, and accident‑related damage. The Pavement Condition Survey, when performed as a simple visual walk‑through, is limited to qualitative observations and cannot supply the quantitative data required for detailed design; consequently a more comprehensive scoping field view that incorporates input from design, construction, maintenance units and the District Pavement Management Engineer is recommended before projects involving rehabilitation or reconstruction that will require a Life Cycle Cost Analysis or fall under Federal Oversight [1]. Because the survey records only surface distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems, it does not quantify subgrade strength or structural stiffness; therefore investigations such as Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer measurements together with material sampling should be undertaken when design decisions depend on the in‑situ strength of supporting layers, for example prior to full‑depth reclamation [1]. The automated STAMPP program provides annual data on a predefined list of surface distresses but may miss premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate‑related issues such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction, and it does not assess ramp pavement condition; field visual inspections or targeted tests such as skid‑resistance testing for suspected Vanport Limestone sections are therefore needed to supplement the survey results [1]. STAMPP coverage is limited to 100 % of National Highway System segments each year and only 50 % of non‑NHS network annually, with the remainder inspected every two years; because the condition data represent a snapshot taken on the survey day and remain unchanged after any maintenance or rehabilitation until a later resurvey, current detailed condition information required for design, especially after recent work or when traffic or history data are outdated, should be obtained through field viewing and direct testing [1]. The Roadway Management System database also limits Pavement Condition Survey data by availability, detail and timeliness; traffic counts may be several years old and pavement‑history layers can be missed due to recording lapses, so complementary investigations are required whenever up‑to‑date traffic or historical layer information is essential for design decisions [1]. The Condition Survey used under the 60‑month warranty is limited to identifying and quantifying surface distresses through automated or manual surveys and does not reveal subgrade or material deficiencies; when fatigue cracking or other indications of unacceptable base condition appear, the Department requires six‑inch diameter cores to be drilled, measured and tested in accordance with PTM No. 715, AASHTO T‑209, PTM No. 702 and PTM No. 739 before any remediation is approved [2]. If cumulative traffic counts indicate that estimated equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life, the survey alone cannot justify rutting repairs and additional investigation of loading effects is needed [2]. The warranty sets a trigger that when the last performance survey (year 5) determines that more than 30 % of a segment area has two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing must be taken; however any underlying cause beyond surface distress—such as base failure or overload—must be examined with core sampling or traffic analysis [2]. Rut depth measurements required for the warranty are verified using a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent, illustrating that quantitative field measurement is employed in addition to the visual survey when rutting is suspected [2].

Existing information and records

The scoping team should first gather the project limits defined by segments or mileposts and then review pavement history and existing condition data—including International Roughness Index values, identified distress types and their severity, and any indication that distress is premature—along with an estimate of patching expressed as a percent of the pavement area [1]. The review must also consider the treatment strategy suggested in the annual State of the Interstate report and its justification if a different approach is proposed, the forecasted conditions and recommended treatments from the Pavement Asset Management System for the letting date, any crash‑cluster information, pavement cross‑slope details, and traffic characteristics such as average daily traffic and the percentage of trucks [1]. When evaluating a full‑depth reclamation candidate, a reliable estimate of future traffic loading should be obtained and the most recent pavement condition survey performed according to PennDOT Publication 336 or 343 (or comparable distress procedures such as MicroPaver) that documents current distresses and their causes must be reviewed [1]. In‑situ testing results—including Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements, dynamic cone penetrometer data, and lightweight deflectometer values—should be included to assess subgrade strength before rehabilitation, and detailed sampling information covering the number, locations, depths, sizes, techniques, and handling of material samples must also be examined [1]. Records of pavement distress and any layer‑analysis data should be collected; if a remaining‑life evaluation is to be used, past traffic data that describe historic loading should also be reviewed [1]. STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System must be obtained, and a field view by the District PME/PM may be necessary to verify accurate values for certain existing‑condition input variables [1]. Each spring the Bureau of Maintenance Operations produces the State of the Interstate report, which provides analysis of pavement condition data that defines the existing condition for each segment and project, includes Pavement and Bridge Condition Performance Measures, and delineates project limits based on the most recent resurfacing, rehabilitation or reconstruction to ensure a uniform pavement type and age [1]. The analyst should gather the annual Automated Pavement Condition Survey data from STAMPP—including cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting—along with any prior Falling Weight Deflectometer results that describe pavement and subgrade properties and concrete joint efficiency, skid‑friction test reports for sections flagged by BOMO, and Equivalent Single Axle Load traffic loading figures used for priority setting [1]. The review must also incorporate the Pavement Condition/Treatment matrices linking condition ratings to recommended actions, records of past routine maintenance where crack sealing should be done every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing every 5 years, plus preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction activities such as patching, micro‑surfacing or overlay placement, noting that patching greater than 10 percent of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects [1]. Any documented special conditions—mid‑slab cracking, aggregate‑related issues, or ramp pavements not captured by STAMPP—should be considered to ensure a complete performance assessment [1]. The Roadway Management System’s stored data for each highway segment should be consulted, which includes pavement history, current condition ratings, roughness measurements, traffic counts and vehicle classifications, friction characteristics, divider type, lane count, and urban or rural coding; Maintenance Functional Codes should be referenced to align design criteria with maintenance policies [1]. The Pavement Condition Survey should examine the prior performance survey results that the Department conducts annually and any supplemental manual surveys for flushing and potholes [2]. When required, rut‑depth measurements shall be verified using a 12 foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent [2]. Traffic classification counts should be used to verify that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values, and all of these data are provided to the Contractor as part of the performance survey results and traffic count records [2].

The existing Roadway Management System records are insufficient for detailed design because they provide only IRI values, distress types, severity and any premature failures, while patching is expressed merely as a percent of pavement area and ancillary data such as project limits, traffic counts and crash‑cluster locations may be incomplete or inconsistent. Because many records lack a recent pavement condition survey, an up‑to‑date documentation of all visible distresses and their severity must be obtained to resolve uncertainty about the mechanisms causing rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes or drainage problems that are needed for selecting an appropriate rehabilitation strategy. The State of the Interstate report assumes uniform deterioration across the limits of the most recent construction, leaving it uncertain whether that assumed loss matches actual distress patterns, which specific deficiencies exist, and whether performance measures accurately reflect current conditions; these ambiguities must be resolved through an on‑site condition survey before finalizing scope. Annual STAMPP surveys may omit or misrepresent certain distress types, such as premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate‑related issues including Vanport Limestone concerns and Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement condition, creating uncertainty about those sections and any associated skid‑resistance issues that would require BOMO testing. The RMS snapshot does not change when maintenance or rehabilitation work is performed after the last survey, so field investigation must verify that recent maintenance is reflected, update traffic counts that may be several years old, and confirm missing or unrecorded pavement layers such as surface‑treatment build‑ups or lapses in history recording. Guide‑rail condition data collected by nonprofessional temporary staff and a drainage inventory that excludes features with clear spans of 8 ft or greater require professional field checks, and because non‑NHS roads are only surveyed every two years, temporal gaps must be filled by the Condition Survey. Field investigation must also resolve uncertainties in the recorded distress percentages and areas that trigger warranty actions, for example confirming whether a segment truly exceeds the 30 % of area with two or more low‑severity distresses identified in the year‑5 survey. It must verify whether combined deviations surpass the >20 % remedial‑action‑area limit that would require full segment replacement, and determine if the actual spacing between repair locations is within the <100 ft distance that mandates a continuous repair. Rut‑depth values measured with a 12 foot straight edge across the lane width must be checked to establish severity and extent, and when fatigue cracking or other signs of possible base failure appear, core samples and laboratory test results are needed to ascertain whether the base condition meets the specified density, asphalt content and gradation criteria before assigning responsibility for remediation. Visual inspection and condition assessment

A Pavement Condition Survey must record all visible signs of deterioration on the existing surface and should specifically evaluate the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and any drainage deficiencies that could affect performance [1]. The annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys required by STAMPP record observable distresses including cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting, as well as premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements [1]. The visual distresses most affecting performance are noted as rutting, fatigue cracking, and shoulder drop‑off, and the condition of related guide‑rail and drainage features is also captured [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty specified in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020, the condition survey must identify flushing, potholes and cracking—including fatigue cracks and medium‑severity cracks—and must measure rutting by checking rut depth with a 12 ft straight edge across the lane [2]. A medium‑severity crack is treated as a deviation affecting six inches on either side of the crack for its full length [2]. Remedial action is required when the year‑5 performance survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses [2]. During the spring evaluation and manual survey required for extended‑season paving, the Department examines the pavement for visible distresses such as fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination [2]. These items constitute the primary defects that a Pavement Condition Survey should document to assess material performance and workmanship [2].

The Pavement Condition Survey classifies observed distresses according to the list defined for asphalt and jointed concrete pavements (plain or reinforced) in Publication 336 [1]. The survey must also identify each distress type—such as flushing, potholes, rutting or cracks—and assign a severity rating using the same Publication 336 procedures [2]. Low‑severity distresses are counted toward a 30 % segment‑area limit, while a medium‑severity crack is treated as affecting six inches on either side of the crack for its full length [2]. Extent is quantified by measuring area percentages within each defined half‑mile Segment (or partial Segment) and, where required, rut depth is verified with a 12 foot straight edge across the lane width to determine both severity and extent [2]. Any repair locations spaced less than 100 feet apart are combined into one continuous repair [2]. Severity and extent are recorded during the semi‑automated video‑log field survey as prescribed by Publication 336 [1]. The precise location of each distress is documented using Pennsylvania’s Location Reference System, which uniquely identifies every roadway segment in the RMS database [1]. Results for every Segment are reported with the segment location, distress type, measured severity and extent, and an indication of whether performance limits have been met or exceeded, and this documentation must be completed within fourteen days of survey completion [2].

Field measurements and testing

A Pavement Condition Survey begins with a full foot‑walk field inspection during which International Roughness Index values are recorded to quantify surface roughness and distress types and their severity are documented [1]. The visual distress component follows the procedures set out in Publication 336, Publication 343, or comparable methods such as MicroPaver™, documenting the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems, thereby indicating both current condition and underlying damage mechanisms [1]. For projects covered by a 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty (Section 496), automated surveys are used to identify and quantify distresses such as cracks, potholes, flushing and rutting in accordance with Publication 336, while manual surveys are employed when flushing or potholes require closer inspection [2]. Rut depth is measured by placing a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to assess the severity and extent of ruts [2]. Traffic classification counts are performed to verify that calculated Equivalent Single Axle Load values remain within the contract’s projected estimates, providing a basis for prioritizing work based on cumulative axle stresses [1][2]. Skid‑resistance testing quantifies surface friction so that sections with verified skid‑friction problems can be identified [1]. Structural capacity is evaluated nondestructively with a Falling Weight Deflectometer, which measures the stiffness of existing pavement layers and subgrade deflection to infer structural characteristics, joint efficiency, and the quantity of concrete patching required [1]. Subgrade strength is further assessed by a Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, which records penetration resistance, and by a Light Weight Deflectometer, which provides an additional measure of subgrade stiffness [1]. The contractor may apply any nondestructive testing methods after notifying the Department to monitor pavement condition during the warranty period [2]. For pre‑placement evaluation, pavement cores may be extracted to directly measure in‑place density, indicating whether the asphalt layer has achieved the required compaction; when core extraction is not feasible, an optimum‑rolling pattern derived from roller data serves as a nondestructive inference of density [2]. An automated videologging survey under the STAMPP program supplies a nondestructive visual assessment that records distress types such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting [1]. Manual performance surveys conducted per Publication 336 record visual distresses including fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination to assess overall pavement condition and the need for remedial action [2].

Sampling and laboratory investigation

The STAMPP pavement condition survey records distress data for both asphalt and jointed‑concrete pavements, whether plain or reinforced, thereby requiring that these surface materials be included in the sampling effort [1]. Sampling locations for the STAMPP program are defined by Pennsylvania’s Location Reference System, which uniquely identifies each roadway segment in the RMS; coverage is set at 100 % of National Highway System routes each year and 50 % of non‑NHS routes each year, with a complete non‑NHS inventory achieved every two years [1]. The program does not prescribe specific sampling depths or per‑segment quantities for these surface surveys [1]. When performance surveys reveal fatigue cracking or other signs of an unacceptable base condition, the Department directs the Contractor to obtain a series of 6‑inch diameter cores from the affected area; each core’s depth is measured and the samples are tested according to PTM No. 715, AASHTO T‑209, PTM No. 702 and PTM No. 739 to assess voids, asphalt content and gradation of the underlying layer [2]. The sampling locations for coring are limited to the identified distress area and are chosen by the Department, while the number of cores is taken “as necessary” to establish condition [2]. The frequency of such coring is triggered by detection of relevant distresses in annual performance surveys or any additional survey that the Department deems needed [2]. In addition, the Contractor may perform nondestructive testing of any pavement course at any time during the warranty period, provided a seven‑day notice is given to the District Executive [2].

Structural and functional performance

In selecting full‑depth reclamation projects, the pavement condition survey is required to be supplemented by in‑situ testing that captures structural capacity, including Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements which provide deflection‑related stiffness information, and subgrade strength tests such as the dynamic cone penetrometer and the light weight deflectometer. The survey must also determine existing layer thicknesses because appropriate thickness is critical for meeting traffic demands, while visual distress data on rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues inform functional condition. Under the pavement testing criteria, skid‑resistance testing is performed to quantify friction, Falling Weight Deflectometer testing supplies deflection data used to derive subgrade and pavement characteristics as well as concrete joint efficiency, and an annual automated pavement condition survey (videologging) records functional distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting. The roadway management system overview specifies that roughness measurements and friction characteristics are collected to assess functional condition, while pavement history together with traffic counts are employed to evaluate structural capacity; these elements are stored in the RMS for maintenance planning. In contrast, the warranty specification for asphalt concrete pavement limits the condition survey to rut‑depth measurements taken with a 12 ft straight edge across the lane width, traffic classification counts to verify that ESAL estimates remain within projected values, and core drilling where each core’s depth is measured to assess base condition; it does not prescribe additional structural or functional metrics such as deflection, load‑transfer efficiency, friction, ride quality or bearing strength. Data interpretation and diagnosis

A pavement condition survey starts with visual observations that record distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, drainage problems, edge deterioration, joint spalling and joint faulting; the Department’s practice augments automated surveys with supplemental manual inspections when flushing or potholes cannot be reliably detected [1][2]. Field measurements corroborate the visual survey; Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provides information on existing stiffness, dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer data assess subgrade strength, skid‑resistance testing verifies friction problems, and ESAL traffic loading is considered for priority ranking [1]. Rut depth is verified using a 12‑foot straight edge laid across the lane width, and core samples are extracted whenever base problems are suspected; these cores are sent to the laboratory for analysis of density, voids, asphalt content and gradation, with acceptance based on pavement‑core results regardless of quantity [2]. Representative samples collected according to the sampling criteria are sent to a laboratory where material properties and appropriate stabilization mechanisms are evaluated, and laboratory analysis also identifies issues such as Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction that cannot be detected by the automated survey [1]. In the extended‑season paving process a pre‑placement quality‑control plan records field observations and measurements such as core densities and temperature checks; laboratory‑derived limits for mixture temperature and LTS testing are incorporated into this plan [2]. The resulting summary links observed distress severity with measured structural capacity and lab‑derived material design, allowing engineers to select a rehabilitation strategy that directly addresses each identified failure mode [1]. All visual observations, field measurements and laboratory test outcomes are entered into an annual performance analysis that also incorporates traffic classification counts to confirm ESAL estimates, and the integrated dataset is compared against the distress limits in Table A; for example, a year‑5 survey showing 30 % of a segment area containing two or more low‑severity distresses triggers microsurfacing, while combined deviations that total a remedial‑action area greater than 20 % of the segment surface require removal and replacement of the entire segment [2]. For pavement preservation projects the integrated information feeds treatment matrices in which patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost-effective or permitted, cyclic crack sealing should be performed every 3 to 5 years, joint sealing should be done every 5 years, and ongoing quality‑assurance supports the condition data [1]. Within 24 hours of paving completion the contractor must submit Form CS‑413EQC with all documentation and measurements, after which a spring manual survey visually inspects fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination; the Representative then interprets the combined visual findings, field data and laboratory criteria to determine acceptance of the work or the need for remedial action [2].

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B. The same source notes that insufficient subgrade strength constitutes a weak‑support problem, which is reflected by observed cracking, rutting, raveling and potholes that signify material deterioration [1]. It further advises that future traffic loading must be evaluated because overloading represents another likely cause of the recorded distresses [1]. Those specifications also state that when cumulative ESALs exceed the 20‑year design life (i.e., exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design ESALs), the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base failures, implying that traffic overloading beyond design expectations can generate such distresses [2]. Additionally, the warranty document lists uncontrolled forces of nature as a factor beyond the contractor’s control, indicating that environmental exposure may also contribute to the observed condition [2].

Findings, confidence, and next actions

The full‑walk Pavement Condition Survey yields a concrete description of the existing pavement state, capturing drainage, shoulder condition, geometry, International Roughness Index values, specific distress types and their severity, any indication that distress is premature, and an estimate of patching area expressed as a percent of the surface. These data are directly used to inform scoping decisions such as selecting appropriate treatment strategies, justifying deviations from State of the Interstate recommendations, feeding Pavement Asset Management System forecasts for project letting dates, defining segment‑level existing conditions for system‑wide funding analyses, and generating recommended treatments for each roadway segment. The survey also produces a systematic inventory of distresses—including rutting, fatigue cracking, shoulder drop‑off, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting—which the STAMPP treatment matrices translate into actions ranging from routine maintenance (crack sealing every 3 to 5 years for asphalt and joint sealing every 5 years for concrete) through preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction. In the context of a 60‑month warranty, the annual condition survey supplies the data required to assess compliance with performance criteria; for example, if a year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied. If combined deviations exceed the threshold that defines a remedial‑action area greater than 20 % of a segment’s surface, the specification mandates removal and replacement of the entire segment. The survey further supports verification of flushing and potholes through manual surveys and measurement of rut depth with a 12 ft straight edge, as well as confirmation of traffic classification counts used to estimate ESALs. However, when patching requirements exceed 10 % of the pavement area, preservation is generally not cost‑effective, indicating that condition‑survey results alone cannot reliably determine the optimal treatment without supplemental analysis. The STAMPP program surveys 100 % of National Highway System roadways each year but only 50 % of non‑NHS roads, so data for many secondary segments may be less current, introducing uncertainty about their present condition. Certain inventory elements such as guide‑rail condition are collected by nonprofessional temporary staff, which adds further uncertainty to the quality of those data. The information from the Roadway Management System cannot be used to develop quantities for detailed design, and “windshield” evaluations are deemed inadequate; because the inspection captures only observable surface conditions, hidden defects or future performance remain uncertain. Because the survey is limited to visible distresses, it does not alone determine subgrade strength or structural capacity; additional in‑situ testing and sampling are required to resolve uncertainties about underlying material properties and future performance. The RMS condition data represent a snapshot on the day of the survey and do not change after maintenance until a later resurvey; traffic counts may be several years old, and a pavement layer could have been missed in the history if it resulted from surface treatments or other maintenance work, making field viewing and testing essential. The method depends on obtaining STAMPP condition data from the RMS, which may require supplemental field verification; where condition data are incomplete or not corroborated, uncertainty remains regarding the accuracy of the survey conclusions. Automated videologging surveys can miss condition‑specific issues such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase, aggregate problems like Vanport Limestone or alkali silica reaction, and ramp pavement conditions; these imperfections necessitate field verification before final decisions are made. In the warranty context, some distresses rely on manual rather than automated measurement, and distress causes may be attributed to factors beyond the contractor’s control—including base condition, excess traffic loading, routine Department maintenance, destructive procedures or natural events—adding uncertainty to the attribution of responsibility. Disputed survey results must be resolved through a conflict‑resolution team, which further introduces uncertainty into the final conclusions drawn from the condition survey. Consequently, project selection and prioritization should not be driven solely by these performance measures but by management objectives aimed at achieving the lowest life‑cycle cost; the condition‑survey information serves as a starting point that must be supplemented with in‑depth field views before definitive scope decisions are made.
