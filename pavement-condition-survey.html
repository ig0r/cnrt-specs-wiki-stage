Pavement Condition Survey

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Pavement Condition Survey is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
condition-surveycondition-surveyspavement-condition-surveyautomated-pavement-condition-surveyautomated-pavement-condition-surveys
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

The Pavement Condition Survey is a systematic field‑inspection that records International Roughness Index values and all observable surface and underlying distress types—rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling or faulting, drainage deficiencies, shoulder conditions and geometric features—to characterize the existing physical condition of each pavement segment[1]. By quantifying the severity and extent of each distress (for example, using a 12‑foot straight edge for rut depth and expressing patching as a percent of segment area) and comparing those values to performance‑criteria limits, the survey determines whether routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction is required, triggering remedial actions such as microsurfacing when thresholds like 30 % of a segment containing two or more low‑severity distresses are exceeded[2]. The data are captured through automated videologging (STAMPP) supplemented by manual verification and entered into the Roadway Management System, where they feed treatment matrices, traffic‑loading analyses, and structural capacity assessments that guide design, budgeting and life‑cycle cost decisions[1][2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The Pavement Condition Survey evaluates the existing physical condition of a pavement segment by recording its surface and underlying characteristics at the time of inspection, capturing International Roughness Index values and documenting visible distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting, as well as drainage problems, shoulder conditions and geometric features [1]. For asphalt‑concrete pavement the survey also inspects material and workmanship indicators including fatigue cracking, transverse or miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, joint or edge deterioration, loss of bond/delamination, in addition to flushing, potholes and rutting [2]. The investigation should answer what the measured IRI values are, which distress types are present and how severe each is, whether any distress appears premature, what observations can be made about the level of each distress and its underlying causes, how the condition interacts with surrounding site characteristics, how the pavement has deteriorated uniformly since the most recent resurfacing, rehabilitation or reconstruction, and what the rate and pattern of deterioration are at the segment‑level [1]. It should also determine which treatment matrix or cycle chart outcome is appropriate—routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction—and which rehabilitation or replacement projects are required based on needs and the minimum treatment [1]. The investigation must answer which distresses are present, their severity and extent—including rut depth verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width—and whether the observed values exceed the performance‑criteria limits set for each segment, such as when 30 % of a segment area has two or more low‑severity distresses [2]. It must also determine if the findings lead to acceptance of the work or require remedial action, including microsurfacing or other Department‑approved treatment, and whether any distress is attributable to factors beyond the contractor’s control (for example base condition deficiencies, traffic overloads, or uncontrolled forces of nature) so responsibility can be assigned [2]. Finally, the survey should establish if repair areas are less than 100 feet apart, which would trigger continuous repairs [2].

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, the field‑inspection component of a Pavement Condition Survey records International Roughness Index values, specific distress types and their severity, and whether any distress is premature; these data quantify existing deficiencies and estimate the percentage of the surface that will require patching [1]. The manual states that the resulting information is cross‑checked with the treatment strategy suggested in the annual State of the Interstate report and with the forecasted treatments from the Pavement Asset Management System, ensuring that the most cost‑effective rehabilitation or reconstruction option is chosen, safety considerations are addressed, and preservation guidelines are followed during design and life‑cycle cost analysis [1]. A recent survey provides a detailed picture of existing distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems and explains the mechanisms that caused them; this information is essential for selecting an appropriate rehabilitation strategy, sizing layers, choosing stabilization methods, and ensuring each distress type is properly addressed in design and maintenance plans [1]. Because the Condition Survey together with any layer analysis yields the effective structural capacity (Deff or SNeff) of the existing pavement, engineers can compare this measured capacity to the capacity required for future traffic (Df or SNf) and determine which overlay type is suitable—for example, a severely cracked concrete slab would be ruled out for a bonded PCC overlay [1]. For jointed plain concrete pavements the survey provides the STAMPP condition data that feed the calculation of Effective Existing Thickness; accurate input values—sometimes verified through a field view by the District PME/PM—allow DARWin to compute the actual asphalt‑overlay thickness required rather than relying solely on a structural number, and documenting how these inputs were obtained is essential for the overlay design submission [1]. The Spring State of the Interstate report compiles the pavement condition data to define the existing condition on each segment and project, identifying deficiencies within the limits of the most recent resurfacing, rehabilitation or reconstruction; based on those limits appropriate treatments are determined and a system‑wide analysis can be used to estimate the types of efforts and funds necessary for improvement [1]. Although project selection and prioritization should not be driven by condition performance measures alone, the survey findings still provide the technical basis for district Interstate plans and the final statewide program, serving as a starting point that must be supplemented with in‑depth field views before the final scope is set [1]. Annual automated Pavement Condition Surveys collect condition data such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting; the resulting matrices match these distress levels to one of four treatment categories—routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction—and thereby dictate which actions should be scheduled [1]. For routine maintenance the survey triggers cyclic crack sealing every 3 to 5 years for asphalt surfaces and joint sealing every 5 years for concrete pavements, while preservation projects are limited to situations where patching does not exceed 10 % of the pavement area because larger patches are generally not cost‑effective for preservation [1]. By linking the condition information with traffic loading (ESAL) and structural data such as FWD results, planners can prioritize more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction when structural capacity is insufficient, allowing funding and work to be spread over multiple years [1]. The Condition Survey results stored in the Roadway Management System give planners a snapshot of each segment’s distress level that can be matched to project priorities and the minimum treatment required; by combining those condition data with pavement history and traffic counts engineers evaluate the existing structural capacity before selecting rehabilitation or replacement options, and the Maintenance Functional Codes linked to the survey information then guide the appropriate design criteria for any chosen treatment [1]. The annual STAMPP pavement condition survey—covering 100 % of the National Highway System and 50 % of non‑NHS roadways—feeds detailed distress data into PennDOT’s RMS, where recommended treatments are generated for each segment; because a surface‑treatment recommendation is normally a good indication that surfacing (typically an asphalt overlay) is needed, the survey results guide rehabilitation design decisions and must be attached to Federal oversight design submissions [1]. The database also produces summaries of specific distresses such as shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking, which county maintenance staff use to pinpoint high‑risk items for timely repair, and condition inventories for guide rails and drainage features allow engineers to identify and schedule needed maintenance actions [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications states that the annual Pavement Condition Survey supplies quantified distress data that are compared with the performance‑criteria limits set out in Table A of Section 496.3(c), and when a segment reaches the threshold of 30 % of its area containing two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty requires remedial treatment such as microsurfacing or an approved alternative [2]. These survey results therefore drive the Department’s decision to schedule remediation, issue written notices to the Contractor, and, if necessary, initiate emergency repairs, while also informing traffic‑load verification and base‑condition investigations that affect future design and maintenance planning [2]. The post‑season manual survey described in Section 413.3(o) provides a systematic inventory of pavement distresses—including fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination—that the Department evaluates to decide whether the work is accepted or remedial action is required [2]. Because the survey follows Publication 336, its documented results give the agency confidence in judging material quality and workmanship, directing where repairs must be made, and informing future design, maintenance and rehabilitation plans; the requirement that any repair meet the surface‑tolerance specifications further ties the condition‑survey findings to concrete engineering actions [2].

Scope and applicability

The Pavement Condition Survey is applied to any existing roadway when a recent assessment of its surface condition is required, including asphalt‑concrete pavements evaluated according to Publication 336, continuously reinforced concrete and unpaved roads covered by Publication 343, or municipal streets for which alternative distress procedures such as MicroPaver™ are employed; it records the severity of typical distresses—including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies—and is considered essential for selecting Full‑Depth Reclamation projects across these pavement types and material conditions. The Condition Survey Method is used when an existing Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is being evaluated for an asphalt‑concrete overlay; it requires STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System must be obtained, and a field view by the District PME/PM may also be necessary to obtain accurate values for the existing pavement‑condition variables. Under the STAMPP program, the Automated Pavement Condition Survey (videologging) is applied annually to all Interstate roadway sections and covers both asphalt‑surfaced and jointed concrete pavements, recording surface‑layer distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting; however, conditions like premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate‑related issues (for example Vanport Limestone or ASR), and ramp pavements are not reliably captured and therefore require additional field verification. In PennDOT’s STAMPP program the Pavement Condition Survey is performed each year on all National Highway System roadways and on half of the non‑NHS network each year (the remaining half are surveyed on a two‑year cycle); it records surface distresses on both asphalt pavements and jointed concrete pavements, whether plain or reinforced, including rutting, fatigue cracking and shoulder drop‑off, and also supports condition inventories for shoulders, guide rails and drainage features. For asphalt‑concrete (flexible) pavements that are covered by the 60‑month warranty, the Pavement Condition Survey described in Section 496 is applied to both the surface course and the underlying base courses; the survey identifies and measures distresses such as flushing, potholes, rutting (verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width), fatigue cracking and any other distress types listed in Table A, and remedial action is required when the year‑5 survey determines that 30 % of a segment area has two or more low‑severity distresses. Surveys are performed annually by automated methods, with manual surveys supplementing the detection of flushing and potholes; additional surveys may be called when traffic loads exceed the design ESALs or when factors beyond the contractor’s control—such as poor base condition, extreme weather events, or accidents—are identified. For flexible asphalt‑concrete courses covered by Section 413, especially those placed in the fall portion of an extended‑season paving operation, a manual Pavement Condition Survey is applied to all PG 64E-22 wearing courses, >10 million ESALs wearing courses, 4.75 mm wearing courses, or any other wearing course installed at a compacted depth less than 1.5 inches, as well as the remaining asphalt‑concrete layers; after such extended‑season work the Department conducts a spring evaluation and manual survey by May 1 that examines fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, loss of bond/delamination, and the survey is triggered when paving occurs outside the normal season or under weather limits such as wet surfaces or air/surface temperatures of 40 F or lower. The Pavement Condition Survey, when performed only as a simple visual walk‑through, yields merely qualitative observations and cannot be used to develop quantities for detailed design; consequently projects that involve rehabilitation or reconstruction requiring a Life Cycle Cost Analysis or that fall under Federal Oversight should incorporate a more comprehensive scoping field view with input from design, construction, maintenance units and the District Pavement Management Engineer as a complementary investigation [1]. The survey’s reliance on drive‑by or “windshield” evaluations is likewise inadequate for detailed design purposes because Roadway Management System data are limited to a snapshot of surface distresses and do not provide the structural information needed for design calculations [1]. While the condition survey provides a snapshot of surface distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems, it does not quantify subgrade or structural stiffness; therefore, before full‑depth reclamation complementary investigations such as Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer measurements and material sampling should be performed, and alternative distress procedures like MicroPaver may be used when a different or more detailed visual assessment is needed for municipal projects [1]. The State of the Interstate report offers only a high‑level overview and should not drive project selection; detailed on‑site inspections and an in‑depth field view are required before finalizing the actual scope of work for any rehabilitation or reconstruction effort [1]. The automated STAMPP survey, although providing annual data on cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, may miss premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement condition is not assessed at all; field visual inspections or targeted tests—such as skid‑resistance testing for suspected Vanport Limestone sections—should be used to supplement the survey results [1]. Pavement Condition Survey data in the RMS are limited by availability, detail and timeliness: the condition data represent only a snapshot taken on the survey day and remain unchanged after any maintenance or rehabilitation until a later resurvey; traffic counts may be several years old; and pavement‑history layers may be missed or omitted due to recording lapses, so field viewing and direct testing of the pavement should be employed as a complementary investigation whenever current, detailed condition information is required for design, after recent work has been performed, or when traffic or history data are outdated or incomplete [1]. The STAMPP program’s coverage schedule—100 % of National Highway System segments surveyed each year but only 50 % of non‑NHS network inspected annually and the remainder every two years—and its focus on a predefined list of surface distresses captured through semi‑automated video logging mean that the resulting treatment recommendations are intended for routine maintenance and are somewhat less than the design strategy needed for contract rehabilitation; consequently a more detailed structural investigation such as core sampling or deflection testing is required when a rehabilitation design or new construction project is being planned, and guide‑rail and drainage condition data collected by nonprofessional temporary staff should be verified with professional inspections before making safety‑critical decisions [1]. The Condition Survey required by Section 496.3(c) relies on automated identification and measurement of distresses per Publication 336 but is limited to surface‑visible defects; when flushing or potholes are present the survey must be performed manually, and rut depth severity is only verified with a 12‑foot straight edge [2]. Because the survey cannot reveal underlying causes such as an unacceptable base, any detection of fatigue cracking or similar signs triggers core drilling and laboratory testing of six‑inch diameter cores evaluated per PTM 715, AASHTO T 209, etc., to confirm base condition [2]. If traffic counts show cumulative ESALs exceeding 100 % of the projected 20‑year design life, additional investigation is required to assess load effects; consequently nondestructive testing or supplemental core sampling should be employed whenever performance surveys indicate possible structural deficiencies or when evidence suggests that the prescribed distress limits are being exceeded [2].

[1] Because many records lack a recent pavement condition survey, an up‑to‑date documentation of all visible distresses and their severity must be obtained in order to resolve uncertainty about the mechanisms causing rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes or drainage problems that are needed for selecting an appropriate rehabilitation strategy. [1] The State of the Interstate report assumes uniform deterioration over the limits of the most recent construction, leaving it uncertain whether that assumed loss matches actual distress patterns, which specific deficiencies exist, and whether performance measures accurately reflect current conditions; these ambiguities must be resolved through an on‑site condition survey before finalizing scope. Visual inspection and condition assessment

In the context of the STAMPP program described in Section 14.3 of the Pavement Policy Manual, a Pavement Condition Survey must record all visible signs of deterioration on the existing surface and should specifically evaluate the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and any drainage deficiencies that could affect performance [1]. The annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys required by STAMPP capture a set of observable distresses that guide treatment decisions; these include cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling, joint faulting, and premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements [1]. The survey also records the visual distresses most affecting performance—specifically rutting, fatigue cracking, and shoulder drop‑off—and documents the condition of related guide‑rail and drainage features [1]. In the warranty‑specified condition survey mandated by Section 496 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, the visible defects that must be recorded include surface flushing, potholes, and any rutting, with rut depth measured using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width [2]. The spring manual survey described in Section 413 and conducted according to Publication 336 also seeks additional visible signs of distress such as fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination, together with rutting, flushing and potholing [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 266–358)

The Pavement Condition Survey classifies observed distresses according to the list defined for asphalt and jointed concrete pavements (plain or reinforced) in Publication 336 [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty specification, each pavement segment—approximately a half‑mile delineated by physical features in the Department’s Location Reference System—is surveyed annually, and both automated video‑log surveys and manual inspections for flushing and potholes identify the distress type and assign a severity rating following Publication 336 [2]. The semi‑automated video‑log field survey prescribed by Publication 336 records the severity of each identified distress as part of the data collection process [1]. For warranty compliance, extent is expressed as the percentage of the segment area occupied by that distress; for example, if 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action is required [2]. Rut depth, when it must be evaluated, is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge placed across the lane width to quantify both severity and longitudinal extent [2]. The precise location of every distress is documented using Pennsylvania’s Location Reference System, which uniquely identifies each roadway segment in the RMS database; the survey report lists for each segment the exact segment identifier together with the distress type, measured severity, calculated extent, and an indication of whether the performance limits in Table A are met or exceeded [1][2].

Field measurements and testing

A Pavement Condition Survey must begin with a full foot‑walk field inspection of the project site, during which International Roughness Index values are recorded to quantify surface roughness and distress types and their severity are documented. A visual distress survey performed in accordance with Publication 336, Publication 343, or comparable methods such as MicroPaver™ documents the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems, thereby indicating both current condition and underlying damage mechanisms. The condition survey also relies on an automated distress survey that, in accordance with Publication 336, identifies, measures and reports cracks, flushing, potholes and other distresses; when automation is not available or when evaluating flushing and potholes the survey is supplemented by a manual visual survey performed according to Publication 336. Rutting severity and extent are quantified by measuring rut depth with a 12‑foot straight edge laid across the lane width. Traffic classification counts and Equivalent Single Axle Load traffic‑loading data are used to confirm that accumulated axle stresses remain within projected design values and to prioritize work. In‑situ nondestructive testing includes Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements of pavement layer stiffness and subgrade deflection, which are used to infer structural characteristics, joint efficiency and the quantity of concrete patching needed. The Dynamic Cone Penetrometer provides subgrade strength information through penetration resistance. A Light Weight Deflectometer offers additional assessment of subgrade stiffness. Skid‑resistance testing quantifies surface friction to identify sections with verified skid‑friction problems. An automated videologging survey (STAMPP) provides a nondestructive visual assessment that records distress types such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting. The contractor may employ approved nondestructive testing methods after notifying the District Executive, and must submit those results for departmental review. Density verification for mixes placed at the minimum compacted depths listed in Table G is achieved by extracting pavement cores; core sampling is required for all such mixtures regardless of quantity. Patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects. [1][2]

The equipment employed for Pavement Condition Surveys is primarily Automated Pavement Condition Survey systems that record video (videologging); these automated surveys are used by the Department or its designee and are supplemented, when necessary, by manual surveys performed in accordance with Publication 336. Rut depth measurements, when required, are verified using a 12‑foot straight edge placed across the lane width, and detection of fatigue cracking triggers the drilling of a series of 6‑inch diameter cores at locations directed by the Department; the extracted cores are tested according to PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T 209. Survey segments are defined as divisions of the State Route approximately one‑half mile in length, and when the distance between repair areas is less than 100 feet a continuous repair must be made. Pavement Condition Surveys are scheduled to occur annually throughout the warranty period, and the same annual frequency is incorporated into the Systematic Technique to Analyze and Manage Pennsylvania Pavements (STAMPP) program; additional or more frequent surveys may be instituted whenever performance criteria indicate that they are not being met. Nondestructive testing may be performed at any time during the warranty period provided the Contractor gives the District Executive at least seven calendar days notice, and inclement weather may postpone permanent remediation work, allowing temporary repairs until conditions improve; emergency repairs are authorized when pavement condition is potentially harmful or unsafe. Sampling and laboratory investigation

The STAMPP pavement condition survey records distress data for both asphalt and jointed‑concrete pavements, whether plain or reinforced [1]. Sampling locations are defined by Pennsylvania’s Location Reference System, which uniquely identifies each roadway segment in the RMS, and the program mandates coverage of 100 % of National Highway System (NHS) routes each year and 50 % of non‑NHS routes each year, with a complete non‑NHS inventory completed every two years; the source does not prescribe specific sampling depths or per‑segment quantities [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty specification, condition surveys must record surface distresses on the asphalt concrete layer through automated or manual surveys performed annually by the Department of Transportation or its designee, and additional surveys may be conducted when needed [2]. When base‑condition problems are suspected, the Department directs a series of 6‑inch diameter cores to be taken within the identified distress area, each core being measured for depth and subsequently tested according to PTM 715, AASHTO T 209, PTM 702 and PTM 739 to evaluate voids, asphalt content and gradation [2]. The locations of these cores are chosen by the Department at the distress sites, and the number of cores is determined on a case‑by‑case basis (“a series”) rather than by a fixed count [2]. Survey frequency for the warranty period is set to an annual performance analysis throughout the warranty term, with extra surveys permissible if evidence shows criteria are not being met [2].

When fatigue cracking or other distresses that may signal an unacceptable base condition are observed in a performance survey, the warranty specification requires drilling a series of 6‑inch diameter cores from the distressed area and sending them to the laboratory; the cores are tested according to PTM 715 and AASHTO T209 to determine percent unfilled voids, according to PTM 702 (or an approved method) to determine percent asphalt, and according to PTM 739 to establish gradation, with density required to exceed 92 % of the theoretical maximum. These laboratory analyses evaluate the base’s structural condition, void content, binder proportion, and aggregate size distribution, which together reveal deterioration mechanisms such as fatigue cracking or base failure. Structural and functional performance

In the context of selecting full‑depth reclamation projects (section 5.15) a recent pavement condition survey must be complemented by in‑situ testing that captures structural capacity, including Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements which provide deflection‑related stiffness information, and subgrade strength tests such as the dynamic cone penetrometer and the light weight deflectometer [1]. The evaluation also has to determine existing layer thicknesses, since appropriate thickness is critical for meeting traffic demands, while visual distress data on rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues inform functional condition [1]. Under the pavement testing criteria (section 13.7) the survey incorporates skid‑resistance testing to quantify friction, Falling Weight Deflectometer testing to obtain deflection data and derive subgrade and pavement characteristics as well as concrete joint efficiency, and an annual automated pavement condition survey (videologging) that records functional distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting [1]. According to the roadway management system overview (section 14.1) the data collected for a pavement condition survey include roughness measurements and friction characteristics, which are used to assess functional condition, while the recorded pavement history together with traffic counts are employed to evaluate the pavement’s structural capacity [1]. The condition survey described in the warranty specification relies on rut‑depth checks performed with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to gauge functional surface distress [2]. It also uses traffic classification counts to verify that Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) estimates stay within the projected values [2]. Targeted core sampling using 6‑inch diameter cores is employed to measure base depth, percent voids, percent asphalt, gradation and to confirm that the pavement meets the specified minimum thickness and a density greater than > 92% of theoretical maximum density for structural capacity [2].

Data interpretation and diagnosis

Visual observations document distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, drainage problems, edge deterioration, joint spalling and joint faulting. [1] Those observations are corroborated with field measurements; Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provides information on existing stiffness, dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer data assess subgrade strength, skid‑resistance testing verifies friction problems, and rut depth is measured using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width. [1][2] Representative samples collected according to sampling criteria are sent to a laboratory where material properties and appropriate stabilization mechanisms are evaluated; core sampling is analyzed per PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T 209 to assess voids, asphalt content and gradation, and laboratory analysis also identifies issues such as Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction that cannot be detected by the automated survey. [1][2] The resulting summary links observed distress severity with measured structural capacity and lab‑derived material design, allowing engineers to select a rehabilitation strategy that directly addresses each identified failure mode. [1] For pavement preservation projects the integrated information feeds treatment matrices in which patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost-effective or permitted, cyclic crack sealing should be performed every 3 to 5 years, and joint sealing should be done every 5 years. [1] Under the warranty specification, the survey results are entered into an annual performance analysis that compares measured distress extents against the limits in Table A; for example, if a year‑5 survey shows 30 % of a segment area containing two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another approved treatment is required. [2] The integrated interpretation therefore determines whether the pavement meets the specified performance criteria and if remedial work is required. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

The condition survey may reveal that excess moisture leading to poor drainage can degrade pavement material integrity and weaken its structural capacity. [1] Insufficient subgrade strength is identified as a weak support issue, and when performance surveys detect fatigue cracking or other distresses suggesting an unacceptable base condition, the weakness can be confirmed by drilling 6‑inch cores and testing for voids, asphalt content and gradation. [1][2] The presence of cracking, rutting, raveling and potholes in the distress survey is taken as evidence of material deterioration. [1] Evaluation of future traffic loading points may indicate overloading as a likely cause, and if Department traffic counts show that cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, the overload is considered beyond the designed traffic loading. [1][2] Distresses attributed to uncontrolled forces of nature such as floods, earthquakes, tornadoes, brush or forest fires, landslides, sinkholes, or other natural disasters are recognized as environmental exposure beyond the contractor’s control. [2]

Findings, confidence, and next actions

A full‑walk Pavement Condition Survey provides the concrete conclusions needed to describe the current pavement condition, including drainage, shoulders, geometry, International Roughness Index values, specific distress types and their severity, an indication of whether any distress appears premature, and an estimate of patching area expressed as a percent of the pavement surface.[1] These data feed directly into scoping decisions such as selecting a treatment strategy, justifying deviations from the State of the Interstate recommendation, informing Pavement Asset Management System forecasts for the project letting date, defining the existing condition of each segment and project for system‑wide analysis of funding needs, and generating recommended treatments for each roadway segment.[1] The survey also supplies a systematic inventory of distresses such as rutting, fatigue cracking, shoulder drop‑off, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting; the STAMPP treatment matrices translate those inventories into actions ranging from routine maintenance (crack sealing every 3 to 5 years for asphalt and joint sealing every 5 years for concrete) to preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction.[1] When patching requirements exceed 10 % of the pavement area, preservation is generally not cost‑effective, indicating that condition survey results alone cannot reliably determine treatment type without supplemental analysis.[1] The condition surveys required by the warranty can be used to confirm whether each half‑mile segment meets the distress limits set in Table A, to trigger remedial actions such as microsurfacing when the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, and to verify that traffic loads remain within the projected ESAL values through annual performance analyses; they also provide the data needed for the Department to issue emergency repair notices.[2] The post‑paving manual condition survey required for extended‑season work can be used to conclude whether the newly placed asphalt meets the Department’s acceptance criteria because the survey evaluates fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination before issuing an acceptance or remedial‑action decision.[2] However, the conclusions are limited because information from the Roadway Management System cannot be used to develop quantities for detailed design, “windshield” evaluations are not adequate, and the inspection captures only observable surface conditions, leaving hidden defects or future performance uncertain.[1] Automated videologging surveys may miss condition‑specific issues such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase, aggregate problems like Vanport Limestone or alkali silica reaction, and ramp pavement conditions; imperfections in the automated data mean that field verification is required before final decisions.[1] Some inventory elements, such as guide‑rail condition, are collected by nonprofessional temporary staff, introducing uncertainty about data quality, and a layer could have been missed in the pavement history if it resulted from a build‑up of surface treatments or other maintenance work.[1] Certain distress types (flushing and potholes) must be measured manually, survey results can be disputed and may require third‑party appraisal or resolution by a Conflict Resolution Team, and any distresses attributed to base condition failures, excess traffic loading, routine Department maintenance, destructive procedures, natural forces, or accidents are excluded from contractor responsibility, introducing uncertainty about cause and liability.[2] The post‑paving acceptance conclusion is limited to the extended‑season portion of construction, depends on a manual survey performed according to Publication 336 before May 1, and final acceptance cannot be issued until a spring evaluation and any required repairs are completed, leaving some uncertainty about long‑term performance until those steps are finished.[2] Because RMS pavement condition data represent a snapshot of each segment on the day it is surveyed, they do not change after maintenance until a later resurvey; traffic counts may be several years old, and the survey covers only 100 % of National Highway System roadways each year but only 50 % of non‑NHS roads, so data for many secondary segments may be less current.[1] Consequently, project selection and prioritization should not be driven solely by these performance measures but by management to achieve the lowest life‑cycle cost; the information serves as a starting point that must be supplemented with in‑depth field views and additional in‑situ testing before final scope decisions are made.[1]

Following a Pavement Condition Survey the engineer must first determine the pavement’s effective structural capacity (Deff or SNeff) and compare it with the capacity required for future traffic (Df or SNf); to refine that assessment non‑destructive deflection testing with a Falling Weight Deflectometer may be performed to obtain in‑situ stiffness data and, when reliable historic traffic information exists, a remaining‑life analysis can be used [1]. The resulting capacity values guide the overlay design check, ensuring that the selected overlay type matches the existing pavement condition—for example, a bonded PCC overlay should not be placed on a concrete deck that is severely cracked or faulted [1]. After the survey documents the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues, the next steps are to verify subgrade strength with in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer before rehabilitation and again after construction, supplemented by Dynamic Cone Penetrometer and Light Weight Deflectometer tests [1]. Representative material samples should be collected following the sampling criteria outlined in Appendix J sections J.2.D.1 through J.2.D.5, appropriate layer thicknesses must be determined and any identified wet subgrade or drainage problems resolved before proceeding with Full Depth Reclamation; Table 5.8 is used to evaluate whether FDR is a suitable rehabilitation strategy based on the observed surface distresses, selecting an appropriate stabilization mechanism thereafter [1]. STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System must be obtained and a field view by the District PME/PM may also be necessary to determine accurate input values for some existing‑condition variables; Table 10.9 provides guidance on appropriate inputs and the overlay design submission must include documentation of where and how the Condition Survey values were obtained [1]. The State of the Interstate analysis directs that appropriate pavement and bridge treatments be selected to remediate identified deficiencies and distresses, but those recommendations must first be verified through detailed on‑site inspections before a final scope is defined; District Interstate plans then incorporate the supplemental information to shape the statewide program, using the survey data as an initial input rather than a definitive design basis [1]. If any section is flagged for a verified skid‑resistance problem, it is sent for additional Skid Friction testing by BOMO and the test results must be addressed in the District Interstate plan; Falling Weight Deflectometer testing then provides pavement and subgrade characteristics needed for structural design and indicates concrete joint efficiency and patching quantities, noting that patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective or permitted for Pavement Preservation projects [1]. The condition data feed the Pavement Condition/Treatment matrices which trigger routine maintenance such as crack sealing (should be done every 3 to 5 years), joint sealing (should be done every 5 years), spall repair, mechanized patch, and preservation treatments including micro‑surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with milling, diamond grinding, base repair or concrete patching; when specific issues not captured by the survey arise—premature mid‑slab cracking from inadequate subbase material, Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction concerns, or ramp pavements that are not assessed via STAMPP—the plan should include targeted monitoring and prompt skid‑resistance testing and may require earlier overlay or other corrective actions before matrix thresholds are reached [1]. Because the RMS condition snapshot remains unchanged until a later resurvey, the survey results should be followed by on‑site visual inspection and physical testing of the pavement to verify its current state; the documented condition data are then combined with the recorded pavement history and traffic volumes to evaluate structural capacity, while Maintenance Functional Codes guide the selection of design criteria and the minimum treatment needed for rehabilitation or replacement, and periodic resurveying is required to keep the database up‑to‑date [1]. After the annual STAMPP pavement condition survey Penn DOT generates a recommended treatment for each segment and when the recommendation is a surface‑treatment it normally signals that an asphalt overlay will be required; the resulting STAMPP Treatment Summary must accompany every Federal Oversight design submission on Form D‑4332, and county maintenance staff use RMS‑based Access tools to monitor high‑priority distresses such as shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking, flagging any high‑risk liability problems for corrective action while guide‑rail and drainage inventories are queried to schedule targeted repairs [1]. Following the automated or manual condition survey the Department must verify rut severity with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width and confirm that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values by conducting traffic classification counts [2]. When the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment shall be applied, with adjacent repair areas less than 100 ft combined into one continuous patch and all repairs meeting the surface tolerance in Section 413.3(l) [2]. If base‑condition distress is suspected, the Contractor shall drill 6‑inch cores within the affected area and have them tested for voids, asphalt content and gradation according to PTM No. 715, AASHTO T 209 and related methods, providing results to the Department [2]. The Contractor may also perform nondestructive testing after giving at least seven calendar days notice and must submit those results for departmental review [2].
