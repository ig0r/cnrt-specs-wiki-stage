Faulting

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Faulting is a topic under Distress.
faultingjoint-faultingfaultedfaultfaulted-joints
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Faulting is a concrete‑pavement distress that appears as a step or bump where one side of a transverse joint sits higher than the other, with fines accumulating near the joint or crack and small depressions (blow holes) observable in the shoulder, and it is quantified by measuring the elevation difference across the joint in inches [1][2]. It occurs on rigid Portland Cement Concrete surfaces at transverse joints of jointed plain, jointed reinforced, continuously reinforced, post‑tensioned or roller‑compacted pavements and is not described for asphalt, composite decks, overlays or shoulders [1][2]. The distress develops when voids form beneath the slab because of pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement—often aided by moisture infiltration—and the resulting loss of support produces excessive slab deflection that degrades structural integrity, can progress to corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup, and impairs ride quality [1].

Description and identification

Faulting is a concrete‑pavement distress that occurs when voids develop beneath a slab—often caused by pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement—and the loss of support results in excessive deflection at transverse joints; on the surface it appears as transverse joint faulting, a step or bump where one side of a joint sits higher than the other, with fines accumulating near the joint or crack on the traffic lane or shoulder and small depressions (blow holes) observable in the shoulder at the joint, and the condition is quantified by measuring the difference in elevation across transverse joints in inches [1]. Faulting is a vertical misalignment that occurs at concrete pavement joints, recognized when an elevation difference across the joint can be measured [2]. Mill joints with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting [2]. If joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch, place a scratch course at the joint or mill/grind joints to reduce faulting before placing the interlayer [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–217); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

In the slab‑stabilization guidance of Chapter 4 of the Pavement Policy Manual, faulting is identified by a measurable drop across a transverse joint—referred to as transverse joint faulting—and is distinguished from other loss‑of‑support symptoms such as fines that accumulate near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) that appear in the shoulder at the joint or crack, and corner breaks that manifest as cracking at slab corners. [1] Under the NGCS specification in section 4.7 Diamond Grinding and Grooving of the same manual, faulting is recognized as a surface irregularity that exceeds the 1/4‑inch elevation difference threshold; this magnitude separates it from smaller roughness or other distresses and requires removal with conventional diamond grinding before applying flush‑ground NGCS. [1] In the unbonded PCC overlay guidance (section 10.7) of the Pavement Policy Manual, faulting is identified by measuring the vertical offset across a concrete joint, and when the offset is greater than 1/4‑inch the joint is considered faulted and should be milled, while an offset that exceeds 1/2‑inch triggers more intensive repair such as placing a Superpave wearing course or grinding the joint; these elevation‑based thresholds separate faulting from other distresses like cracking. [1] The performance measures of section 13.4 describe faulting as the vertical difference between the two faces of a transverse joint, which distinguishes it from rutting—defined as the depth of surface depressions in asphalt pavement—and from cracking—quantified as the percentage of the pavement surface that is cracked. [1] In SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, faulting is identified by measuring the vertical offset at a concrete joint or crack; when the offset exceeds the limits specified, it is treated as faulting rather than other surface defects. [2] For placement of a geotextile interlayer the specification calls for milling any joint that shows more than 1/4‑inch of faulting, while an asphalt interlayer requires a scratch course or joint grinding if faulting exceeds 1/2 inch; these dimensional thresholds distinguish faulting from other distresses such as loose debris or surface cracks that do not produce measurable elevation differences. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–94), Ch. 10 (p. 185), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–217); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Where it occurs

Faulting is identified as a distress that appears on rigid pavement and therefore occurs on Portland Cement Concrete (PCC) surfaces; no comparable fault‑related item is described for asphalt concrete surfaces, composite decks, overlays, or shoulders in the general section [1]. The same manual notes that faulting may affect joints between slabs and even those linking a concrete lane to an asphalt shoulder, but it does not associate faulting with pure asphalt surfaces, composite pavements, or overlay constructions [1]. In jointed plain (JPCP), jointed reinforced (JRCP), continuously reinforced (CRCP), post‑tensioned slab (PCPS) or roller‑compacted (RCCP) pavements, faulting is evaluated by measuring the difference in elevation across transverse concrete joints in inches and is listed as one of the three distress components for concrete pavements, while it is not included among the distress components for asphalt pavements [1]. The Publication 408 specifications address faulting only for concrete pavements that are being overlaid under Section 548; joints showing greater than 1/4‑inch faulting must be milled before a geotextile interlayer is placed, while joint faulting that exceeds 1/2 inch requires a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint prior to applying an asphalt interlayer [2]. These thresholds apply specifically to unbonded concrete overlays on existing concrete‑surfaced pavements and do not extend to asphalt‑only surfaces or composite structures [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–185), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–217); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), faulting is evaluated by taking measurements at concrete pavement joints, and the results are used to decide which joints need replacement or slab‑stabilization work (section 4.1) [1]. The manual describes faulting as a joint‑performance problem that appears where one slab has settled relative to the adjacent slab across a transverse or longitudinal joint, indicating that the distress is confined to joints rather than cracks, corners, edges, wheel paths, slab interiors, layer interfaces, or the subgrade (section 4.3) [1]. In Portland cement concrete pavement the phenomenon manifests as transverse‑joint faulting that arises where the slab loses support beneath the surface; voids most often develop near transverse joints and cracks, particularly at outside slab corners, so the distress is located at transverse joints and adjacent cracks with a pronounced occurrence at outer slab corners (section 4.5) [1]. The unbonded concrete overlay guidance treats faulting as occurring at concrete joints, recommending milling of concrete joints that exhibit greater than ¼‑inch faulting and, when joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, placing a Superpave Wearing Course at the joint or mill/grind the joints to reduce faulting (section 10.7) [1]. Rigid‑pavement preservation guidelines also treat faulting as a joint problem; patching quantities must include faulted joints, confirming that the distress occurs at concrete slab joints rather than elsewhere (section 12.3) [1]. Performance‑measure definitions quantify faulting by the elevation difference across transverse joints in inches, further confirming that measurement and assessment are limited to pavement joints (section 13.4) [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2026 similarly addresses faulting specifically at concrete pavement joints; joints showing greater than ¼‑inch faulting shall be milled, and if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint must be performed to reduce the elevation difference (section 548.3) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 10 (p. 185), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–217); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Causes and mechanisms

Faulting in jointed concrete pavements arises when voids develop beneath the slab, which the source attributes to pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement; these loss‑of‑support conditions produce excessive deflections that manifest as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks and diagonal cracking, especially near transverse joints, cracks and outside slab corners. Moisture infiltration is noted as a factor that contributes to the pumping process, so keeping water out through joint sealing and functional subsurface drains helps mitigate the problem. The subsection 4.5 Slab Stabilization states that while stabilization restores support it does not eliminate faulting. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Faulting develops when the support beneath a concrete slab is compromised—either because pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement may create small voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and outside corners, or because load‑transfer across a joint is insufficient. The resulting loss of support causes excessive slab deflection and stress that manifests as a difference in elevation across the joint. Restoration of either the sub‑grade support (for example by slab stabilization or jacking) or the load‑transfer capability (such as a dowel bar retrofit) is required to stop its progression. The mechanism is accelerated when drainage is not functional or the subgrade is unstable, because water infiltration promotes pumping and introduces incompressible materials that further reduce support; inadequate sealing of joints and cracks and poor subsurface drainage likewise increase void formation and the likelihood of progressive faulting, and joint and crack sealing must be performed to reduce the amount of water that enters the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92)

Severity levels and rating

The unbonded PCC overlay guidance in the Pavement Policy Manual defines two faulting thresholds: when concrete joints exhibit greater than 1/4‑inch faulting the specification calls for milling the joints before installing a geotextile interlayer, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch the design requires placing a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix at the joint or further mill/grind to reduce the faulting [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for unbonded concrete overlay similarly prescribe two quantitative limits: for the geotextile interlayer any joint showing greater than 1/4‑inch of faulting must be milled before the fabric is placed, and for the asphalt interlayer a joint faulting that exceeds 1/2 inch requires either a scratch course at the joint or milling/grinding to reduce the faulting prior to overlay placement [2]. No tabular severity rating system appears in these excerpts; the measured limits themselves trigger the respective treatments [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

The rating system for faulting is provided by the FHWA Interstate Management Program Guidelines, Chapter 13 subsection 13.4 Performance Measures – Pavement Condition, which uses pavement‑condition performance measures to assign a good, fair or poor condition score based on the measured elevation difference across concrete joints; when the faulting component receives a poor rating it contributes to the segment being classified as in poor condition (a segment is poor if two or more of its three distress components are rated poor). [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 216–217)

Measurement, survey and extent

Faulting on jointed concrete pavements is quantified as the difference in elevation across transverse joints, expressed in inches; this measurement is one of the three distress components used for FHWA pavement‑condition reporting on concrete surfaces. The source does not describe a specific instrument or detailed procedure, only that the elevation difference is measured. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 216–217)

Effects on performance

Faulting is a symptom of loss of slab support that produces excessive slab deflections and stresses, which degrade the pavement’s structural integrity and can progress to corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and eventual slab breakup, thereby shortening service life. Because slab stabilization does not increase the design structural capacity or eliminate faulting, the resulting surface depressions impair ride quality. Restoring full support by filling voids reduces deflections and thus lowers the likelihood of further pumping, faulting, and cracking, helping to recover structural integrity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

In section 4.3 JOINTS, when faulting develops in a joint and is not repaired, the distress escalates so that simple resealing is no longer sufficient; more extensive repairs will be necessary the longer distressed joints are left unattended. Consequently the pavement may require restoration of support or load‑transfer, which can be achieved by slab stabilization or jacking for support problems, a dowel‑bar retrofit to regain load transfer, or, if those measures are unsuitable, replacement of the joint with full‑depth concrete patching. Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION explains that voids that develop beneath a concrete slab—especially near transverse joints, cracks, and outside corners—cause loss of support, producing excessive slab deflections and stresses; this first manifests as joint faulting, then progresses to corner breaks, followed by diagonal cracking, and ultimately the complete breakup of the slab. Because slab stabilization only restores support and does not eliminate existing faulting, filling the voids reduces deflections and therefore lessens the potential for further pumping, faulting, and slab cracking. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92)

Repairs and treatments

Slabjacking corrects faulted or sunken rigid‑pavement areas back to the original grade and is applied whenever such faulted or sunken sections are present as noted in the Portland Cement Concrete surface review (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. Slab stabilization is listed among concrete pavement restoration treatments that address faulting; it is indicated when measurements or non‑destructive testing identify joints whose elevation differences require support, and it is used to restore full support beneath slabs showing loss‑of‑support signs such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks, or slab settlement (sections 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION and 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION) [1]. When joint performance problems such as faulting are detected, the guidance recommends restoring support with slab stabilization or jacking and, where drainage is functional and the subgrade is stable, adding a dowel‑bar retrofit to re‑establish load transfer across the joint (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. If those measures cannot correct the elevation difference, full‑depth concrete pavement patching of the joint may be employed; this approach is prescribed for faulted joints that cannot be otherwise corrected and requires verification of all failed joints by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, with a limitation that patching not exceed ten percent of the total pavement area unless cost‑effectiveness is demonstrated (sections 4.3 JOINTS, 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY, and 12.3 DESIGN GUIDELINES) [1]. Conventional diamond grinding is identified as the only treatment in its section that directly addresses faulting; it is to be applied when significant roughness, faulting, or surface irregularities exceed one‑quarter inch before any Next Generation Concrete Surface work (section 4.7 DIAMOND GRINDING AND GROOVING) [1]. Any slab movement or faulting, including rocking, pumping, or visibly faulted slabs, should be stabilized prior to overlaying (section 4.9 REHABILITATION) [1]. An unbonded concrete overlay combined with full‑depth slab repairs is recommended for visibly rocking slabs that cannot be cost‑effectively repaired; the overlay provides uniform support and shallow repair of faulting, and when a geotextile interlayer is specified joints with more than one‑quarter inch faulting should be milled, while joint faulting greater than one‑half inch calls for placement of a Superpave wearing course or milling/grinding to reduce the faulting (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY) [1]. Milling of joints together with placement of a geotextile interlayer is prescribed when joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch, as indicated in the unbonded concrete overlay specification (Section 548.3 3.a) [2] and also referenced in the Pavement Policy Manual (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY) [1]. If joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch, a one‑inch Superpave scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint is required before overlay placement, per the specification (Section 548.3 3.b) [2] and reiterated in the Pavement Policy Manual (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY) [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Slab stabilization or jacking may be used when faulting is identified as a joint‑performance problem, provided that existing drainage is functional and the faulting is not caused by an unstable subgrade (section 4.3) [1]. Cross‑stitching can be applied to longitudinal joints that are just beginning to fault (section 4.3) [1]. Slab stabilization is permitted when transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks, slab settlement, or other indications of loss of slab support are observed, and any of the three grout options—cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane—may be used, with no specific faulting severity threshold stated (section 4.5) [1]. NGCS may be used only after any surface irregularities exceeding 1/4 inch have been removed with conventional diamond grinding; consequently, when faulting is greater than 1/4 inch the treatment is ruled out for direct NGCS application and must be corrected first (section 4.7) [1]. Slab stabilization or jacking must be performed before any rehabilitation work when slab movement or faulting is observed, and slabs that are rocking, pumping, or faulted should be stabilized prior to overlaying (section 4.9) [1]. A bonded Portland cement concrete overlay is considered inappropriate for an existing concrete roadway that shows severe faulting (section 10.1) [1]. For a geotextile‑interlayer unbonded PCC overlay, joint faulting greater than 1/4 inch requires milling before the fabric is placed, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch the guidance shifts to placing a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) at the joint or milling/grinding the joint to reduce the faulting (section 10.7) [1]; the same requirement for milling joints with faulting greater than 1/4 inch is reiterated in the Pennsylvania specification under Section 548.3, Geotextile Interlayer Placement, 3.a (source [2]). When an asphalt interlayer is specified, any joint whose faulting exceeds 1/2 inch requires a scratch course at the joint or milling/grinding to reduce the faulting prior to overlay placement (Section 548.3, Asphalt Interlayer, 3.b) [2]. Concrete full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation may be performed only when the total patched area does not exceed 10 % of the pavement surface; exceeding that percentage is not eligible unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be shown (section 12.3) [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–185), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Prevention in design and construction

Slabjacking is listed as a design measure that corrects faulted or sunken rigid‑pavement areas back to the original grade, and it is to be used whenever such faulted or sunken sections are present (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. Slab stabilization restores full support by filling voids beneath the concrete, thereby reducing future pumping, faulting and cracking; it shall be applied when voids are present—specifically at least 25 % of transverse joints and all patch joints if no preliminary testing has been performed (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION) [1]. In addition, any slab that is rocking, pumping or faulted must be stabilized before an overlay is placed (section 4.9 REHABILITATION, Surface Preparation) [1]. The specification further requires that areas with unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads be stabilized or repaired prior to placement of an interlayer, in order to prevent non‑uniform support and thus reduce faulting (section 548.3) [2]. Joint and crack sealing is required to be performed together with slab stabilization because limiting water entry helps reduce faulting (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION) [1]. A dowel bar retrofit may be employed to re‑establish load transfer across joints, thereby addressing faulting that results from inadequate load transfer; it is applicable provided the existing drainage system is functional and the faulting is not caused by an unstable subgrade, and it is primarily used on roadways receiving heavily channeled loads (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. Cross‑stitching is indicated for longitudinal joints that are beginning to fault, providing near‑complete load transfer across such cracks or joints (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. Conventional diamond grinding is identified as the only design‑related treatment that directly addresses faulting; it is recommended to remove significant roughness and correct faulting or surface irregularities greater than ¼ inch before applying Next Generation Concrete Surface, so the measure applies when NGCS is planned and the existing pavement exhibits faulting exceeding that threshold (section 4.7 DIAMOND GRINDING AND GROOVING) [1]. Bonded concrete overlays eliminate surface distress—including faulting—by adding structural capacity, and they are applicable when the existing pavement is in good to fair structural condition with a stable subgrade and functional drainage (section 4.9 REHABILITATION, Bonded Overlays) [1]. An unbonded concrete overlay module applied over an existing concrete pavement with full‑depth repairs for visibly rocking slabs provides uniform support and shallow repairs that address faulting; it is intended when the slab condition is unstable or when the pavement cannot be cost‑effectively repaired but can serve as a relatively uniform subbase (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, concrete joints that exhibit faulting greater than ¼ inch shall be milled prior to placement of the interlayer in order to remove excess elevation (section 10.7 … and §548.3, 3.a Geotextile Interlayer Placement) [1][2]. If joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix shall be placed at the joint or the joint shall be milled/grinded to lower the fault, thereby reducing faulting (section 10.7 … and §548.3, 3.b Asphalt Interlayer) [1][2]. Concrete full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation are prescribed to repair all failed joints—including faulted joints—provided that patching does not exceed ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is shown and the pavement has been verified as structurally sound by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing (section 12.3 DESIGN GUIDELINES, B) [1]. When an asphalt overlay is placed on concrete pavements, sawing and sealing of the overlay’s transverse joints over the existing concrete joints is recommended to mitigate faulting of those joints, except when the overlay is a micro‑surfacing treatment (section 12.3 DESIGN GUIDELINES, B.6) [1].

Slabjacking is identified as a construction practice intended to correct faulted or sunken areas of rigid pavement; it is required whenever such faulted or sunken sections are identified and must be performed in accordance with Publication 408, Specifications, Section 681 (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. Slab stabilization is prescribed as a technique that fills voids at the slab‑subbase interface to address loss of support that leads to transverse joint faulting; it applies when indicators such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks, or slab settlement are observed (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION) [1]. The same source requires joint and crack sealing to be performed concurrently with slab stabilization in order to limit water entry that contributes to pumping and future faulting, and it calls for subsurface drains to be kept in good condition (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION) [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofit may be used to restore load‑transfer capacity for faulting or poor transfer provided existing drainage is functional and the subgrade is not unstable (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. For longitudinal joints that are just beginning to fault, cross‑stitching is recommended to tie the lanes together and prevent further migration (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. When a joint already exhibits faulting, replacement of the joint with full‑depth concrete pavement patching is permitted as an alternative (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. Slab stabilization or jacking must be performed before placing a concrete overlay whenever slab movement or faulting is present, and any slabs identified as rocking, pumping, or faulted shall be stabilized prior to overlay placement (section 4.9 REHABILITATION) [1]. In the context of unbonded concrete overlays, joints that show greater than ¼‑inch faulting must be milled when a geotextile interlayer is specified, and if joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch either a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix shall be placed at the joint or the joint shall be milled/grinded to reduce the faulting (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. The specifications for unbonded concrete overlays similarly require that any area showing unstable slabs or slab fragments, which would otherwise give non‑uniform support to the overlay, be stabilized or repaired before the interlayer is placed (Section 548.3(e) Surface Preparation) [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is used, joints exhibiting more than ¼‑inch of faulting must be milled (Section 548.3(3)(a)) [2][1]. When an asphalt interlayer is specified, any joint with faulting greater than ½‑inch must receive either a scratch course or milling/grinding to reduce the faulting (Section 548.3(3)(b)) [2][1].

Specification limits and acceptance

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242 Change No. 10) Chapter 10 Subsection 10.7 for unbonded PCC overlay of PCC pavement, faulting is used as a treatment trigger; any joint with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting must be milled before the geotextile interlayer is placed [1]. The same manual states that when joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch the specification calls for placing a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix at the joint or milling/grinding the joint to reduce the faulting [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfac ed Pavements, subsection 548.3 Construction requires that any joint exhibiting faulting greater than 1/4‑inch be milled prior to placement of the geotextile interlayer [2]. The Pennsylvania specification further requires that when joint faulting exceeds 1/2‑inch a scratch course must be placed at the joint or the joint must be milled/grinded to reduce the faulting before installing the asphalt interlayer [2].
