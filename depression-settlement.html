Depression / Settlement

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Depression / Settlement is a topic under Distress.
depressiondepressionssettlementsettlements
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Depression or settlement is a localized low‑spot profile deficiency that can occur in both rigid (concrete) and flexible (asphalt) pavements, appearing as small surface depressions—often called blow‑holes, wheel ruts, potholes, settled base areas, or slab settlement near transverse joints, cracks, corners, or embankment movement[1][2]. When the depth of a low spot exceeds the allowable tolerances—generally greater than three‑sixteenths to one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot run, edge slump limited to one‑eighth inch between lanes, or depressions deeper than three‑eighths inch in the base course—the section is deemed defective because it loses the positive cross‑slope needed for drainage[1][2]. The resulting water trapping pumps beneath the slab, reduces subgrade support and increases deflection and stresses that manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and accelerated deterioration, undermining ride quality, structural integrity and service life[1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Depression or settlement is described as a localized low‑spot profile deficiency that can appear in both rigid (concrete) and flexible pavements, manifesting as small surface depressions often called blow‑holes, wheel ruts, potholes, settled base areas, or slab settlement near transverse joints, cracks, slab corners, or embankment movement. When these low spots exceed the allowable tolerance—generally greater than three‑sixteenths to one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot length, with edge slump limited to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes and depressions deeper than three‑eighths of an inch—the pavement is considered defective. The primary performance problem addressed by correcting depression/settlement is the loss of positive cross‑slope that allows water to become trapped, compromising drainage and accelerating water infiltration into the pavement structure. Trapped water promotes pumping at the lowest point of a depression, increases slab deflection, generates excessive stresses that lead to joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and other distress that degrade ride quality and threaten structural integrity. Consequently, the condition undermines both the smoothness required for safe vehicle operation and the long‑term load‑bearing capacity of the pavement system. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 149–151), SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 263–305), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–441), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 597–598)

The primary aim of treating pavement depressions and settlement is to restore the structural support and rideability of the affected area so that the surface again meets its intended functional performance [1][2]. This is achieved by filling voids at the slab‑subbase interface, which re‑establishes full bearing and reduces deflection‑related distress, thereby repairing loss of support and mitigating future cracking, pumping, or faulting [1]. In addition, the treatment seeks to eliminate low spots that exceed accepted tolerances—typically deviations greater than one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot run or depressions deeper than three‑eighths inch—by removing and patching the defective portion of the pavement [2]. When such depressions are identified, the guidelines call for excising at least ten feet of lane width containing the low area, grinding or cutting high points that exceed one‑quarter inch, and installing new asphalt mix or a scratch course that conforms to the surrounding grade, thus restoring a smooth, functional surface [2]. By recreating the original cross‑slope and providing a positive drainage gradient, the repaired section prevents water accumulation that could otherwise accelerate moisture‑related deterioration, further protecting the pavement from progressive damage [1][2]. The combined effect of restoring structural integrity, correcting surface geometry, and re‑establishing proper drainage not only repairs existing damage but also prevents additional deterioration and thereby extends the pavement’s service life [1][2].

Distress characterization and causes

Depressions are identified on the hardened concrete surface of the base course and on the finished reinforced cement‑concrete riding surface, most often observed between adjacent lanes. The specifications set a series of depth limits that define when a low spot becomes a defect: any depression of three‑sixteenths of an inch (0.187 in) or greater is deemed unacceptable for acceptance; a depth exceeding one‑quarter inch (0.25 in) on the riding surface triggers corrective grinding to bring the surface within tolerance; and depressions deeper than three‑eighths of an inch (0.375 in) on the base‑course concrete require removal of the affected lane segment and replacement for at least ten feet in length. Because each threshold is tied to a required remedial action, these depressions are classified as severe enough to be considered defective. The documents do not quantify how rapidly such low spots deteriorate, so no progression rate is provided. Nevertheless, the presence of depressions that exceed the allowable surface‑variation limits is recognized as detrimental to ride quality and can promote water accumulation, thereby creating operational concerns and potential safety hazards if left unaddressed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–374)

Depression and settlement in pavements arise primarily from moisture intrusion that reduces the support beneath the slab, leading to loss of structural capacity [1][2]. Water entering through joints, cracks, or surface defects is pumped by traffic loads into the slab‑subbase interface, producing subgrade consolidation and creating voids that diminish bearing capacity [1]. Inadequate drainage allows those voids to persist and permits ponded water on the subgrade or frost‑affected fill, further weakening support and promoting low spots that exceed allowable tolerances [2][1]. Settlement of the embankment or fill lowers the overall pavement profile, compounding the loss of capacity and generating localized depressions that collect additional water, accelerating deterioration [1][2]. Insufficient compaction of sub‑base or fill layers creates weak zones; specifications require at least 97 % of maximum dry density (100 % in the top three feet) for fills and a minimum field density of 95 % for concrete, with lower densities leading to settlement under traffic [2]. Poor moisture control during construction—such as placing concrete when evaporation exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h or allowing excessive drying at joints—induces shrinkage and loss of strength that further reduces support [2]. Construction deficiencies, including improper joint fabrication, curing, finishing, cracking, honeycombing, and core depths more than 0.5 in below design thickness, lower the effective load‑carrying ability of slabs and hasten depression formation [2][1]. Sub‑base irregularities greater than one inch, open patch areas left un‑patched for extended periods, or foreign material on the subgrade also diminish support and contribute to settlement [2]. The combined effect of water infiltration, drainage failure, inadequate compaction, weak sub‑base conditions, and construction shortcomings accelerates the development of surface depressions that may exceed the defect thresholds of a quarter inch for pavement and three‑sixteenths of an inch in patch areas [2][1].

Applicability and treatment selection

Depression or settlement in a Portland cement concrete slab is suitable for slabjacking when the pavement exhibits a localized low area but the ambient temperature is not excessively high, because high heat expands the slabs and puts joints in compression, making lifting impractical. The method works best on slabs that are already in compression for extensive profile deficiencies such as embankment settlement, provided the subgrade and drainage conditions are acceptable and joint or crack locations allow proper hole placement. Traffic impact is limited by confining drilling and pumping to a single lane protected with a temporary traffic control (MPT) setup, ensuring safe operation while the slab is raised. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93)

Depressions or settlements that are readily visible as surface dips, that demand additional structural capacity, or that involve extensive slab movement are considered unsuitable for slab‑stabilization treatment because the process only restores sub‑base support where voids have caused minor joint faulting and does not raise the pavement surface or increase its design load‑bearing strength [1]. Consequently, when a depression remains after stabilization or when higher bearing capacity is required, more comprehensive interventions such as full‑depth patching, joint and crack sealing, or broader rehabilitation must be selected instead of slab stabilization [1]. The typical stabilization operation involves drilling holes at joints or cracks and injecting cement‑based grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane, followed by a traffic closure until the material cures; however, this method is impractical when pavement temperatures are high enough to place slabs in compression, when the depression exceeds practical lift limits, when subgrade or drainage conditions are poor, or when numerous joints and cracks would impede grout placement, prompting the use of full slab replacement or an alternative preservation technique [1]. Across the guides, any depression that exceeds the dimensional tolerances prescribed in the specifications is also deemed unsuitable for minor preservation actions such as surface grinding or simple patching and must be addressed with more extensive rehabilitation measures [2]. For concrete pavements, a cross‑slope deviation greater than one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot length cannot be eliminated by extra‑depth diamond grinding; instead the pavement must first receive structural repairs—including slab stabilization, slab jacking, dowel retrofit, or spall patching—before any grinding is attempted [2]. Likewise, when a low spot in the base course exceeds three‑eighths of an inch, surface grinding or cutting is prohibited and the affected lane segment, at least ten feet long and full width, must be removed and replaced [2]. In flexible pavements, potholes or abrupt depressions deeper than three inches are beyond the scope of a simple Type A manual asphalt patch and require the more extensive Type B procedure that adds an asphalt binder course; patches smaller than one square foot also fall outside acceptable limits, necessitating an alternative maintenance approach [2]. Finally, if a rigid pavement exhibits depressions or high points that, in the judgment of the Representative, cannot be brought within the surface‑tolerance limits of Section 506.3(o), the lot is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced with a new panel rather than patched or otherwise preserved [2].

Condition assessment and timing

A systematic visual inspection is the first step, looking for signs of lost support such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small blow‑hole depressions at joints or cracks, corner breaks and observable slab settlement. The same visual walk also seeks low spots or high points that exceed the tolerances defined in the applicable specifications, with particular attention to cross‑slope uniformity and edge slumping. To quantify any suspected depression, a profile measurement is made using string lines positioned about one inch above the intended finished grade together with gauge blocks, recording the vertical gap at regular intervals along the affected length. In addition, cross‑slope uniformity is checked at approximately 100‑foot longitudinal intervals and any deviation greater than a quarter inch over a twelve‑foot run is recorded as a depression requiring correction. Straightedge surveys are performed after concrete hardening; a twelve‑foot straightedge is laid longitudinally on the base‑course surface and across joints, and low spots exceeding three‑eighths of an inch (or three‑sixteenths of an inch for finished patches) trigger removal, grinding or replacement. Reinforced concrete slabs are similarly surveyed with a twelve‑foot straightedge or crown template across the width at five‑foot intervals, and variations greater than one‑quarter inch are deemed defects that must be corrected. A detailed roadway profile survey compares the as‑built profile to the design grade line, requiring the finished surface to fall within ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs; over‑jacking beyond 0.10 ft mandates removal and replacement. In‑place field density testing according to ASTM C1040 is conducted within thirty minutes of final compaction to verify that wet‑density requirements have been met, since insufficient compaction can lead to settlement. Edge slumping is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge while the concrete is still plastic and again after hardening; depressions greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch between adjacent lanes are considered defective and must be corrected. The measurements of length, width and maximum depth obtained from the string‑line/gauge block method, together with the locations of joints or cracks, guide the placement of grout injection holes; the hole pattern is defined in the field based on the size of the depressed area, required correction magnitude, drainage conditions and proximity to joints. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–441), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 597–598)

Depression or settlement treatment is required as soon as the pavement exhibits early indications that the slab no longer has adequate support, such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small blow‑hole depressions at joints or cracks, corner breaks, or any measurable overall slab settlement [1]. In addition, the guides specify that treatment must be initiated whenever a low‑spot can be measured and exceeds the dimensional tolerances established for the surface profile [2]. A cross‑slope deviation greater than one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge is considered out of tolerance and triggers corrective work to restore the original slope [2]. Likewise, any vertical depression in the base course that exceeds three‑eighths of an inch requires removal of the affected lane segment for at least ten feet and replacement of the section [2]. Between adjacent lanes a depression of three‑sixteenths of an inch (0.1875 in) or more is classified as defective, and variations greater than one‑quarter inch are also deemed defects that must be brought back within tolerance, often by grinding [2]. When low spots are encountered during surface preparation before placement of new binder or wearing courses, a scratch or leveling course is applied to bring the surface level with surrounding pavement [2]. For abrupt depressions or potholes, the depth governs the patch type; depressions three inches deep or less are repaired with a Type A manual asphalt patch using only the wearing course, while deeper depressions receive a Type B patch that incorporates both binder and wearing courses [2]. In reinforced‑concrete pavements, the recommended method is cement‑grout jacking to raise the slab until the profile falls within approximately 0.02 ft of the design grade, after which acceptance is based on post‑stabilization profiling [2]. The material selected for void filling in concrete slabs—cement grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane—is pumped through drilled holes and must be allowed to cure (approximately twelve hours for grout) or cool and harden (minimum thirty minutes for asphalt/polyurethane) before traffic is reopened [1]. By addressing these conditions promptly, the full support of the slab is restored, deflections are reduced, and further faulting, pumping, and cracking are mitigated [1].

Materials and repair details

Depression or settlement in concrete pavements is typically corrected by a slab‑jacking operation that begins with drilling a series of holes at the low point of the defect, often located along a joint or crack; the pattern and spacing of the holes are selected based on the size of the depressed area, the required lift, subgrade conditions, and proximity to existing joints [1]. Once the holes are positioned, grout material is pumped beneath the slab until full support is regained and the surface reaches the target elevation that is established with string lines and gauge blocks placed about one inch above the desired grade [1]. Three grout options are permitted: a cement‑based fluid grout composed of Portland cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and any required admixtures, typically estimated at roughly one cubic foot per hole or about a quarter bag of cement (approximately 0.40 bag per hole) [1]; hot liquid asphalt used as an undersealing agent, generally requiring between thirty and fifty gallons per drilled hole [1]; and a two‑component high‑density polyurethane foam that is low‑viscosity before mixing, with design quantities of about 0.4 pound per square foot when the asphalt method is employed and about 0.7 pound per square foot for the slab‑jacking foam, which expands into a rigid closed‑cell material that fills the void [1][2]. After pumping, the cement grout is allowed to cure for roughly twelve hours before traffic can be reopened, the hot asphalt must cool, and the polyurethane must harden, requiring at least thirty minutes as specified by the mix design; finally, the treated joint or crack is sealed to limit future water infiltration [1].

For concrete pavements, any necessary patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization or slab jacking must be completed before diamond grinding can commence, and these actions restore the original transverse cross‑slope so that low spots exceeding a quarter‑inch deviation measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge are eliminated to provide positive drainage [2]. High points above the one‑quarter‑inch limit are removed by grinding or cutting tools, while depressions deeper than three‑eighths of an inch trigger excavation of the full lane width for a minimum length of ten feet and replacement with standard concrete mix and the prescribed joint configuration; if after grinding a low spot of three‑sixteenths of an inch or more remains between adjacent lanes, the slab is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced, observing edge‑slip limits of one‑eighth inch between lanes (or one‑quarter inch to shoulders) [2]. An alternative stabilization method uses cement grout pumped under continuous pressure up to 200 psi through a drill equipped with an expanding rubber packer; the pavement is jacked until it lies within ±0.02 ft of the design string line for continuously reinforced slabs or within ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, after which any excess lift is ground off and over‑jacking beyond 0.10 ft triggers removal and replacement [2].

For flexible asphalt surfaces, depressions three inches deep or less are repaired with a Type A manual patch, while deeper voids require a Type B patch; the repair begins with a vertical cut and excavation to sound material, creating a minimum one‑square‑foot patch area, followed by application of a tack coat to the bottom and all vertical faces before placement of hot‑mix asphalt, and edges are sealed with emulsified asphalt, Class TACK or NTT, or the same PG‑binder used in the existing pavement [2]. Shallow patches receive only an asphalt wearing course that is compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller to match the surrounding grade, whereas deeper patches are built up by placing an asphalt binder course in lifts not exceeding three inches uncompacted, followed by a 1½‑inch wearing layer; all work must be completed within the same workday and compacted to conform to the existing surface, and plant‑mix material may be placed manually or with mechanical spreading and finishing equipment [2]. When an overlay is planned, a dedicated scratch course is installed to fill wheel ruts and other localized low spots flush with the existing pavement, and a leveling course provides a uniformly smooth platform for subsequent binder or wearing courses; both are applied after removal of loose material and before any tack coat [2].

The performance of depression and settlement repairs is first governed by the condition of the existing slab and its supporting layers, because voids that have developed next to transverse joints, cracks or corner breaks, and infiltration of fines and water into the sub‑grade all diminish the ability of any grout or fill material to bond and transfer load [1]. Loss of slab support creates excessive deflection and stress that can appear as joint faulting, diagonal cracking and eventual breakup of the pavement, so a sound base is essential before any corrective action is taken [1][2]. Ambient temperature also influences treatment choice; high temperatures cause concrete slabs to expand and place joints in compression, making slab‑jacking impractical, whereas slabs already under compression respond more favorably to lift operations [1].

When selecting a repair material, the chosen product must match both the physical condition of the pavement and the compatibility requirements of existing sealants. A two‑component high‑density polyurethane with low viscosity can flow into fine voids and cure to a rigid closed‑cell foam that resists water and chemical attack, providing long‑term durability for both flexible and rigid sections [1]. Cementitious grout is another option but it requires sufficient curing time before traffic returns, while hot asphalt may be used only after it has cooled to an appropriate temperature [1]. For flexible pavements the void must be excavated to sound, stable material, all loose debris removed, and the remaining surfaces dried before any bonding agent is applied; a uniform tack coat—either a PennDOT‑approved emulsified asphalt (Class TACK) or the hot binder that matches the existing PG‑binder grade—is required on both the bottom and vertical faces of the void, and the edges are sealed with an emulsified asphalt or compatible Class TACK/NTT sealant using the same binder as the surrounding course [2].

Rigid‑pavement repairs must respect the concrete’s designed thickness and strength. The slab must retain at least the design thickness minus 0.5 in, and its compressive‑strength quality index (Percent Within Limits) must be 55 percent or greater; any core below these limits renders the lot defective [2]. Concrete placed for the repair should have a water‑to‑cement ratio between 0.37 and 0.42, be mixed at temperatures of 50 °F to 90 °F, and be compacted to at least 85 percent of maximum wet density for lifts under six inches [2]. The subgrade or subbase beneath the repair must be clean, free of foreign material, ponded water, frost, and any irregularities deeper than one inch; such irregularities must be removed or filled and then re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors before concrete placement [2].

During cement‑grout stabilization the applied pressure may not exceed 200 psi continuously, may rise to 300 psi only for short periods, and can reach 600 psi briefly when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. When jacking continuously reinforced concrete, the lift is limited to within ±0.02 ft of the string‑line grade; any overjacking greater than 0.10 ft requires removal and replacement of the surface [2]. After the patch cures, a 12‑foot straightedge is used to verify surface tolerance: depressions or high points greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch between lanes are considered defective and must be ground out, while overall surface deviation may not exceed one‑quarter inch; edge slump is limited to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes, and longitudinal joint tolerances must stay within one‑quarter inch [2]. Reinforcement used in the repair must be free of rust, oil, grease, or other contaminants to ensure proper bond and prevent localized settling [2].

Timing also affects performance; an excavated area may not remain open for more than 48 hours without a temporary graded and compacted fill to prevent water accumulation that could exacerbate settlement, and if the patch must stay open longer, suitable material must be placed to grade and compact to non‑movement [2]. Finally, maintaining subsurface drainage after repair is essential to avoid further pumping of fines or water into voids, thereby supporting the long‑term effectiveness of the treatment [1].

Treatment procedures

The treatment of a pavement depression begins with establishing temporary traffic‑control measures and developing a quality‑control plan that includes surveyed profiles of the existing low spots and the target grade line, which are submitted for approval before work starts [1][2]. String lines are staked approximately one inch above the intended finished elevation and gauge blocks are positioned on the slab to provide a visual reference for the required lift, while the surface is cleared of loose debris, foreign material, excess joint sealer or crack filler; if the binder course cannot be cleaned satisfactorily it is removed and replaced [1][2]. High spots exceeding one‑quarter inch are identified with a straight‑edge test after the concrete base has hardened and are ground or cut away, and any depressions deeper than three‑eighths inch trigger removal of the full lane width for a minimum length of ten feet, or, when the defect cannot be corrected, the entire panel is removed and replaced with new concrete matching the original design thickness [2]. For smaller depressions, vertical edges are cut around the marked area, the material is excavated to sound, stable substrate, debris is cleared, and the exposed surfaces are cleaned and dried before a tack coat is applied to the bottom and sides and allowed to cure prior to overlay placement [2]. Holes are then drilled at the affected joints or cracks immediately before any fill material is placed; the drilling pattern follows the size of the low spot, subgrade conditions, and joint locations, allowing both the drilling crew and the stabilizing crew to share the same traffic‑control protection [1][2]. The selected fill—either a cement‑based grout, hot liquid asphalt, or a two‑component high‑density polyurethane—is pumped into the voids at the slab/subbase interface, beginning at the lowest point of the depression and progressing outward in incremental lifts while monitoring contact between gauge blocks and string lines to achieve the desired profile [1][2]. For grout jacking, on‑site mixed grout is pumped through expanding rubber packers at pressures up to 200 psi until the surface is raised within 0.02 ft for continuous reinforced concrete or 0.03 ft for jointed slabs; any excess lift greater than 0.10 ft is corrected by grinding, and over‑jacking beyond that limit requires removal and replacement of the pavement [2]. When asphalt is used, a binder course is placed in lifts not exceeding three inches uncompacted depth, each lift being leveled and compacted with a mechanical tamper, followed by a top wearing layer of about one‑and‑a‑half inches finished with roller compaction; small wheel ruts or local depressions may be filled with a scratch course and a leveling course applied to provide a uniform working platform before the binder or wearing layers [2]. Full‑depth rigid‑pavement repairs involve diamond‑tipped saw cuts around the patch perimeter, removal of the damaged concrete in one or more pieces while minimizing disturbance to the subbase, placement of rapid‑set or specified concrete per the mix design, striking off and finishing to match surrounding texture, and application of curing compounds or a white pigmented membrane as recommended by the manufacturer [2]. After placement, the surface is kept closed to traffic until the fill cures: cement grout requires up to twelve hours, hot asphalt must cool for at least thirty minutes, polyurethane needs a minimum of thirty minutes for expansion set, and newly placed concrete may be opened only after achieving a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi [1][2]. Joints and cracks are then sealed, drainage maintenance is performed to limit future water infiltration, and test cylinders are taken from each sublot for verification of depth, strength, and air content before final acceptance [1][2].

Implementation begins with a surface assessment that measures cross‑slope and identifies low spots exceeding the tolerance of one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot length before any repair work proceeds [2]. For rigid pavements the crew first establishes string lines and places gauge blocks across the affected lane to define the target slab profile, after which a drilling rig creates holes at the predetermined locations; the drilling is timed to occur immediately before the stabilizing or slab‑jacking operation so that both activities can be protected by a single Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) setup confined to one travel lane [1]. The pump then delivers the selected fill material—cement‑based grout, hot liquid asphalt, or low‑viscosity polyurethane—into the drilled voids, with pumping initiated at the lowest point of the depression and progressing outward while the slab is lifted incrementally until the gauge blocks contact the string line [1]. The choice of material determines the post‑injection traffic reopening interval; cement grout may require up to twelve hours of curing before traffic can be restored, whereas hot asphalt and polyurethane generally need a minimum of thirty minutes for cooling or hardening [1]. High ambient temperatures are avoided because slab expansion places joints in compression, rendering slab‑jacking ineffective; therefore operations are scheduled for cooler conditions when the slabs are already in compression [1]. When concrete repairs such as patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization or jacketing are required, they must be completed before any diamond grinding is undertaken, and a power‑driven self‑propelled machine equipped with diamond blades is used to grind the surface to the specified smoothness and texture tolerances [2]. Grinding is prohibited within two feet of curbs, bridge barriers or other roadway obstacles, and high points exceeding one‑quarter inch are removed by grinding or cutting tools; depressions greater than three‑eighths of an inch trigger removal of the full lane width for a minimum length of ten feet followed by replacement with new material [2]. For flexible pavements damaged sections are cut out using a milling or planing machine, excavated to sound subgrade, and all debris is cleared before a tack coat is applied to thoroughly dried surfaces; the tack coat must cure prior to placement of asphalt lifts [2]. Asphalt is placed in successive lifts—binder or wearing course as required—with each lift leveled and compacted using approved equipment such as steel‑wheeled rollers, smooth‑drum rollers, padfoot or vibratory rollers operated at speeds not exceeding 1.5 ft/s, while moisture content is maintained within a few percent of optimum and frozen material is prohibited [2]. After each lift the surface is fine‑graded to eliminate low spots and sealed at the end of the workday with a smooth‑drum roller; any remaining wheel ruts or localized depressions are filled with a scratch or leveling course before overlay, and milling may be required at tie‑in locations using the equipment specified in Section 491.3(a) [2]. When grout stabilization is selected for rigid pavements, a dedicated grout plant, water tanker and drill are mobilized; continuous pump pressures up to 200 psi are allowed, with short‑term increases to 300 psi and brief spikes to 600 psi permitted only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, and over‑jacking beyond one‑tenth foot must be corrected by grinding or replacement [2]. Throughout all activities slurry, cooling water or dust must be prevented from entering traffic lanes or drainage features, and any open patch area may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours; if rain infiltrates an open patch, an outlet is excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point to drain water [2]. Traffic protection measures include restricting work to a single lane under MPT, keeping traffic off the binder course whenever feasible to avoid contaminating fresh material, and delaying reopening of repaired sections until the concrete attains required strength and drop‑off conditions are mitigated as specified in Section 901.3(j) [1][2]. The overall sequence therefore integrates surface assessment, targeted drilling, material injection or removal, curing or compaction, and final grinding or finishing, all governed by temperature, moisture, and traffic control constraints to ensure safe and durable correction of depressions and settlements [1][2].

Quality and workmanship

When a depression or settlement condition is being evaluated, the first material‑related checks focus on the sub‑base and the structural layers that will support the pavement surface. Inspectors confirm that the sub‑base consists of the specified granular material and that any surface irregularities deeper than one inch are corrected by loosening, adding or removing material before it is compacted to the required grade, while the concrete base course is examined after hardening with a twelve‑foot straightedge both longitudinally and transversely to identify high spots greater than one‑quarter inch for grinding or cutting and low areas deeper than three‑eighths inch for removal and replacement; the appropriate asphalt wearing‑course or binder‑wearing combination for Type A or Type B patches is also verified for correct lift thicknesses before compaction [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 304–305), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–441), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 597–598)

A satisfactory treatment of a pavement depression or settlement is declared when the slab regains full structural support and none of the distress indicators that signal loss of support are observed, such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, blow‑holes at joints or cracks, corner breaks, or measurable slab settlement. [1] The underlying voids must be completely filled and the lifted portion raised to a predetermined reference line normally positioned about one inch above the intended finished grade, with pumping slowed as gauge blocks on the slab approach this line and stopped when contact is made; if grout or polyurethane begins to extrude from joints, cracks, or edges before that elevation is reached, additional lifting and injection are required. [1] Traffic may be reopened only after the injected material has fully cured—at least twelve hours for cement‑based grout or a minimum of thirty minutes for polyurethane—to ensure the restored support remains stable. [1] In addition to these functional criteria, the repaired surface must satisfy the tolerance limits set for pavement elevations, whereby transverse deviation over a twelve‑foot straightedge may not exceed one‑quarter inch on general surfaces and three‑sixteenths of an inch on repaired rigid pavements. [2] Any depression in a hardened concrete base‑course greater than three‑eighths of an inch must be eliminated by removing the full lane width for a minimum length of ten feet, while high points exceeding one‑quarter inch must be ground smooth to bring the grade within the allowable range. [2] Reinforced cement concrete surfaces are acceptable only when the finished grade lies within a quarter‑inch of the intended elevation, and plain concrete pavements require an even tighter control of three‑sixteenths of an inch measured both parallel to the centreline and perpendicular within a lane. [2] Edge slump between adjacent lanes is limited to one‑eighth inch and between a lane and its shoulder to one‑quarter inch, ensuring that no edge furrows impede runoff. [2] When correcting a depression the original cross‑slope must be restored to a uniform positive slope that provides continuous drainage, and any repair patch must cover at least one square foot; after compaction the patch surface must align with the surrounding pavement grade so that no residual low spot remains. [2] Continuously reinforced concrete slabs must be raised or lowered to fall within plus or minus 0.02 ft of the target string line, whereas jointed slabs and bridge‑end slabs may deviate up to plus or minus 0.03 ft; any overjacking greater than 0.10 ft is considered defective and requires removal and replacement. [2] Embankments or fill surfaces are acceptable only after fine‑grading to eliminate all depressions, sealing the surface, and compacting the material to at least 97 % of maximum dry density (100 % in the top three feet), with no edge furrows that would prohibit free drainage. [2] The work is inspected by the Representative, and any depression or high point that cannot be brought within these tolerances is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no cost to the Department. Performance and service life

Depression or settlement beneath a concrete slab creates voids that remove support, leading to excessive slab deflection and stresses that manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup, thereby degrading the pavement’s condition, functional ride quality, durability and shortening its service life. When these voids are filled with grout, hot asphalt or polyurethane, full support is restored, reducing deflections and limiting further pumping, faulting and cracking, which improves structural integrity and extends service life, although it does not correct surface depressions nor increase the design structural capacity. Surface depressions and settlement that exceed allowable tolerances—generally one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge or three‑sixteenths of an inch between adjacent lanes—interrupt the designed cross‑slope, trap water and impede drainage. The resulting ponding promotes moisture infiltration into the pavement structure, degrades ride quality, compromises load transfer and accelerates cracking and other forms of deterioration, reducing structural condition, functional performance, durability and anticipated service life if left untreated. To restore the intended condition, specifications require correction of any area exceeding the tolerance by diamond grinding or fine‑grading to remove high points and fill depressions, followed by re‑establishing a positive cross‑slope that ensures continuous runoff. The corrected surface must be verified with straightedge testing in both longitudinal and transverse directions and documented for each test location; if the surface still exceeds the limits, the defective panel—particularly in low‑strength concrete pavements—must be removed and replaced according to standard drawings. By eliminating depressions, re‑creating proper drainage and confirming compliance through repeated testing, the pavement regains its designed structural integrity, maintains smoothness and ride quality, and thereby preserves the durability and service life originally projected for a properly constructed surface. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 149–151), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–441)

Depression and settlement in rigid pavements generate a set of recurring distresses that stem from loss of support beneath the slab, producing excessive deflection that appears as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and, when severe, full‑depth breakup; small blow‑hole depressions may also develop at transverse joints, cracks or outer corners. The primary mechanisms that create the underlying voids are pumping action, subgrade consolidation and water infiltration, which are aggravated by construction deficiencies such as concrete depth falling more than 0.5 in below design thickness, post‑wet‑cure compressive strength less than 55 % of the specified PWL, rain exposure during placement, inadequate joint formation, insufficient finishing, honeycombing or cracking, and subbase irregularities deeper than one inch that are not fully recompacted. These conditions also give rise to low‑spot surface irregularities that exceed the tolerance of three‑sixteenths of an inch between adjacent lanes (or one‑eighth inch edge slump), causing the lot to be classified as defective when they cannot be corrected by grinding. Treatment failures most often occur when remedial actions do not fully address the voids or are performed under unfavorable conditions, such as slab‑jacking with an improperly designed hole pattern that ignores the size of the depressed area, required lift, subgrade and drainage characteristics, and proximity to existing joints or cracks, which can induce additional cracking, tilting and faulting. Performing slab‑jacking during periods of high ambient temperature is also impractical because thermal expansion places slabs in compression, increasing the risk of joint damage. Stabilization methods that fill the voids—cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or high‑density polyurethane foam—can restore support and reduce further deflection but do not correct surface depressions nor increase overall structural capacity; each material imposes its own traffic restoration window, with cement grout requiring roughly twelve hours to cure and asphalt or polyurethane demanding a minimum of thirty minutes before traffic may resume. Successful grout stabilization demands precise control of volume and injection pressure, limiting continuous pressures to 200 psi (briefly up to 300 psi, and up to 600 psi only when the slab is bonded to the subgrade), and achieving a final elevation within ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs; exceeding an over‑jacking of ten inches triggers removal and replacement of the pavement at no additional cost. Elevation control during any lift is typically monitored with a string line positioned about one inch above the target grade together with gauge blocks to verify incremental lift and maintain the intended profile. If a depression equal to or greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch cannot be eliminated by grinding, the section is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced under the applicable provisions; persistent edge slumping after hardening also requires corrective methods approval and may lead to replacement. Detecting low spots while the concrete is still plastic using a straightedge and applying immediate correction, followed by proper striking‑off, consolidation and refinish, helps prevent many of the settlement‑related failures that later manifest as recurring depressions. Monitoring and follow-up

After the repair has cured and the material has achieved sufficient strength, a comprehensive post‑treatment inspection is required. First, the cross‑slope of the repaired lane is surveyed at roughly 100‑foot intervals along its length; each station is documented together with the deepest residual depression, and any deviation exceeding one‑quarter inch over a 12‑ft run is corrected. The surface is then examined with a 12‑foot straightedge or crown template, moving the tool in successive positions parallel to the centerline so that the entire width of the repair is covered both longitudinally and across joints. Low spots greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch are ground down and rechecked, while depressions exceeding one‑quarter inch are deemed defective and must be removed or repaired before acceptance. For concrete repairs, if a depression exceeds three‑eighths of an inch the affected lane segment is removed and replaced for at least ten feet in length. A field quality‑control plan also calls for a resurveyed roadway profile that records the existing grade, the design grade, and the as‑built grade; these values are compared with allowable tolerances of 0.02 ft for continuous pavement or 0.03 ft for jointed slabs, and any excess may trigger additional grout injection. Finally, a ride‑quality evaluation such as longitudinal roughness or IRI testing is performed to verify that the repaired surface meets the required smoothness criteria before final acceptance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–402), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 597–598)

When the pavement surface exhibits localized low areas that exceed the dimensional tolerances established for the structure, additional work is required [2]. A transverse survey that records a depression greater than one‑quarter inch over any twelve‑foot segment is taken as a trigger for corrective action [2]. Similarly, straightedge or crown‑template inspections that reveal low spots of three‑sixteenths of an inch or more are deemed deficient and must be ground to within the allowable limit; failure of grinding to achieve this result classifies the area defective and calls for removal and replacement at no cost to the agency [2]. In hardened concrete base courses, localized depressions deeper than three‑eighths of an inch require removal and replacement of the affected section, and when such low spots extend across the full lane width for ten feet or more the entire lane segment must be reconstructed [2]. The presence of a cross‑slope that does not provide a uniform positive drainage gradient also signals the need for repair because water can collect in the depression [2]. Visual signs of loss of slab support, such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines along joints or cracks on either the traffic lane or shoulder, and the appearance of small blow‑hole depressions at a joint or crack, are interpreted as indicators that further maintenance is required [1]. Corner breaks observed in the pavement edge likewise point to inadequate correction of the depression and may necessitate additional treatment [1]. If grout begins to extrude from joints, cracks, or the pavement edge before the slab reaches its prescribed profile line, this is taken as evidence that the original jacking was insufficient and new drill holes with further slab‑jacking are needed [1]. After a lift, any cracking, tilting, or faulting of the slab indicates improper jacking and prompts re‑evaluation for additional repair or rehabilitation [1]. Post‑stabilization profile checks that show the lifted pavement remaining outside the allowable grade tolerance—within plus or minus 0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete or plus or minus 0.03 ft for jointed slabs—trigger further injection, and an overjacking exceeding one‑tenth of a foot requires removal and replacement of the pavement [2]. When the cumulative evidence shows that depressions cannot be satisfactorily corrected to meet the surface‑tolerance limits set out in Section 506.3(o), the lot is declared defective and the responsible authority directs repair or replacement under district jurisdiction [2]. When surface variations exceed one‑quarter inch, the guides require removal of high points by grinding to bring the profile within tolerance before any other remedial action is taken [2].
