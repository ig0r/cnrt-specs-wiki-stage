Depression / Settlement

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Depression / Settlement is a topic under Distress.
depressiondepressionssettlementsettlements
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Depression or settlement in concrete pavements refers to a localized loss of support that creates a low spot on the surface and may appear as small depressions—often called blow‑holes—in the shoulder, overall slab sagging, or excessive deflection [1]. The condition arises when pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or water infiltration generate voids beneath the slab, most commonly adjacent to transverse joints, cracks, outside slab corners, and at the interface with plain cement‑concrete shoulders [1]. This loss of support can lead to joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup, thereby reducing structural capacity, ride quality and safety [1].

Description and identification

Depression or settlement in concrete pavements refers to a localized loss of support that creates a low spot on the surface and may also be observed as overall slab sagging or excessive deflection. The condition can arise when pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or other causes create voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and at outside slab corners. The loss of support produces small depressions—often called blow‑holes—in the shoulder adjacent to a joint or crack and may be observed as an extensive profile deficiency in which the slab sits lower than surrounding sections. In polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing (Section 483) depression or settlement appears as a longitudinal streak, rut, ridge, bump or depression that drops below the surrounding level; any ridge, bump or depression greater than ¼ inch measured over a 10‑foot straightedge, or any rut showing a measurable depression when checked with a 12‑foot straightedge, is considered defective. In a plain cement concrete base course (Section 301) a depression or settlement is a localized low spot on the hardened surface that exceeds 3⁄8 inch when measured with straightedges; the specification requires removal and replacement of the full lane width for a length of at least 10 feet when such a condition is directed in writing. Manual asphalt patching (Section 450) treats depressions as potholes or abrupt depressions that create localized low spots; a depression three inches deep or less is classified as Type A, while one deeper than three inches is classified as Type B, and each type requires preparation and patching of the affected area with asphalt plant‑mix material placed manually or with mechanical spreading and finishing equipment. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) define a depression as a localized low spot where the finished surface deviates from a straightedge by more than ¼ inch; such depressions are identified with a 12‑foot straightedge test and must be corrected by grinding high points until the surface conforms to tolerance. Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) defines depression or settlement as a low‑spot surface defect that falls outside the mainline pavement surface tolerance, and if it cannot be satisfactorily corrected the lot is considered defective under the “Other than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance” provision. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 506) likewise treat depressions or settlements as localized low spots that are defects; a lot is defective when such depressions (or high points) cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative. Next‑generation concrete surfaces (Section 509) describe depression or settlement as a dip caused by subsidence, edge slump, or other localized factors that exceeds the allowable slope tolerance; no depressions or misalignment of slope greater than 1⁄8 inch over a 12‑foot length when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline will be allowed, although depressed areas may be excluded from this requirement if approved. Concrete pavement patching (Section 516) defines depression as a localized low spot on the finished patch surface that exceeds the allowable tolerance; it appears as a dip along longitudinal or transverse joints and is identified by testing with a 12‑foot straightedge in both directions, and any dip greater than 3⁄16 inch must be removed by grinding. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501) define depression or settlement similarly as a localized low spot that creates a dip on the riding surface and can trap water; when the hardened slab deviates downward more than 3⁄16 inch between adjacent lanes, the pavement is classified as defective under the Surface Tolerance requirements, and while the concrete is still plastic the same straightedge test is used to immediately correct low areas before striking off, consolidating and refinishing. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–341), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–452)

Depression/settlement is recognized by observing small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at transverse joints or cracks, which signals loss of slab support. It is distinguished from transverse joint faulting, fines that accumulate near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, and corner breaks, each presenting a different symptom despite all being linked to voids beneath the concrete. In Section 514, the transverse tolerance check measures pavement cross‑slope at 100‑foot intervals and records any deviation that exceeds a depth of greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length; the maximum depth at each test location is documented and all areas showing those deviations must be corrected, which separates this condition from other distresses addressed by texture, longitudinal roughness, or IRI criteria, and a positive cross slope is required to ensure drainage. In Section 483, a micro‑surfaced lane is inspected for longitudinal streaks that form a ridge, bump or depression, and the condition is considered defective when the irregularity exceeds a 1/4 inch depth measured with a 10‑foot straightedge; this measurement distinguishes it from transverse profile issues such as ruts, which are evaluated separately with a 12‑foot straightedge. In Section 301, after the concrete has hardened, the base‑course surface is retested with straightedges as directed in Section 501.3(k)3, and any low spot measured deeper than 3/8 inch is classified as a depression that must be removed and replaced, whereas high points exceeding 1/4 inch are treated separately by grinding or cutting. In Section 502, the finished RCC surface is tested with a 12‑foot straightedge or crown template moving parallel to the road centerline until the whole width is examined; any area where the pavement surface varies by more than 1/4 inch is flagged as a depression and considered defective, while high points that cause the same variation are corrected by grinding the slab back within tolerance. In Section 506, depressions or settlements are identified by inspecting the pavement surface for low spots or high points that exceed the tolerances set out in Section 506.3(o); they are deemed defective only when a Representative determines they cannot be satisfactorily corrected, which distinguishes them from other surface defects such as rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, finish problems, cracking, or honeycombing that are listed separately and evaluated on their own repairability. In Section 681, depression or settlement is identified by producing a detailed roadway profile of the affected segment; the survey must display any low spots together with the intended grade line, and before acceptance the contractor submits surveyed profile drawings showing the existing profile grade, the proposed grade, and the grade actually achieved after stabilization, allowing the reviewer to differentiate true depressions from other surface irregularities. In Section 509, cross‑slope is checked with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline, and any depression or slope misalignment that exceeds 1/8‑inch over the 12‑foot length is considered non‑conforming; such measured depressions are distinguished from spalls, cracks, or joint faults because they are evaluated as a longitudinal differential, although localized depressed areas caused by subsidence, edge slump, or similar causes may be excluded when approved. In Section 516, depressions are identified during the surface‑tolerance check of a concrete patch by running a 12‑foot straightedge along the longitudinal direction and across the patch joints; any low spot that exceeds three‑sixteenths inch is recorded as a depression, and the same measurement flags high points, allowing the contractor to distinguish true settlement from other surface irregularities, with depressions caused by deficiencies in adjacent existing pavement excluded from corrective responsibility, otherwise requiring grinding smooth at no extra cost. In Section 501, depression or settlement in a concrete slab is identified by performing straight‑edge testing for low spots; when the measured depression between adjacent lanes reaches or exceeds 3/16 inch it is deemed defective according to the surface‑tolerance criteria, while edge slumping is evaluated separately and is acceptable up to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and up to 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders; the distinction therefore lies in the location of the low spot—between travel lanes versus at the edge—and in the specific tolerance values prescribed for each condition. Where it occurs

In the Pavement Policy Manual, depression or settlement is documented on Portland cement concrete pavements, where pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement may create small voids beneath the slab that appear as small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at transverse joints or cracks and as overall slab settlement, especially near transverse joints, cracks and outside slab corners. The same manual notes that slabjacking is applied to Portland cement concrete pavement slabs, filling voids beneath the slab that produce localized low spots affecting rideability; the repair involves lifting the slab from the lowest point of a depression and injecting either cement grout (approximately 0.40 bags per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (approximately 0.7 lb/ft²), using a string line positioned about 1 inch above the desired grade and working in increments to avoid cracking. Additionally, the manual observes depression or settlement at the interface of plain cement‑concrete shoulders and the adjoining cement‑concrete pavement, indicating that any type of cement‑concrete pavement tied to a plain concrete shoulder can develop settlement, while the tied shoulder reduces differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications recognize depression or settlement on a variety of rigid and flexible pavements and associated structures. For plain cement concrete base courses, after hardening the surface must be re‑tested with straightedges; any high point exceeding ¼ inch shall be ground, and any depression greater than ⅜ inch mandates removal of the full lane width for a minimum length of 10 feet. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements are inspected with a 12‑foot straightedge and depressions exceeding ¼ inch are deemed defective and must be corrected by grinding the high points until the tolerance is met. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement treats depression as a surface defect; when depressions or high points cannot be satisfactorily corrected in accordance with Section 506.3(o) the lot is considered defective. Plain and reinforced cement concrete pavements also list depression as a defect; if such depressions cannot be satisfactorily corrected, at least 10 feet of pavement between transverse joints or an entire panel must be removed and replaced. Slabjacking procedures apply to continuously reinforced concrete slabs and jointed concrete slabs—including bridge‑end slabs—where the goal is to raise the surface to within 0.02 foot (continuously reinforced) or 0.03 foot (jointed) of a string‑line grade. The Next Generation Concrete Surface specification requires that no depression or slope misalignment exceed ⅛ inch over a 12‑foot length measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, and localized depressed areas caused by subsidence or edge slump are excluded only if approved; this applies to the rigid concrete surface and its adjacent feather‑pass grind shoulders. On flexible pavements the manual asphalt‑patching provisions describe depression as potholes or abrupt depressions in an asphalt wearing course; depressions three inches deep or less are classified as Type A repairs and shall be performed manually with asphalt wearing‑course material, while deeper depressions are treated as Type B repairs. When a depression extends into a shoulder that has an asphalt base, the shoulder portion shall be filled with asphalt and compacted in the same manner as the roadway, whereas shoulders of other types receive suitable fill material. Concrete pavement patches treat depressions as a surface‑tolerance condition; any high points or depressions greater than 3/16 inch must be corrected by grinding before acceptance, and if the condition remains the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. Under the slip‑form paving method for plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements a low spot of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes is considered defective after the concrete has hardened, evaluated in accordance with the surface‑tolerance provisions of Section 501.3(o). According to the Pavement Policy Manual, depression or settlement in Portland‑cement concrete pavements is found where voids develop beneath the slab, most often adjacent to transverse joints and cracks and especially at the outside corners of the slab. The manual also notes that small depressions (blow holes) may appear in the shoulder directly over a transverse joint or crack, indicating loss of support at the slab/subbase interface. In jointed plain concrete pavements the distress is further noted at the outer edge where the main‑line pavement meets its concrete shoulder. Section 483 of the specifications for a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing system states that depression occurs on the surface layer, particularly along longitudinal joints or edges where streaks appear and in wheel‑path ruts across the transverse profile. The same section requires that any longitudinal streak with a ridge, bump or depression greater than ¼ inch measured over a 10‑foot straightedge be considered a defect, and that ruts be filled so that no depressions remain when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge. Section 450 covering manual asphalt patching describes depressions as potholes or other abrupt low spots on the pavement surface and also as deteriorated edges of the pavement, which are the locations where patching work is performed. In the Superpave mixture design provisions (Section 413), depression or settlement is described as localized low spots such as wheel ruts and other small depressions that develop within the riding surface, i.e., within the wheel paths, and a scratch course shall be used to fill these wheel‑rut depressions so they are even with the surrounding pavement. The next‑generation concrete surface specifications (Section 509) indicate that depressions can arise from subsidence, which would manifest within the slab interior, and from edge slump, which occurs at the pavement edges. Section 501 for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements characterizes depression as surface low spots rather than internal failures; depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more between adjacent lanes are considered defective, and edge slumping measured in the six‑inch width contiguous to the pavement edge shall be corrected. That same section explicitly notes that no association is made with joints, cracks, corners, wheel paths, layer interfaces, base or subgrade for these surface depressions. [1][2]

Causes and mechanisms

According to the PCCP guidelines, depression or settlement in concrete slabs is caused primarily by pumping action, subgrade consolidation, and resulting settlement that create voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and outside corners; water infiltration into the pavement contributes to the pumping phenomenon, making moisture and drainage a key driver of these depressions. The loss of support appears as small depressions (blow holes) at joints or cracks, transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Depression or settlement in Portland cement concrete pavements develops when pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement create small voids beneath the slab, with most voids forming near transverse joints, cracks, and especially at outside slab corners; the loss of support then causes excessive slab deflection that appears as small depressions, joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup. Conditions that accelerate this mechanism include water entering the pavement—particularly through unsealed joints or cracks—and inadequate subsurface drainage, which increase pumping and promote further void formation. During construction, depression in a concrete slab can originate as edge slumping when the freshly placed concrete is not fully consolidated or vibrated, producing localized low spots that are detected with a 12‑foot straightedge before the mix hardens. The specification permits edge slump not to exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and between lanes and ramps, and not to exceed 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders or ramps and shoulders; if these limits cannot be maintained, paving operations must be suspended after 200 feet of work and the section may be deemed defective when depressions reach 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes, allowing water to collect and accelerate further settlement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Severity levels and rating

The plain cement concrete base‑course specifications (Section 301) treat depression as a binary condition: any surface low spot greater than three‑eighths of an inch is deemed unacceptable and must be removed and replaced, with the entire lane width taken out for a minimum length of ten feet when directed in writing. In the manual asphalt patching provisions (Section 450) two severity categories are defined by measured depth: depressions that are three inches deep or less are classified as Type A patches, which are repaired using only an asphalt wearing course; depressions exceeding three inches fall into the Type B category and require a binder‑course lift not to exceed three inches uncompacted before applying a wearing‑course layer, and each patch must cover at least one square foot. The roller‑compacted concrete pavement standards (Section 502) consider any depression that exceeds one quarter of an inch to be defective; such depressions must be corrected by grinding the high points until a 12‑foot straightedge or crown surface template shows no observable departure, with the inspection performed in stages of no more than five feet. The concrete pavement patching guidelines (Section 516) set a single tolerance limit whereby any depression greater than three sixteenths of an inch must be ground out at no additional cost to the department unless the low spot is caused by an existing adjacent pavement deficiency. Finally, the plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavement specifications for slip‑form paving (Section 501) accept surface variation up to three sixteenths of an inch both parallel and perpendicular to the centerline, but any depression of three sixteenths of an inch or more between adjacent lanes is considered defective. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–313), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–412)

The rating of depression or settlement is governed by a number of rigid‑pavement specifications that each prescribe a surface‑tolerance or defective‑work procedure. In the Rigid Pavements specifications, Section 502.3(k) Surface Tolerance states that depressions that exceed the 1/4‑inch limit are classified as defective and must be corrected by grinding or other surface treatment, while any variation within that tolerance is acceptable. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement construction specification uses the defect‑determination procedure in Section 530.3(t); under the ‘Other than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance’ clause a lot is classified as defective when depressions (or high points) identified per Section 506.3(o) cannot be satisfactorily corrected by the Representative, indicating an unacceptable severity. The Rigid Pavement Standard provides a similar rating in Section 506.3(t) Defective Work; the ‘Other Than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance’ clause likewise classifies a lot as defective when depressions (or high points) referenced in Section 506.3(o) cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative, indicating a severe or defective severity. For Next Generation Concrete Surface work, Section 509.3 Construction requires that depressions or slope misalignments not exceed 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot straightedge, so any depression greater than that limit is treated as unacceptable severity. The concrete pavement patching specification in Section 516.3(n) Surface Tolerance treats any measured depression of three‑sixteenths of an inch or more as exceeding the allowable limit and therefore requiring correction by grinding; if the surface still exceeds the above limits, the area is defective. Finally, the slip‑form paving method in Section 501.3(o) Surface Tolerance defines a depression of three‑sixteenths of an inch or more between adjacent lanes as defective. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–452)

Measurement, survey and extent

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), depression is quantified as the vertical deviation of the slab surface from the desired grade and is measured in the field by positioning a string line about 1 inch above the intended grade and placing gauge blocks on the slab; the depth is read when a block contacts the string line, indicating that the slab has reached the target profile, with hole patterns sized according to the length of the depressed area (section 4.6 SLABJACKING) [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications define depression as the vertical deviation of the pavement surface relative to a straightedge or reference plane, and each construction section prescribes its own tolerance and measurement procedure [2]. For diamond grinding (Section 514), a cross‑slope test is performed at 100‑foot intervals and any area where the deviation exceeds 1/4 inch over a 12‑ft length must be corrected; the maximum depth of the depression or misalignment is recorded for each test location [2]. In micro‑surfacing (Section 483), depression is measured by placing a calibrated straightedge—10 feet long for general longitudinal streaks or 12 feet long when checking longitudinal joints—and reading the amount the surface falls below the straightedge, with an allowable ridge, bump or depression limited to no greater than 1/4 inch [2]. For plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301), after the concrete has hardened the surface is retested with a straightedge as specified in Section 501.3(k)3; any low spot whose depth exceeds 3/8 inch must be removed and replaced, while high points exceeding 1/4 inch are identified for correction [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete (Section 502), the finished surface is evaluated by moving a 12‑foot straightedge (or crown surface template) in contact with the pavement parallel to the centreline, advancing no more than five feet at a time and checking the full width; any localized low spot that causes the surface to vary by more than 1/4 inch relative to the straightedge is deemed a defect and must be corrected [2]. For slabjacking (Section 681), depression is quantified by surveying the roadway elevation with standard surveying equipment to produce existing roadway profiles; the measured low spots are compared with design and post‑jacking grades, and the lift is controlled so that the pavement is raised to within 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) of a string line grade [2]. Section 509 requires measurement of vertical deviation over a twelve‑foot length using a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centreline, and the measured gap must not exceed 1/8 inch in 12 feet [2]. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516), depression is quantified as the depth of a low spot relative to an assumed smooth plane; the contractor tests the patch surface longitudinally and across joints with a 12‑foot straightedge, and any high points or depressions of 3/16 inch or more are corrected by grinding after observing gaps between the straightedge and the pavement [2]. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), depression is measured as the vertical depth of a low spot using a 12‑foot straightedge swung across successive positions; measurements are taken both while the concrete is still plastic for edge slumping within a 6‑inch strip at the pavement edge and after hardening for surface depressions, with any low area of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes considered defective [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–412), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 611–612)

Under the diamond‑grinding contract described in Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement (subsection 514.3), depression and settlement are surveyed by performing cross‑slope tests; the uniformity of cross slope is checked at 100‑foot intervals longitudinally and each test point is documented with the maximum depth observed. The survey unit is each recorded cross‑slope test point, and the extent of the distress is expressed as that maximum depth rather than as a percent, count, or length. In the concrete‑pavement patching provisions of Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching (subsection 516.3), depression in a patch is surveyed manually with a 12‑foot straightedge in both the longitudinal direction and across joints as part of the final surface‑tolerance check before opening to traffic, which constitutes a one‑time construction‑phase survey for every patched segment. The unit of measurement is the individual patch area (segment), and any depression that exceeds 3/16 inch is recorded; thus extent is expressed by counting each occurrence of a depression greater than 3/16 inch. For slip‑form paving covered in Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements (subsection 501.3(g)), depression or settlement is surveyed manually with a 12‑foot straightedge swung from handles, performed during surface‑tolerance checks after the concrete has hardened and again after any corrective work; each slab or joint is inspected in turn rather than on a set calendar schedule. The survey unit is essentially the individual pavement slab (or joint) measured across adjacent lanes, and extent is expressed by noting any low area that exceeds 3/16 inch between adjacent lanes, which triggers a defective‑work determination. [2]

Effects on performance

In slab pavements, settlement creates small voids beneath the concrete slab, resulting in loss of support that leads to excessive slab deflections and stresses; these manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup, which degrades structural capacity, ride quality and safety while shortening durability and service life. Slab stabilization can restore full support when the voids are filled sufficiently, reducing deflections, but it does not increase the pavement’s design structural capacity nor eliminate surface depressions, so the underlying deterioration potential remains unless the voids are adequately treated. At the pavement edge, depression or settlement creates differential heaving and horizontal separation that raise stresses on the outside edge, thereby reducing structural capacity and ride quality and accelerating deterioration; tied concrete shoulders mitigate these effects by lowering edge stresses, limiting settlement and differential movement, and adding stiffness that reduces deflection magnitude, which improves safety relative to other shoulder types, decreases maintenance requirements and extends the service life of the rigid pavement. In concrete pavement work, depressions that exceed three‑sixteenths inch are treated as unacceptable surface tolerances. Under Section 516.3 of the Concrete Pavement Patching specifications, any depression in a patch greater than 3/16 inch must be ground out or otherwise corrected; if correction cannot bring the surface within this limit the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department, a requirement intended to prevent degradation of ride quality and serviceability of the repaired portion. Similarly, Section 501.3(o) for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements specifies that the hardened surface may not vary more than three‑sixteenths inch when measured with a straightedge both parallel and perpendicular to the centerline; depressions of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes are classified as defective under Section 501.3(t) and must be repaired or replaced, reflecting their adverse impact on ride quality, safety, and overall serviceability of the pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–412)

In Portland cement concrete pavement (PCCP) slabs, settlement and pumping can create voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks and outer corners; this loss of support produces excessive deflection and stress that first manifest as transverse joint faulting, fines or small depressions (blow‑holes) at those locations. If those initial depressions are not corrected, the distress progresses to corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately the complete breakup of the slab. The specification for polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing requires that any longitudinal streak, joint or rut contain no bump, ridge or depression greater than a ¼‑inch as measured with the straightedge lengths prescribed (10 ft for streaks, 12 ft for joints and ruts); when this limit is exceeded the work is deemed defective and surface correction must be performed before traffic can be opened. Likewise, the cement concrete pavement specification states that a localized low spot of three‑sixteenths inch or more between adjacent lanes causes the pavement to be considered defective, which triggers the remedial provisions that require removal and replacement of the defective slab at no additional cost, with a minimum removal length of ten feet for reinforced cement concrete pavements and new transverse joints installed in accordance with the standard drawings. [1][2]

Repairs and treatments

Slab stabilization treats depression‑type blow‑holes and slab settlement by filling voids at the slab/subbase interface with either cement‑based fluid grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam, and it is to be used when loss of slab support is indicated by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, or observable slab settlement [1]. Slabjacking restores structural integrity and rideability by filling voids beneath the pavement and raising slabs to an acceptable profile, thereby addressing depressions or settlement such as embankment settlement; it is more likely to succeed when correcting an extensive profile deficiency if the slab is in compression, but it is usually impractical when the temperature is high [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofits are typically employed together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support [1]. In pre‑overlay surface preparation a scratch course may be placed to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions, thereby addressing depression/settlement where indicated; a leveling course of minimum 1‑inch thickness provides a uniform working platform and is the only means to correct an erratic longitudinal profile or re‑establish the proper cross‑section, thus addressing settlement when profile correction is required [1]. Superpave mixture design permits placement of a separate scratch or leveling course ahead of resurfacing when indicated; a scratch course is used to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions even with the surrounding pavement, and a leveling course provides a relatively uniform working platform for placing binder or wearing courses, thereby correcting settlement‑related unevenness before the new overlay [2]. Plain cement concrete tied shoulders mitigate settlement by reducing differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation between pavement and shoulder, and they are required for all projects that employ Long‑Life Concrete Pavement on urban Interstate/Expressway; full‑depth cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are considered when there is a high probability of incident‑management activity on high‑volume corridors or when future work‑zone traffic control will rely on the shoulder [1]. Diamond grinding of concrete pavement is performed after any required concrete‑pavement rehabilitation and does not remove minor depressions; extra‑depth grinding to eliminate such depressions is prohibited, and any cross‑slope deviation greater than ¼ inch over a 12‑foot length must be corrected before grinding so water can drain, with the prerequisite repairs listed in the Concrete Pavement Rehabilitation clause—concrete pavement patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization and slab jacking—required to be completed prior to diamond grinding, thereby addressing settlement or low spots that could cause depressions [2]. Micro‑surfacing uses its spreading and finishing step to fill ruts, cracks and voids and to restore the designed cross‑section profile; it applies whenever the mixture is being spread, especially when ruts are present, and if after placement the finished surface shows longitudinal streaks, ridges, bumps or depressions greater than ¼ inch measured with a straightedge, the specification calls for surface correction of those depressions as defective work [2]. The plain cement concrete base‑course tolerance requires that any depression in the hardened base exceeding ⅜ inch be removed and replaced, but only when a written directive is issued; the replacement must cover the full lane width for at least 10 feet [2]. Manual asphalt patching Type A is specified for potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth, using an asphalt wearing‑course patch, while Manual asphalt patching Type B is specified for potholes and abrupt depressions over three inches in depth, requiring both a binder course and a wearing‑course layer; the treatment applies whenever the surface exhibits these localized low spots [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavement any depression that causes the surface to vary by more than ¼ inch is classified as defective, and the required repair is to grind the high points with a self‑propelled diamond grinder until a 12‑foot straightedge test shows no observable departures and the slab meets the required grade and cross‑section [2]. When depressions or high points cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the Representative may deem the condition a defect in Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, and the required repair is to remove and replace at least an entire panel of LLCP pavement as shown on the Standard Drawings [2]. Next‑generation concrete surface diamond grinding and grooving is applied when a depression or slope misalignment exceeds ⅛ inch in a 12‑foot span, and such depressions must be eliminated by regrounding, although depressed areas caused by subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes may be excluded from this requirement if approved [2]. Concrete pavement patching grinding requires grinding to correct any depression or high point that is 3⁄16 inch or more as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge; the treatment is invoked when surface tolerance testing reveals such low spots, and subbase irregularities exceeding 1 inch in depth must be corrected by loosening, removal or addition of material whenever sub‑grade disturbances create depressions larger than that limit [2]. During slip‑form placement immediate correction of low spots is required while the concrete is still plastic using straightedge testing; after hardening any depression of three‑sixteenths inch or more between adjacent lanes renders the slab defective, and the prescribed remedy is removal and replacement of the defective pavement under the Defective Work provisions that reference surface‑tolerance criteria in Section 501(o) [2]. If depressions fall under the ‘Other Than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance’ criteria and cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the lot is classified as defective and the required repair is to remove and replace at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints on reinforced cement concrete or an entire panel of plain cement concrete pavement [2]. Slabjacking uses cement grout stabilization and high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization to correct depressions or settlement; a profile showing existing depressions and the proposed grade line must be prepared, continuously reinforced concrete pavement is raised to within 0.02 ft of the string line (jointed slabs to within 0.03 ft), and any overjacking greater than 0.10 ft requires removal and replacement as directed in Section 516 [2].

Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual prescribes a pre‑overlay scratch course to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions, thereby eliminating surface low spots that could collect water; this measure applies only where indicated (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. The same manual requires a minimum 1‑inch leveling course when a uniform working platform or correction of an erratic longitudinal profile is needed, re‑establishing the proper cross‑section and removing depressions in the pavement surface; this requirement applies only when such profile correction or platform uniformity is present (section 5.12) [1]. The manual also identifies plain cement‑concrete shoulders tied to the adjacent concrete pavement as a design measure that reduces differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation at the pavement edge; these tied shoulders must be used on all projects employing Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) and are mandatory for urban Interstates/Expressways, while they may be employed for rural Interstates/Expressways when the shoulder is not expected to support work‑zone traffic or when two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 (section 8.15) [1]. When a high probability exists of incident‑management activity on a high‑volume corridor, the manual requires full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints; this also falls under the tied‑shoulder concept and therefore contributes to settlement mitigation (section 8.15) [1]. Diamond grinding performed in accordance with Section 514.3(d)(1) must restore the original pavement cross‑slope and maintain a uniform cross‑slope, correcting any area where the cross‑slope deviation exceeds ¼ inch in a 12‑foot length and providing a positive cross‑slope so that water can drain; these actions are intended to prevent or reduce localized depressions or settlement during the grinding treatment (section 514.3) [2]. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, per Section 483.3(l), requires that the finished surface contain no longitudinal streak with a ridge, bump or depression greater than ¼ inch measured over ten feet, that joint seams be neat with no more than a ¼ inch bump/ridge/depression measured over twelve feet, and that rut fills show no depressions when checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge; compliance is verified during the final surface inspection before traffic opening and serves to prevent or reduce depression (section 483.3) [2]. When depressions or high points identified in Section 506.3(o) cannot be satisfactorily corrected, Section 530.3(t) directs removal and replacement of at least an entire panel of long‑life concrete pavement as shown on the Standard Drawings; the Representative’s determination that the depressions are unrepairable triggers this measure, which eliminates the defective surface area to prevent or reduce depression/settlement (section 530.3) [2]. Section 413.3(g) permits placement of a separate scratch course ahead of resurfacing to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions so they are even with the surrounding pavement, and allows a leveling course where indicated to provide a relatively uniform working platform for binder or wearing courses; both measures apply only when the project specifications call for them and serve to reduce depression‑type irregularities (section 413.3) [2]. Slabjacking may be performed with cement grout (Section 681.3(b)) or high‑density polyurethane grout (Section 681.3(c)) whenever the field QC plan and roadway profile report identify existing depressions and a proposed grade line to be achieved; the work raises continuously reinforced concrete pavement to within ±0.02 ft of the string‑line grade or jointed slabs to within ±0.03 ft, thereby correcting low spots, while overjacking greater than 0.10 ft requires removal and replacement per Section 516 (section 681.3) [2]. Section 466.3(e) calls for repair of potholes and other pavement distresses as specified in Section 450 before installing the asphalt paving interlayer system, and also mandates that the interlayer be placed on a drainable surface with no depressions; completion of these steps prior to interlayer installation prevents new localized lows from forming (section 466.3) [2]. During concrete pavement patching, Section 516.3(c) requires correction of any subbase surface irregularities exceeding one inch by removing or adding material and compacting to proper grade and slope, and Section 516.3(n) mandates testing the finished patch with a twelve‑foot straightedge and grinding any high points or depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more; these actions are intended to prevent settlement that could create low spots (section 516.3) [2]. The slip‑form paving method, per Section 501.3(g), limits edge slumping to no more than 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and no more than ¼ inch between a lane (or ramp) and its shoulder, requiring cessation of paving after any 200‑foot stretch where these limits cannot be maintained; continuous straightedge testing while the concrete is still plastic with immediate correction of low areas (Section 501.3(k)) and the post‑hardening tolerance that deems depressions of 3/16 inch or greater between adjacent lanes defective (Section 501.3(o)) together serve to prevent depression/settlement defects (section 501.3) [2].

In the Pavement Policy Manual, slab‑stabilization work—filling voids at the slab/subbase interface—is required to prevent loss of support that appears as small depressions, blow holes, and overall slab settlement; it shall be applied whenever voids are suspected and, if no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints must be stabilized in accordance with Publication 72M RC‑26M and Publication 408 Section 679 [1]. The same manual requires joint and crack sealing to be performed together with slab stabilization so that water is kept from entering the pavement, thereby reducing pumping that leads to settlement, and subsurface drains are to be maintained in good condition for the same purpose [1]. Slabjacking is prescribed as a construction practice to prevent or correct depressions caused by voids beneath concrete pavement and extensive profile deficiencies such as embankment settlement; it should be applied when the slab can be placed in compression (i.e., not during high temperatures that cause joint compression) [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofits, referenced from Publication 72M and Specification 527, are required together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support [1]. For overlay construction, a scratch course is prescribed where indicated to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions, thereby eliminating low spots that could collect water [1]. A leveling course—minimum 1‑inch thick—may be placed when an erratic longitudinal profile exists because it is the only layer allowed to correct the profile or re‑establish the proper cross‑section, which also removes depressional areas; both measures are applied before the binder or wearing course is laid [1]. Tied concrete shoulders reduce differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation; plain cement‑concrete shoulders are required for all projects using Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) as described in Section 4.12 [1]. All urban Interstate/Expressway projects must use tied concrete shoulders implemented as full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, thereby preventing settlement under the condition of an urban Interstate/Expressway [1]. For rural Interstates/Expressways where no anticipated work‑zone traffic support is needed, tied shoulders with undowelled transverse shoulder joints using JPCP are prescribed, again preventing settlement for those low‑traffic corridors; when a high‑volume rural corridor does need work‑zone support, full‑depth shoulders with dowelled joints are mandated, providing the same settlement mitigation [1]. When diamond grinding of concrete pavement is performed under Section 514.3(d), the contractor must restore the original cross‑slope and correct any surface deviation greater than 1/4 inch in a 12‑foot length, thereby providing a positive cross‑slope that allows water to drain and prevents low spots that could become depressions; this transverse‑tolerance requirement applies during the grinding construction phase [2]. Section 514.3(e) further requires that all concrete pavement repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization, and slab jacking—be completed before any grinding begins, ensuring that underlying settlement or damage does not produce depressions after the work [2]. Under the Defective Work criteria of Section 483.3(l), longitudinal seams in polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing must be finished so that any bump, ridge or depression does not exceed 1/4 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, and all ruts must be filled to eliminate depressions as measured with the same straightedge; meeting these limits prevents low spots that could collect water [2]. Section 301.3(o) mandates a post‑hardening surface retest of plain cement concrete base courses using straightedges; high points that exceed 1/4 inch are to be removed by grinding or cutting tools as directed, and sections containing depressions of more than 3/8 inch are to be removed and replaced when directed in writing, with removal extending across the full lane width for a length at least 10 feet [2]. For potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth (Type A), Section 450.3 requires the work to be performed manually with an asphalt wearing course that is compacted until its surface matches the surrounding grade, directly addressing shallow depressions by restoring a smooth, water‑shedding surface [2]. For depressions deeper than three inches (Type B), Section 450.3 specifies that a binder‑course lift not to exceed three inches uncompacted be placed before the top 1½‑inch wearing course, each lift being leveled and compacted, again ensuring the final patch conforms to the surrounding grade [2]. Section 450.1 states that preparing and patching of potholes, abrupt depressions, and deteriorated edges uses asphalt plant‑mix material placed manually or with mechanical spreading and finishing equipment, directly addressing depression‑type distresses whenever those low‑area defects are identified [2]. When roller‑compacted concrete pavements are constructed, Section 502.3(k) requires testing the finished RCC surface with a 12‑foot straightedge or crown template in areas the Representative deems may be deficient or irregular; when the pavement surface varies by more than ¼ inch, high points must be removed by grinding, and the Representative will consider depressions in the pavement surface of more than ¼ inch defective [2]. The Next Generation Concrete Surface program, Section 509.3, requires the contractor to maintain the existing roadway cross‑slope and verify that any localized low spot does not exceed a differential of 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot straightedge measured perpendicular to the centerline; this tolerance is intended to prevent depression or settlement forming during grinding and grooving, and it applies only while executing the NGCS texture work [2]. Section 509.3 also mandates a feather pass of at least 2 feet into adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes, extending a minimum of 100 feet and performed with conventional diamond grinding, to provide proper drainage and thereby reduce the likelihood of water‑filled depressions during the NGCS texture work [2]. The Asphalt Paving Interlayer System, Section 466.3(e), requires the contractor to dry and clean the existing pavement, seal cracks, repair potholes and other distresses, and then place the interlayer on a drainable surface with no depressions; this practice is intended to eliminate low spots that could collect water and thus prevent depression or settlement of the overlay [2]. For concrete pavement patching, Section 516.3(c) specifies that any subbase surface irregularities deeper than 1 inch must be corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material before concrete placement, a practice listed to avoid low spots that could cause settlement of the patch [2]. Section 516.3(n) mandates testing the finished patch with a 12‑foot straightedge and grinding away any high points or depressions of 3/16 inch or more, ensuring the surface does not retain depressions that would collect water or lead to further deterioration [2]. The slip‑form paving method, Section 501.3(g), requires edge slumping to be checked with a 12‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses its plasticity and must not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes (or ¼ inch between lane and shoulder); if this limit cannot be kept within any 200‑ft stretch, paving is to be suspended, thereby preventing surface depressions that could lead to settlement [2]. While the concrete remains plastic, Section 501.3(k) requires immediate correction of low areas by striking off, consolidating and refinishing after testing the surface with a 12‑foot straightedge, thereby avoiding surface depressions [2]. Sections 501.3(p) and (o) declare pavement defective when depressions between adjacent lanes are 3/16 inch or more, ensuring settlement‑related lows are identified and repaired [2]. Section 681.3 mandates a field quality‑control plan that documents existing roadway profiles showing current depressions and the intended grade line before slab‑jacking begins; subsequent injection may be performed to raise continuously reinforced concrete pavement to within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade, or jointed slabs and bridge‑end slabs to within 0.03 foot, using continuous pressures up to 200 psi (short‑period pressures up to 300 psi, brief rises to 600 psi when bonded to subgrade); if overjacking exceeds 0.10 foot the pavement must be removed and replaced as directed in Section 516 [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

Under Section 514.3(d)(1) of the Rigid Pavements specifications for diamond grinding of concrete pavement, any cross‑slope deviation that includes a localized depression greater than ¼ inch in a 12‑foot length must be corrected; the condition is judged by performing cross‑slope tests at 100‑foot intervals and recording the maximum depth of depression or slope misalignment at each test location. Section 483, which governs polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving systems, specifies that longitudinal streaks may not contain a ridge, bump or depression greater than ¼ inch when measured with a 10‑foot straightedge, and that ruts must be filled so that no depressions are present when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge; these limits form part of the finished surface and transverse profile requirements in subsection (l). In the Plain Cement Concrete Base Course specifications, Section 301.3(o) requires that after the concrete has hardened any surface depression exceeding 3/8 inch be removed and the affected section replaced, with removal extending across the full lane width for at least 10 feet; the evaluation is made by retesting the surface with straightedges as prescribed in Section 501.3(k)3. The Flexible Pavement specifications in Section 450.3 classify depressions that appear as potholes or abrupt depressions by vertical depth, treating defects three inches and less with Manual Asphalt Patching Type A and defects over three inches with Manual Asphalt Patching Type B; the measurement is the vertical depth of the defect in inches. For roller‑compacted concrete slabs, Section 502.3(k) sets an acceptance limit that any depression greater than ¼ inch, as determined by testing the finished surface with a 12‑foot straightedge (or crown surface template), renders the slab defective and requires correction. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specification, Section 530.3(t), states that a lot is deemed defective for depression or settlement when it contains depressions or high points that fall under the tolerance criteria of Section 506.3(o) and cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative; no numeric depth tolerance is provided in this excerpt. Section 506.3(o) (Other Than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance) further clarifies that a lot is defective if depressions or high points specified therein cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative. Under the Incidental Construction contract, Section 681.3 requires surveyed roadway profiles to document existing depressions and the proposed grade line before work and a profile report after slabjacking; the corrected pavement must be raised to within 0.02 foot plus or minus from a string‑line grade for continuously reinforced concrete slabs, or within 0.03 foot plus or minus for jointed slabs, and any overjacking exceeding 0.10 foot triggers removal and replacement of the pavement at no additional cost. The Next Generation Concrete Surface specification, Section 509.3, limits depressions (settlement) to no greater than 1/8 inch in a 12‑foot length when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline; any depression exceeding that limit is non‑compliant and must be reground, although depressed areas caused by subsidence, edge slump or other localized causes may be excluded if approved. In Concrete Pavement Patching, Section 516.3(n) requires that any depression or high point in a patched area that measures 3/16 inch or more with a 12‑foot straightedge in the longitudinal direction across joints be corrected by grinding, and if after grinding the surface still exceeds this limit the area is considered defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional charge. The Slip‑Form Paving Method provision of Section 501.3(g) (referencing the post‑hardening straightedge test described in 501.3(o)) limits depression (settlement) to less than 3/16 inch as measured by that straightedge test; any depression of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes is deemed defective.
