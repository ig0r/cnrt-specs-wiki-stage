Depression / Settlement

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Depression / Settlement is a topic under Distress.
depressiondepressionssettlementsettlements
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Depression or settlement in concrete pavements is a localized loss of support that creates a low spot on the surface and may also appear as overall slab sagging or excessive deflection, often manifested as small blow‑hole depressions adjacent to transverse joints, cracks, or at outside slab corners [1]. It occurs on all rigid pavement types—including jointed plain slabs, reinforced slabs, long‑life concrete, roller‑compacted concrete and the tied shoulder interface—and is generated primarily by pumping action, subgrade consolidation and water‑induced void formation beneath the slab, especially where joints or cracks permit moisture ingress [1] [2]. The loss of support leads to excessive slab deflection, joint faulting, corner breaks and eventual slab breakup, degrading structural capacity, ride quality and safety while shortening service life [1].

Description and identification

Depression or settlement in concrete pavements is defined as a localized loss of support that creates a low spot on the surface and may also be observed as overall slab sagging or excessive deflection.[1] The condition can arise when pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or other causes create voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and at outside slab corners.[1] The loss of support produces small depressions—often called blow‑holes—in the shoulder adjacent to a joint or crack, and may be observed as an extensive profile deficiency in which the slab sits lower than surrounding sections.[1] On the pavement surface the depression appears as a low spot or depressed area that serves as the starting point for slabjacking pumping, while settlement may manifest as overall slab settlement where the slab sags.[1] In the plain cement concrete base course, a depression is a localized low spot on the hardened surface that, when measured with a straightedge, exceeds a depth of more than 3/8 inch; such depressions are unacceptable and must be removed by cutting out the full width of lane where they occur for a length of at least 10 feet, and high points that rise above the surface tolerance of 1/4 inch are similarly corrected by grinding or cutting tools.[2] In flexible pavement under manual asphalt patching, depression or settlement appears as localized low spots such as potholes or abrupt depressions that create a sunken area on the surface; when the depth does not exceed 3 inches it falls under the Type A manual asphalt patching procedure, requiring each patch to be at least 1 square foot, cut out with vertical edges, cleaned, dried, tack‑coated and filled with wearing‑course asphalt so that after compaction the repaired surface matches the surrounding grade, and potholes which extend into the shoulder do not require excavation beyond the edge of roadway.[2] In Superpave mixture design, depression or settlement is a localized low area that appears as wheel ruts or other small depressions on the surface, creating an uneven riding plane; these are treated by placing a separate scratch or leveling course ahead of resurfacing to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions so they are even with the surrounding pavement.[2] In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, depression/settlement refers to a surface defect where localized low spots exceed the tolerances set out in Section 506.3(o); when such depressions cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the lot is classified as defective under the “Other Than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance” provision of Section 506.3(t)(4).[2] In next‑generation concrete surface, depression or settlement is a localized low spot that creates a dip in the cross‑slope and is identified when a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline shows more than 1/8 inch of vertical deviation over the 12‑foot length, indicating subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes (exemptions may apply if approved).[2] In roller‑compacted concrete pavement, depression or settlement is a localized low spot on the finished surface that exceeds the allowable tolerance; it appears as a dip of more than 1/4 inch when the slab is checked with a straightedge, and such variations are deemed defective by the Representative during the Surface Tolerance inspection.[2] In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements using the slip‑form method, depression or settlement shows up as a dip when the surface is checked with a straightedge; depressions of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes are considered defective, and edge slumping is limited to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders.[2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 263–305), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–390)

Depression or settlement is recognized by observing small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at transverse joints or cracks, which signals loss of slab support; this visual cue distinguishes the condition from transverse joint faulting, fines that accumulate near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, and corner breaks, each presenting a different symptom despite all being linked to voids beneath the concrete [1]. In the diamond‑grinding contract (Section 514.3 Construction), depressions are recognized during transverse tolerance surveys when the measured cross‑slope deviates more than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length; they are recorded as existing pavement depressions that remain uncorrected under the current contract and are treated separately from spalls or joint cracks, which are addressed by equipment restrictions [2]. In the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system (Section 483.3 Construction), a depression or settlement is identified when a straightedge measurement shows any longitudinal ridge, bump or depression greater than 1/4 inch over a 10‑foot straightedge; these low spots are distinguished from excess emulsified asphalt, washboard ripple patterns, and tear or drag marks [2]. For plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301.3 Construction), depressions are identified after the concrete has hardened by retesting with straightedges, and any low spot that exceeds three‑eighths of an inch is classified as a depression; high points exceeding one‑quarter of an inch are treated separately, and when a depression greater than three‑eighths of an inch is found the specification calls for removal and replacement of the full lane width over at least ten feet [2]. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 506.3 Construction), depressions are identified during visual inspection when surface irregularities exceed the tolerances set out in Section 506.3(o); they are deemed defective only if a Representative determines that these irregularities cannot be satisfactorily corrected, thereby distinguishing them from rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction, cracking, honeycombing, or edge slumping [2]. Under the next generation concrete surface (Section 509.3 Construction), depressions are identified by placing a 12‑foot straightedge perpendicular to the centreline and measuring vertical deviation; any depression or slope misalignment that exceeds 1/8 inch over the 12‑ft length is not permitted, although low areas caused by subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes may be excluded when approved [2]. In long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530.3 Construction), depressions are identified under the “Other Than Mainline Pavement Surface Tolerance” clause; a pavement is deemed defective when it contains depressions that meet the criteria of Section 506.3(o) and cannot be satisfactorily corrected by the Representative, distinguishing them from rain damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, finish problems, cracking or honeycombing [2]. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3 Construction), depressions in a patch are identified by measuring the finished surface with a 12‑foot straightedge placed longitudinally across the patch and its joints; any low spot that registers a depression of 3/16 inch or more is considered out of tolerance and must be corrected by grinding unless the irregularity originates from a surface deficiency in the existing adjacent pavement, and if the depression still exceeds the 3/16‑inch limit the area is deemed defective [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502.3 Construction), depression or settlement is identified during the surface‑tolerance inspection when a 12‑foot straightedge (or crown template) placed across the pavement shows a variation of more than 1/4 inch; such depressions are treated as defects and corrected by grinding high points with a self‑propelled diamond grinder until the surface meets the 1/4‑inch tolerance, thereby distinguishing them from other distresses not defined by straightedge deviation [2]. In slip‑form concrete pavements (Section 501.3 Construction), depression or settlement is identified during post‑placement straightedge testing by locating low spots that exceed the surface‑tolerance limit of 3/16 inch between adjacent lanes; this condition renders the pavement defective, and it is distinguished from edge slumping, which is evaluated only within the 6‑inch zone next to the pavement edge and is considered acceptable up to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and ramps and 1/4 inch between lane‑shoulder interfaces [2].

Where it occurs

Depression and settlement are reported on Portland cement concrete pavements, including jointed plain concrete slabs, reinforced concrete slabs, long‑life concrete, roller‑compacted concrete, and on the interface where a plain‑cement concrete shoulder is tied to such pavement (section 4.5, 4.6, 8.15) [1]. The Pennsylvania specifications likewise identify depression as a surface distress on all rigid concrete pavements, covering plain cement‑concrete base courses, plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements, long‑life concrete, roller‑compacted concrete, and next‑generation concrete surfaces (various sections) [2]. For plain cement‑concrete base courses, any depression that exceeds 3/8 inch requires removal of the full lane width for a minimum length of 10 feet (section 301.3) [2]. In reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements and their adjoining shoulders, depressions of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes are deemed defective (section 501.3(g)) [2]. The diamond‑grinding specification for concrete surfaces treats existing pavement depressions as an excluded item from lot calculations, indicating that such low spots are addressed only on concrete surfaces (section 514.3) [2]. For concrete pavement patches applied to rigid pavements, any surface depression greater than 3/16 inch must be corrected by grinding to meet the specified tolerance (section 516.3(n)) [2]. On roller‑compacted concrete rigid pavements, a surface depression exceeding 1/4 inch shall be treated as a defect and corrected by grinding under the Surface Tolerance provisions (section 502.3(a) and 502.3(k)) [2]. The next‑generation concrete surface specification prohibits depressions or slope misalignment greater than 1/8 inch measured over a 12‑foot straightedge on the concrete surface or adjacent shoulders (section 509.3) [2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications also consider depressions a defect and deem the lot defective when they cannot be satisfactorily corrected (section 530.3) [2]. Slabjacking operations for continuous reinforced concrete pavement require raising the slab to within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade, with jointed slabs including bridge‑end slabs raised to within 0.03 foot; overjacking greater than 0.10 foot mandates removal and replacement (section 681.3) [1]. On flexible asphalt surfaces that receive polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, transverse profile depressions must be absent when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, and longitudinal joints or edges may not exhibit bumps, ridges or depressions greater than 1/4 inch (section 483.3) [2]. Manual asphalt patching procedures address potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth on flexible pavement, including cases where the defect extends into a shoulder; when the shoulder has an asphalt base, the shoulder portion of the pothole is filled with asphalt (section 450.3) [2].

In Portland‑cement concrete pavements, voids that develop beneath the slab produce depressions most often adjacent to transverse joints and cracks and especially at the outside corners of the slab; small blow‑hole type depressions may also appear in the shoulder directly over a transverse joint or crack, indicating loss of support at the slab/subbase interface, and the distress is further noted at the outer edge where the main‑line pavement meets its concrete shoulder. Within polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, depressions are addressed at longitudinal joints where the two passes meet and in the transverse profile of ruts; any bump, ridge or depression at a joint must not exceed 1/4 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, and ruts must be filled so that no depressions are present as measured with a 12‑foot straightedge. In plain cement concrete base courses, depression or settlement is treated as a surface condition of the hardened base, and when a depression greater than 3/8 inch is identified the specification requires removal and replacement of that section across the full lane width for at least 10 feet. For next‑generation concrete surfaces, depressed pavement areas may result from subsidence in the general slab interior or edge slump at the pavement edges; such localized depressions can be excluded from the tolerance requirement if approved. Roller compacted concrete pavements identify depressions as surface defects, and when the pavement surface varies by more than 1/4 inch low spots are considered defective and must be corrected, indicating that settlement occurs on the slab surface rather than being confined to joints, cracks, corners, edges, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements constructed by slip‑form method, depression is observed at pavement edges where edge‑slump must not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes (or ramps) and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, and also in the interior between adjacent lanes where any low spot of 3/16 inch or more is deemed defective. [1][2]

Causes and mechanisms

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) in its slab‑stabilization section, depression or settlement in concrete slabs is caused primarily by pumping action, subgrade consolidation, and resulting settlement that create voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and outside corners, with water infiltration contributing to the pumping phenomenon and thus making moisture and drainage a key driver of these depressions. The same source describes the loss of support as appearing as small depressions (blow holes) at joints or cracks, transverse‑joint faulting, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2020, Section 516.3(n) Surface Tolerance requires that any depression greater than 3⁄16 inch be ground away, but it exempts correction when the depression is caused by a surface deficiency in existing adjacent pavement, thereby attributing such depressions to design or construction‑related surface deficiencies rather than to traffic loading, climate, moisture, or material issues. The specification excerpt does not contain a table classifying distresses by cause. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–402)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), section 4.5 Slab Stabilization explains that depression or settlement in Portland‑cement concrete pavements develops when pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement may create small voids beneath the slab, with most of the voids developing near transverse joints and cracks, particularly at outside slab corners [1]. The loss of support then causes excessive slab deflection that appears as small depressions (blow holes), joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup [1]. Conditions that accelerate this mechanism include water entering the pavement—particularly through unsealed joints or cracks—and inadequate subsurface drainage, which increase pumping and promote further void formation [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Section 509.3 Construction, describe depression/settlement as occurring when the slab undergoes subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes that produce low spots on the surface [2].

Severity levels and rating

The diamond‑grinding specifications for concrete pavement (Section 514.3) treat depression or slope misalignment solely by a tolerance that requires correction of any transverse deviation greater than ¼ inch measured over a 12‑foot length; no additional severity rating is provided. In the plain cement concrete base‑course specifications (Section 301.3), depressions exceeding 3/8 inch must be removed and replaced for a full lane width over a minimum length of 10 feet, whereas depressions of 3/8 inch or less do not require replacement; high points that exceed ¼ inch are to be ground or cut as directed. The manual asphalt‑patching specifications (Section 450.3) define two depth‑based categories: depressions three inches and less are classified as Manual Asphalt Patching Type A and are repaired with an asphalt wearing course, while depressions deeper than three inches fall under Type B and require both a binder course and a wearing course. Concrete pavement patching specifications (Section 516.3 (n)) consider any high point or depression of 3/16 inch or more to be a surface‑tolerance issue that must be corrected by grinding, and if the limit remains exceeded after grinding the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502.3 (a)), surface variation up to ¼ inch is acceptable; any depression greater than ¼ inch is considered defective and must be corrected by grinding or other approved means. Finally, the slip‑form paving method for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501.3) accepts the pavement surface only when variation does not exceed 3/16 inch both parallel and perpendicular to the centerline, and any depression of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes is deemed defective. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 304–305), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–402)

The Manual Asphalt Patching procedure described in Section 450—MANUAL ASPHALT PATCHING (Section 450.3) assigns a severity rating based on the measured depth of a depression: depressions that are three inches or less are treated as lower‑severity conditions suitable for Type A patching, while depressions exceeding three inches are classified as higher‑severity and require Type B patching. The defect‑determination procedure in Section 506.3(o) of the Rigid Pavement specifications (Section 500) classifies a lot as defective when it contains depressions that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, which represents a severe condition. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specification adopts this same approach in Section 530.3(t), declaring a lot defective if depressions that fall within the tolerance limits of Section 506.3(o) cannot be satisfactorily corrected; this is interpreted as a severe depression or settlement condition. The surface‑tolerance procedure in Section 502.3(k) of the Rigid Pavements specification evaluates pavement with a straightedge and deems any low spot that causes the surface to vary by more than 1/4 inch defective, indicating a severe condition. Finally, the Surface Tolerance criteria in Section 501.3(o) of the Rigid Pavements specification treat depressions of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes as defective, which likewise reflects a severe depression condition. [2]

Measurement, survey and extent

Depression or settlement is measured as the vertical deviation of the pavement surface from a reference grade [1][2]. In the Pavement Policy Manual (section 4.6 SLABJACKING) the field procedure positions a string line approximately 1 inch above the desired grade and places gauge blocks on the slab; the depth of the depression is read when a block contacts the string line, indicating that the slab has reached the intended profile [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications quantify depression by measuring vertical deviation over defined lengths with straightedges, templates, or surveyed profiles, each section prescribing its own tolerance. For example, Section 514 requires a cross‑slope test at 100‑foot intervals and records any deviation greater than ¼ inch within a 12‑ft length, noting the deepest measured depth [2]. Section 483 calls for measurement of ridges, bumps or depressions with a 10‑foot straightedge for longitudinal streaks and a 12‑foot straightedge for transverse profile depressions, and the allowable limit shall not exceed ¼ inch [2]. Section 301 (after concrete has hardened) directs depth measurement with a straightedge operated as specified in Section 501.3(k)3; localized low spots exceeding ⅜ inch are identified for removal or replacement, while high points over ¼ inch are to be ground or cut [2]. For slabjacking work, Section 681 quantifies depression as the vertical difference between the existing roadway profile and the proposed grade line obtained from surveyed profiles before and after the lift; the raised pavement is acceptable when it falls within 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) of the string‑line grade [2]. Section 509 evaluates depression after grinding by placing a 12‑foot straightedge perpendicular to the centerline over a 12‑ft span; the dip must not exceed ⅛ inch [2]. Section 516 measures depressions on concrete patches with a 12‑foot straightedge in both longitudinal direction and across joints, and any depression greater than 3/16 inch must be corrected by grinding [2]. Section 502 checks surface deviation from a reference plane with a 12‑foot straightedge or crown surface template moved parallel to the road centerline; variations greater than ¼ inch are considered defective [2]. Finally, Section 501 defines edge slumping as vertical deviation measured with a 12‑foot straightedge swung from handles extending at least three feet beyond half the slab width, placed in contact and advanced in positions no more than five feet apart; depressions of 3/16 inch or greater between adjacent lanes are deemed defective [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 201), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 332–334), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–402), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 597–598)

In Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement, depression or settlement is surveyed by performing cross‑slope tests; measurements are taken at 100‑foot intervals along the longitudinal direction, making each test point a sample unit, and the results are documented for every location with the maximum depth of depression recorded, so the extent is expressed as a measured depth per test point. In Section 301—Plain Cement Concrete Base Course, depression is surveyed manually after the concrete has hardened by retesting the surface with straightedges in accordance with Section 501.3(k)3; any high points exceeding 1/4 inch are removed and sections that contain depressions greater than 3/8 inch are identified for removal and replacement, with the required treatment being the removal of the full lane width over a length of at least 10 feet where such a depression occurs, thus the extent is expressed as a length of at least 10 feet per occurrence. In Section 502—Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements, depression or settlement is surveyed manually by the Representative using a 12‑foot straightedge (or crown surface template) placed on the finished RCC surface and moved in successive positions no more than 5 feet apart across the width; the inspection is performed whenever the Representative determines an area may be deficient rather than on a fixed schedule, each measurement segment corresponds to the straightedge length applied in stages of up to 5 feet, and any depression that exceeds more than 1/4 inch is recorded as defective, so the extent is expressed by the depth threshold greater than 1/4 inch. [2]

Effects on performance

Depression or settlement in slab pavements creates small voids beneath the concrete, resulting in loss of support that leads to excessive slab deflections and stresses; these manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup, which degrades structural capacity, ride quality and safety while shortening durability and service life. [1] Slab stabilization can restore full support when the voids are filled sufficiently, reducing deflections, but it does not increase the pavement’s design structural capacity nor eliminate surface depressions, so the underlying deterioration potential remains unless the voids are adequately treated. [1] At the pavement edge, depression or settlement creates differential heaving and horizontal separation that raise stresses on the outside edge, thereby reducing structural capacity and ride quality and accelerating deterioration. [1] Tied concrete shoulders mitigate these effects by lowering edge stresses, limiting settlement and differential movement, and adding stiffness that reduces deflection magnitude, which improves safety relative to other shoulder types, decreases maintenance requirements and extends the service life of the rigid pavement. [1] The specifications consider depressions in the pavement surface of more than 1/4 inch to be defective, because such low spots impair the smoothness of the riding surface and can jeopardize safety and long‑term performance if left uncorrected. [2] When the pavement surface varies by more than 1/4 inch, high points must be removed by grinding the surface to within the tolerance. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–374)

In Portland cement concrete pavement (PCCP) slabs, settlement and pumping can create voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks and outer corners; this loss of support produces excessive deflection and stress that first manifest as transverse joint faulting, fines or small depressions (blow‑holes) at those locations, then progress to corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately the complete breakup of the slab if left untreated. Because slab stabilization restores support by filling voids but does not correct surface depressions, failure to intervene allows this chain of distresses to continue unchecked. When a depression or settlement is not corrected, the surface fails to meet the uniformity criteria of Section 483.3(l); any longitudinal ridge, bump or depression greater than ¼ inch measured with a 10‑foot straightedge or any rut depression measurable with a 12‑foot straightedge is classified as defective work and must be repaired by surface correction, otherwise the Representative will deem the pavement defective and require corrective treatment. If a long‑life concrete pavement (LLCP) exhibits depressions or low spots that cannot be satisfactorily corrected as defined in Section 506.3(o), Section 530.3(t) declares the lot defective; this defect status triggers the requirement to remove and replace at least an entire panel of the concrete pavement as shown on the Standard Drawings. When a pavement depression that remains after grinding exceeds 3/16 inch, Section 516.3(n) classifies the patch as defective; the defect must be corrected by removing and replacing the area at no additional cost to the Department. During straight‑edge testing, any low spot that is 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes is declared a defect under Section 501.3(p); because such depressions are classified as defective surface tolerance, the work must be treated as defective pavement under Section 501.3(t), which requires removal and replacement of the affected slab with a minimum removal length of ten feet for reinforced cement concrete or six feet for plain cement concrete, and new transverse joints must be constructed in accordance with the standard drawings. [1][2]

Repairs and treatments

Depression‑type blow‑holes and slab settlement are treated by slab stabilization, which fills voids at the slab/subbase interface with cement‑based fluid grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam; this treatment is indicated when loss of slab support is shown by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, or observable slab settlement [1]. Slabjacking restores structural integrity and rideability by filling voids beneath the pavement and raising slabs to an acceptable profile, thereby addressing depressions or settlement such as embankment settlement; it is more likely successful when correcting an extensive profile deficiency if the slab is in compression and is usually impractical when temperature is high because the slabs will have expanded and joints will be in compression [1]. Dowel bar retrofits are typically used together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support [1]. In pre‑overlay surface preparation a scratch course may be placed to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions, addressing depression or settlement where indicated, while a leveling course of minimum one‑inch thickness provides a uniform working platform and is the only means to correct an erratic longitudinal profile or re‑establish the proper cross‑section, thus addressing settlement when profile correction is required [1]. Plain cement concrete tied shoulders mitigate settlement by reducing differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation between pavement and shoulder; they are required for all projects that employ Long‑Life Concrete Pavement on urban Interstate/Expressway, and full‑depth cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are considered when there is a high probability of incident‑management activity on high‑volume corridors or future work‑zone traffic control will rely on the shoulder [1]. Diamond grinding of concrete pavement requires slab stabilization or slabjacking beforehand; this lifts and stabilizes low spots to eliminate depressions or settlement and applies only when diamond grinding is planned and the transverse tolerance provision identifies cross‑slope deviations greater than ¼ inch over a 12‑ft length [2]. The polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing system is used to fill ruts and restore the designed cross‑section profile, thereby removing localized depressions that can retain water; acceptance requires that longitudinal streaks or transverse profile depressions not exceed ¼ inch ridge, bump or depression as measured with a straightedge [2]. Plain cement concrete base‑course mandates removal and replacement of any segment that contains a depression exceeding ⅜ inch when a written directive is issued, with the replacement extending across the full lane width for at least ten feet [2]. Manual asphalt patching prescribes Type A patching for potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth using an asphalt wearing course placed manually, and Type B patching when such depressions exceed three inches in depth, requiring placement of an asphalt binder course in lifts not to exceed three inches uncompacted followed by a wearing course; both methods are performed manually [2]. Superpave mixture‑design specifications call for a scratch course to fill wheel ruts or other local low spots and a leveling course when broader unevenness exists, each placed ahead of resurfacing only where indicated in the project specifications [2]. The rigid‑pavement specification classifies any pavement that exhibits depressions (or high points) that cannot be satisfactorily corrected by the Representative as defective and mandates removal and replacement; this treatment applies only when those depressions are present as defined in Section 506.3(o) and are judged unrepairable [2]. Slab‑jacking treats depression or settlement in concrete pavements by cement‑grout stabilization after a field quality‑control plan documents the existing depressions and a roadway‑profile report confirms the areas to be stabilized; grout is pumped through drilled holes and jacked until the pavement surface is raised within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced slabs or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs, using continuous pressures up to 200 psi (300 psi only briefly and up to 600 psi when the pavement is bonded), and any overjacking greater than 0.10 foot triggers removal and replacement as specified in Section 516 [2]. Next‑generation concrete surface requirements call for regrounding any area where a depression exceeds ⅛ inch over a 12‑ft span measured with a straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline; such non‑compliant depressions must be removed by diamond grinding, although depressed pavement areas caused by subsidence, edge slump, or other localized factors may be excluded if an approval is obtained [2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement requires removal and replacement of at least an entire panel when a lot is found to contain depressions or high points that the Representative judges cannot be satisfactorily corrected; this treatment eliminates the defective surface area and applies when such depressions or high points exist and cannot be corrected [2]. Concrete pavement patching mandates correction, by grinding, of any high point or depression that measures three‑sixteenths inch or more by straightedge testing at no additional cost to the Department, and if after grinding the surface still exceeds the limit the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements specify that when straightedge testing shows a variation greater than one‑quarter inch, high points must be removed by grinding with a self‑propelled diamond grinder until the surface meets tolerance; the Representative will consider depressions of more than one‑quarter inch defective, triggering this grinding repair [2]. The slip‑form paving method permits edge slump not exceeding ⅛ inch between adjacent lanes and ramps and ¼ inch between lanes and shoulders; if straightedge testing after hardening shows edge slumping beyond those limits the contractor must submit correction methods for review and apply the approved method, otherwise the slab must be removed and replaced under Section 501.3(t), and pavement that exhibits depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or greater between adjacent lanes is considered defective and must be removed and replace dunder the same defect‑work provisions [2].

Slab stabilization is applicable when small depressions (blow holes) appear in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, but it does not correct pavement surface depressions. Slabjacking may be applied to correct an extensive profile deficiency such as an embankment settlement when the slab is in compression; however it is usually impractical, if not impossible, to perform slabjacking when the pavement temperature is high because the slabs will have expanded and the joints will be in compression. For slabjacking, hole patterns must be determined in the field considering the size or length of the depressed area, the amount of correction required, subgrade and drainage conditions, and the location of joints and cracks. Diamond grinding of concrete pavement may be applied only where a surface deviation exceeds 1/4 inch measured over a 12‑foot length, and the operation shall not be performed within 2 feet of existing curbs, bridge barriers or roadway barriers. For plain cement concrete base courses, depressions deeper than 3/8 inch must be removed and replaced across the full lane width for a minimum length of 10 feet when directed in writing. Manual Asphalt Patching Type A is prescribed for potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth, whereas depressions exceeding three inches in depth require Manual Asphalt Patching Type B. A scratch course may be placed to fill wheel ruts or other local small depressions that can be brought level with the surrounding pavement; no explicit severity, depth, width or extent thresholds are provided beyond the description of “local small”. When a concrete lot contains depressions that cannot be satisfactorily corrected according to the tolerances in Section 506.3(o), the pavement is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced. Slabjacking with cement grout may be used provided the lift brings the surface within ±0.02 ft of the target string‑line grade for continuously reinforced slabs or within ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs; if the required overjacking exceeds 0.10 ft, removal and replacement is required. For Next Generation Concrete Surface, depressions caused by subsidence, edge slump or other localized causes may be excluded from the true‑to‑grade requirement when approved, effectively ruling out diamond‑grinding or grooving for those areas. Under Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications, a depression that cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative renders the lot defective and requires removal and replacement of at least an entire LLCP panel; no other severity limits are specified. An asphalt paving interlayer system may be placed only on a drainable surface that contains no depressions; consequently any localized low area must first be repaired in accordance with Section 450 before the interlayer can be installed, and placement is ruled out until those repairs are completed. Concrete pavement patching requires that any depression measured at 3/16 inch or more with a 12‑foot straightedge be corrected by grinding; if after grinding the surface still exceeds that limit, the defective area must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, a depression that exceeds 1/4 inch is considered defective and mandates removal of the high points by grinding with a self‑propelled diamond grinder or equivalent cutting tools until the surface meets tolerance. Under the slip‑form paving method for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, depressions of 3/16 inch or more between adjacent lanes are deemed defective and require removal and replacement of the affected concrete as specified. Prevention in design and construction

A pre‑overlay scratch course is prescribed to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions, thereby eliminating surface low spots that could collect water; this measure applies only where indicated [1]. When a uniform working platform or correction of an erratic longitudinal profile is required, a minimum 1‑inch leveling course is used to re‑establish the proper cross‑section and remove depressions in the pavement surface; the requirement applies only when such profile correction or platform uniformity is present [1]. Plain cement‑concrete shoulders that are tied to the adjacent concrete pavement are required to reduce differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation at the pavement edge; they must be used on all projects employing Long‑Life Concrete Pavement and are mandatory for urban Interstates/Expressways, while for rural Interstates/Expressways they may be employed when the shoulder is not expected to support work‑zone traffic or when two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 [1]. Where a high probability exists of incident‑management activity on a high‑volume corridor, full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are required, and because they fall under the tied‑shoulder concept they also contribute to settlement mitigation [1]. The design specifications require restoring the original cross‑slope and maintaining a uniform slope so that water drains away; any deviation greater than ¼ inch over a twelve‑foot segment must be corrected, which eliminates localized low spots that could retain water [2]. In micro‑surfacing work the finished‑surface criteria require ruts to be filled so that no depressions are detectable with a twelve‑foot straightedge and longitudinal ridges, bumps or depressions may not exceed ¼ inch; these limits keep the surface from developing low spots that could become depressions [2]. For plain cement concrete base courses any section containing a depression greater than 3⁄8 inch must be removed and replaced when directed in writing, with the full lane width removed for at least ten feet, thereby eliminating depressions of that magnitude [2]. Where indicated, a separate scratch or leveling course is placed ahead of resurfacing; the scratch course fills wheel ruts and other small local depressions to be even with the surrounding pavement while the leveling course provides a relatively uniform working platform for binder or wearing courses, both actions preventing depression‑type distress [2]. Cement‑grout must be pumped in slabjacking until the surface is raised within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced concrete or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs; this tolerance is required to eliminate low spots after lifting [2]. If overjacking exceeds 0.10 foot, the pavement must be removed and replaced as specified [2]. The next‑generation concrete surface specification mandates a feather pass of at least two feet into shoulders and limits surface deviation to no more than 1⁄8 inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge measured perpendicular to the centerline; maintaining this cross‑slope prevents water‑filled low spots that could lead to depression or settlement [2]. The asphalt paving interlayer system must be placed on a dry, clean, drainable substrate with no depressions; repairing potholes, sealing cracks and removing loose debris during preparation eliminates low spots that could retain water [2]. Concrete pavement patching requires grinding any high points or depressions of 3⁄16 inch or more, re‑compacting disturbed subbase material, and replacing disturbed material deeper than one inch; limiting open patch time to less than forty‑eight hours and grading/compacting adjacent areas also prevents the formation of low spots [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete specifications require the subgrade or subbase to be clean and free of foreign material, ponded water, frost or frozen material before placement, a condition intended to prevent localized low spots caused by weak or saturated support layers [2]. The same RCC specification mandates achieving at least 85 % of maximum wet density for lifts thinner than six inches and at least 90 % for lifts six inches thick or greater directly behind the paver, followed by final rolling that must exceed 96 % of maximum wet density for all thicknesses; these compaction thresholds are prescribed to reduce settlement arising from insufficient densification [2]. After finishing the RCC surface any depression greater than ¼ inch is classified as a defect and must be removed by grinding, eliminating residual depressions that could collect water [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual in section 4.5 requires slab‑stabilization work—filling voids at the slab/subbase interface—to prevent loss of support that appears as small depressions, blow holes, and overall slab settlement; this practice shall be applied whenever voids are suspected and, if no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints must be stabilized in accordance with Publication 72M RC‑26M and Publication 408 Section 679, and joint and crack sealing must be performed concurrently to keep water from entering the pavement and reduce pumping that leads to settlement, while subsurface drains should be kept in good condition for the same purpose [1]. In section 4.6 slabjacking is prescribed as a construction practice to prevent or correct depressions caused by voids beneath the concrete pavement and extensive profile deficiencies such as embankment settlement; it should be applied when the slab can be placed in compression (i.e., not during high temperatures that cause joint compression) and dowel‑bar retrofits, referenced from Publication 72M and Specification 527, are required together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support [1]. Section 5.12 calls for a scratch course where indicated to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions, thereby eliminating low spots that could collect water, and a leveling course—minimum 1‑inch thick—may be placed when an erratic longitudinal profile exists because it is the only layer allowed to correct the profile or re‑establish the proper cross‑section, both measures being applied as part of overlay construction before the binder or wearing course is laid [1]. In section 8.15 tied concrete shoulders are required to reduce differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation; plain cement‑concrete shoulders are mandated for all projects using Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, urban Interstates/Expressways must use tied concrete shoulders implemented as full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, and rural Interstates/Expressways without anticipated work‑zone traffic support may use tied shoulders with undowelled transverse shoulder joints, while high‑volume rural corridors that need work‑zone support must employ full‑depth shoulders with dowelled joints, all to prevent settlement under the respective traffic conditions [1]. Section 514.3(d)1 of the Pennsylvania Specification requires the original pavement cross‑slope be restored and a uniform cross‑slope be maintained, and any transverse deviation greater than ¼ inch in 12 feet must be corrected; this provides a positive cross‑slope so the pavement drains and prevents localized low spots that could become depressions [2]. Section 483.3(g) calls for avoiding excess crowning of rutted areas and leaving a uniform surface when spreading micro‑surfacing, which is intended to prevent localized depressions that could develop from over‑filled or unevenly spread mixture, and section 483.3(h) mandates compaction of the entire placed area with a pneumatic‑tire roller at 40–60 psi before traffic, thereby helping eliminate settlement‑related depressions by achieving uniform density prior to opening to traffic [2]. Section 483.3(l) sets finished‑surface acceptance criteria that prohibit longitudinal streaks or bumps/depressions greater than ¼ inch measured with a 10‑ft straightedge and require ruts to be filled so no depressions remain as measured with a 12‑ft straightedge; these limits apply during the final inspection of the micro‑surfacing work [2]. Section 301.3(o) requires that after the concrete base has hardened the surface be retested with straightedges, that high points exceeding ¼ inch be removed by grinding or cutting, and that depressions greater than ⅜ inch be removed and replaced when directed in writing, with removal extending across the full lane width for at least 10 feet; these actions specifically target surface low spots that could collect water [2]. Section 450.3 specifies Type A patching for potholes or abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth, requiring cutting to sound material, cleaning, tack coating, placement of an asphalt wearing‑course and compaction until the patched surface conforms to the grade of the surrounding pavement, thereby addressing shallow depressions; when the depression exceeds three inches, Type B patching adds a binder‑course lift not to exceed three inches uncompacted beneath a 1½‑inch wearing‑course, with the same preparation and compaction steps, thus treating deeper depressions [2]. Section 413.3(g)2 calls for placing a separate scratch or leveling course ahead of resurfacing where indicated; a scratch course is used to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions so they are even with the surrounding pavement, and a leveling course provides a relatively uniform working platform for binder or wearing courses, thereby preventing low‑area depressions that could lead to settlement [2]. Section 509.3(b) requires diamond grinding or grooving to include a feather pass extending at least 2 feet onto shoulders and no shorter than 100 feet, to maintain the existing cross‑slope and forbid any surface depression or slope misalignment greater than ⅛ inch in a 12‑ft straightedge measurement; this directly limits localized depressions [2]. Section 509.3(d) notes that depressed pavement areas caused by subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes may be excluded from the 98 % texture requirement only when approved, indicating those depressions are treated as exceptions rather than conditions to be prevented [2]. Section 466.3(e) requires surface preparation of the existing pavement to be dry, clean and placed on a drainable surface with no depressions, and also calls for repairing potholes and other distresses as specified in Section 450 before the interlayer is laid; this directly prevents formation of localized low spots that could collect water and applies prior to installation of the interlayer system [2]. Section 516.3(n) mandates correction of any subbase surface irregularities deeper than one inch by loosening, removing or adding material as needed and recompacting with small vibratory compactors when disturbance occurs; after placement the patch surface must be checked with a 12‑ft straightedge and any high points or depressions greater than 3⁄16 inch must be ground smooth before acceptance, keeping patched concrete from developing low spots that could collect water [2]. Section 502.3(d)‑3 requires that the roller‑compacted concrete paver be of a type that does not tear, shove, gouge, produce tracks, indented areas, segregation, or other permanent surface blemishes, which avoids low spots created during spreading [2]. Section 502.3(e) mandates the subgrade/subbase be clean and free of foreign material, ponded water, frost or frozen material and be uniformly moistened before placement, eliminating settlement caused by uneven moisture conditions [2]. Section 502.3(c) prohibits placement during a hard rain that may damage the pavement surface, preventing rain‑induced depressions [2]. Section 502.3(k)‑2‑3 requires that after curing the pavement be inspected with a straightedge and any depression exceeding ¼ inch be removed by grinding to bring the surface within tolerance [2]. Section 501.3(g)5.a calls for edge‑slump control by testing the pavement surface with a 12‑ft straightedge before the concrete becomes non‑plastic and limiting edge slump to the specified tolerances, preventing low edges that could develop into depressions [2]. Section 501.3(k)3 requires continuous straightedge testing while the concrete remains plastic and immediate correction of any low areas, ensuring no observable departures from the straightedge before hardening [2]. Finally, section 501.3(o) mandates a surface‑tolerance check after curing to verify that depressions between adjacent lanes do not exceed 3⁄16 inch, otherwise the slab is deemed defective, thereby enforcing depression control [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

The Diamond Grinding specification (Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement, §514.3(d)) controls depression or settlement through a transverse tolerance: any cross‑slope deviation that exceeds ¼ inch over a 12‑foot length must be corrected and documented, with cross‑slope measurements taken at 100‑foot intervals along the pavement and the maximum depth of depression recorded for the Representative. The Polymer‑Modified Emulsified Asphalt Paving System (Micro Surfacing) specification (Section 483—Polymer‑Modified Emulsified Asphalt Paving System, §483.3(l) Defective Work) requires that longitudinal streaks may not have a ridge, bump or depression greater than ¼ inch when measured with a 10‑foot straightedge; longitudinal joints must be made so that any bump, ridge or depression does not exceed ¼ inch as measured with a 12‑foot straightedge; and the transverse profile requirement calls for ruts to be filled so that no depressions are observable using a 12‑foot straightedge. The Plain Cement Concrete Base Course specification (Section 301—Plain Cement Concrete Base Course, §301.3(o) Surface Tolerance) mandates rechecking the hardened surface with straightedges as called out in Section 501.3(k)3; any depression exceeding ⅜ inch must be removed and the affected lane segment replaced for a length of at least 10 feet, while high points over ¼ inch are to be ground or cut. The Manual Asphalt Patching specification (Section 450—Manual Asphalt Patching, §450.3) judges depressions by vertical depth: any pothole or abrupt depression three inches and less in depth must be repaired using Manual Asphalt Patching Type A, and depressions over three inches in depth must be addressed with Manual Asphalt Patching Type B. The Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavement specification (Section 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, Defective Work clause §506.3(t) point 4) treats a lot as defective when depressions or high points defined in Section 506.3(o) are present and cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative; this constitutes the acceptance limit for depression/settlement under that warranty. The Slabjacking specification (Section 681—Slabjacking, §681.3) requires the contractor to provide surveyed roadway‑profile drawings showing existing depressions and the grade achieved after jacking; the pavement is considered acceptable only if the jacked surface is within ±0.02 ft of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced concrete pavement, or within ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs or bridge‑end slabs, and overjacking greater than 0.10 ft triggers removal and replacement in accordance with Section 516 at no extra cost to the Department. The Next Generation Concrete Surface specification (Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface, §509.3) limits depressions or slope misalignments to no more than ⅛ inch over a 12‑foot length when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline; any area exceeding this must be reground, although depressed pavement areas caused by subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes may be excluded if approved. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specification (Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, §530.3(t)) similarly declares a lot defective when depressions or high points defined in Section 506.3(o) cannot be satisfactorily corrected as determined by the Representative; this visual/field determination is the acceptance limit within the LLCP warranty. The Asphalt Paving Interlayer System specification (Section 466—Asphalt Paving Interlayer System, §466.3(e) Surface Preparation) requires a drainable surface with no depressions; the acceptance limit for depression or settlement is zero and any detectable depression must be corrected before placement, verification being performed by visual inspection of the prepared pavement prior to installing the interlayer. The Concrete Pavement Patching specification (Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching, §516.3(n) Surface Tolerance) judges depressions with a 12‑foot straightedge applied across the patch area in the longitudinal direction and across joints; any depression of 3⁄16 inch or more must be corrected by grinding, at no additional cost to the Department unless the low spot originates from a surface deficiency in existing adjacent pavement. The Roller Compacted Concrete Pavement specification (Section 502—Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements, §502.3(k) Surface Tolerance) evaluates depressions using either straight‑edge testing or a crown surface template; any pavement surface variation that exceeds ¼ inch is deemed defective and must be corrected by grinding the surface to within tolerance. The Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavement specification (Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, §501.3(o) Surface Tolerance, referenced from Section 500 Rigid Pavements) sets the acceptance limit for depression or settlement at 3⁄16 inch or more between adjacent lanes; such depressions are considered defective, with judgment made by straightedge testing after the concrete has hardened as directed in Section 501.3(k)3.
