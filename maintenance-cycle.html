Maintenance Cycle

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Maintenance Cycle is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
maintenance-cyclemaintenance-cyclesinterstate-treatment-cycles
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The Maintenance Cycle is a systematic preservation approach that intervenes early‑stage surface distresses—such as rutting, loss of skid resistance and ride‑quality degradation—to keep pavements in a condition that postpones major rehabilitation or reconstruction and reduces the backlog of “out‑of‑cycle” mileage[1]. It operates by matching each route to a treatment‑type network defined by surface type and functional class, then using condition indicators (Pavement Surface Age, Pavement Structure Age, Overall Pavement Index) to trigger prescribed thin preservation treatments—micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, limited mill‑and‑overlay thicknesses, or targeted concrete shoulder ties—before advanced distresses appear[1]. The cycle’s defining characteristics include mandatory core inspection and structural verification (e.g., Falling Weight Deflectometer testing), strict limits on overlay depth (≤2 in for flexible pavements, ≤4½–5 in for rigid pavements) and patching area (≤10 % of concrete surface), and the requirement that any segment classified as “out of cycle” be treated within the timeframes set in the cycle tables to maintain service‑life targets[1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

The Maintenance Cycle is intended to address premature pavement deterioration that would otherwise cause earlier major rehabilitation or reconstruction, by keeping pavements in a condition that extends service life and reduces the backlog of roadways that have become “out of cycle” because maintenance was missed. In addition, the preservation‑guideline activities within the cycle target early‑stage surface failures such as rutting and other material problems, and they aim to restore lost skid resistance and ride quality; mill and overlay treatments are prescribed to eliminate rutting or problematic materials, while micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are used to improve skid resistance, ride quality, and fill ruts, and these interventions must be applied early in the cycle before advanced distresses emerge. The cycle also serves to identify pavement sections whose prescribed treatment intervals have been exceeded—i.e., mileage that is “out of cycle” according to the Interstate treatment cycles—signaled by Pavement Surface Age indicating a preservation need and by Pavement Structure Age that can trigger rehabilitation or reconstruction, often reflected in a low Overall Pavement Index where the percent of mileage in Poor condition is < 75. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 191–193), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 219–220)

The objective of a pavement maintenance cycle is to extend the pavement’s service life, which delays the first major rehabilitation or reconstruction and consequently reduces overall maintenance requirements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166), Ch. 11 (pp. 191–193)

Distress characterization and causes

Excessive edge stresses in a rigid pavement generate differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation at the pavement‑shoulder interface, while inadequate stiffness allows larger deflections that accelerate cracking and loss of serviceability; both mechanisms drive the distress that triggers scheduled maintenance cycles. By providing tied concrete shoulders—either full‑depth with dowelled joints or undowelled as specified—the edge stresses are reduced, stiffness is increased, and the resulting heave, settlement and separation are mitigated, thereby extending the pavement’s service life and postponing the first maintenance cycle for long‑life concrete pavements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166)

Applicability and treatment selection

Maintenance cycles are applied according to the treatment‑type network that matches the pavement surface and functional classification of a route. For concrete pavements (surface type codes 71–76) and unpaved surfaces (code < 50) the cycle is defined by those surface types, while asphalt routes are split into High Level Asphalt (surface codes 51, 52, 61, 62 with functional class codes 01, 02, 06, 07, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 99) and Low Level Asphalt (same surface codes but functional class codes 08 or 09). A separate Recycling Network is used for routes that typically have an ADT < 1000 and a Truck Percentage < 10%, which are considered part of the Low Level Asphalt network. Selecting the appropriate maintenance cycle therefore depends on the pavement surface type, the functional class code, and, for the recycling subset, the low traffic volumes indicated by those thresholds. [1]

When a proposed preservation treatment requires an overlay thickness greater than the limits set for Pavement Preservation—more than 2 inches on flexible pavements, or more than 4½ inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix (or more than 5 inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix) on concrete surfaces—the maintenance‑cycle approach is unsuitable and a full rehabilitation must be considered; likewise, if concrete patching would exceed 10% of the pavement area without demonstrated cost effectiveness, preservation is not appropriate. In addition, micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are only suitable early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses appear, so once such distresses have developed another repair strategy should be used. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Condition assessment and timing

To determine whether a maintenance cycle is required, the pavement must be cored and the resulting cores should be inspected to confirm both the depth of any distress and the integrity of the existing layers in accordance with the design‑guideline provisions; structural adequacy of concrete joints is verified with Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing before any patching or rehabilitation is planned. In parallel, the district must assess how much pavement mileage is “out of cycle” based on the treatment cycles defined for each pavement type, using Pavement Surface Age as an indicator of preservation needs and Pavement Structure Age as one of several triggers for rehabilitation or reconstruction; the assessment also requires calculating the Overall Pavement Index by determining the percent of miles in Excellent or Good condition that is greater than 85 % and the percent in Poor condition that is less than 75 %, and any interim maintenance that has improved surface condition must be recorded so that prior work does not mask the underlying need indicated by these measurements. [1]

According to the design‑guidelines section, the maintenance cycle for pavement preservation should be initiated when early‑stage distresses such as loss of skid resistance, minor rutting, or ride‑quality degradation are observed, and before any advanced distresses develop; at that point micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course may be applied to restore surface performance. The pavement‑project criteria state that a maintenance cycle should be applied when a segment is classified as “out of cycle” under the treatment cycles of the Interstate Management Program, which is identified either by the Pavement Surface Age indicating preservation needs or by the Pavement Structure Age reaching a factor that triggers rehabilitation or reconstruction, and may also be forecast to become out‑of‑cycle in upcoming years; additionally, mileage classified in Poor condition (less than 75) on the overall pavement index signals that part of the network is likely out of cycle and therefore requires treatment. Materials and repair details

The maintenance cycle for flexible pavements may use a mill‑and‑overlay limited to 1 1/2 inches or 2 inches unless deeper removal is needed to correct rutting, in which case coring determines the required depth; micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course are applied early to improve skid resistance and ride quality, while cold in‑place recycling of the base is allowed to a maximum depth of 4 inches followed by up to a 1 1/2 inch asphalt overlay containing 60 pounds per square yard of scratch course, and an ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlay may be placed as a non‑structural wearing course with an asphalt depth of 2 inches. For rigid pavements, scratch overlays must not exceed 4 1/2 inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or 5 inches when a Superpave 12.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used, and full‑depth concrete patching is limited to 10 % of the total pavement area unless cost effectiveness can be shown; additional options include diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, thin asphalt overlays of 1 inch (6.3 mm) or 1 1/2–2 inches of Superpave mixes, and dowel bar retrofit as part of concrete pavement restoration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

The performance of a maintenance cycle for concrete pavements is affected by the stiffness of the pavement because greater stiffness reduces deflections and prolongs service life. Tied concrete shoulders improve that performance by limiting differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation between the shoulder and the main pavement. When full‑depth cement concrete shoulders are used, all materials from the subgrade to the surface must match those of the mainline structure, providing material compatibility that supports longer intervals before maintenance is required. Projects employing Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) are expected to delay the first maintenance cycle relative to conventional concrete pavements. The suitability and expected performance of a maintenance cycle are also governed primarily by the pavement’s surface type and its functional class as defined in the RMS data; concrete, unpaved, high‑level asphalt and low‑level asphalt networks each have distinct treatment indicators that may be adjusted only for usage or other factors, while any change in surface type or functional class automatically updates the indicator. For the Recycling Network, which is a subset of the Low Level Asphalt network, routes are generally limited to an average daily traffic of less than 1000 and a truck percentage of less than 10%, conditions that affect how often recycling treatments are applied. Districts may manually edit treatment network indicators from “L” to “H” or vice versa based on facility usage or other factors, but they cannot change the indicators for concrete or unpaved surfaces. Existing pavement condition and material thickness limits further govern the appropriateness of preservation treatments. Overlay projects on existing flexible pavements that exceed 2 inches are not eligible for consideration as pavement preservation unless deeper milling is justified to remove rutting, and cores should be inspected to determine the depth and integrity of the current surface. Micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses must be applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge. Cold in‑place recycling of base material may not exceed 4 inches in depth and may be topped with a maximum 1½‑inch asphalt overlay containing 60 lb/yd² scratch course. For rigid pavements, scratch overlays must stay within 4½ inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm wearing mix is used or within 5 inches when a Superpave 12.5 mm mix is used, and concrete pavement patching may be performed up to, but not exceeding, 10 % of the area unless cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated; all joint repairs and structural adequacy must be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. Finally, the condition of the existing pavement drives how well scheduled treatments perform. Pavement surface age serves as an indicator of preservation needs, while pavement structure age is one of several factors that trigger rehabilitation or reconstruction. The overall pavement index influences cycle decisions by tracking the percent of mileage in Excellent or Good condition (> 85) versus Poor condition (< 75), and these thresholds determine when a pavement falls out of its prescribed cycle and guide the timing of interim maintenance or more extensive treatments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166), Ch. 11 (pp. 191–193), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 219–220)

Treatment procedures

To initiate a maintenance‑cycle treatment the guidelines require coring of the existing pavement so that the required milling depth—typically 1 ½ inches or 2 inches, but possibly deeper to correct rutting—can be established, and the cores must be inspected for depth and integrity. Removal is accomplished by milling to the determined depth, after which the specified overlay (e.g., a 1 ½‑inch maximum asphalt overlay over cold‑in‑place recycled base or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course) is placed. For concrete patching projects the required patching quantities are identified before final design and, when no asphalt overlay is used, diamond grinding may be performed to obtain a smooth ride. All preparation steps, including determination of patching areas such as faulted joints and cracks, must be completed prior to construction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Implementation of the maintenance cycle is constrained by traffic characteristics that define which network a route belongs to; for example, routes classified in the Recycling Network must have an average daily traffic less than 1000 and a truck percentage less than 10 percent. Additionally, districts can manually adjust a route’s treatment‑network indicator between low‑level and high‑level asphalt based on facility usage or other factors that affect how the route is maintained. [1]

Quality and workmanship

During a maintenance cycle the existing pavement material must be examined by coring and inspecting cores to confirm both the required milling depth and the integrity of the current layers. Any joint repairs or full‑depth patches are required to be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing, and overlay thicknesses may not exceed 2 inches on flexible pavements nor more than 4½ inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix (or 5 inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix) on rigid pavements; likewise concrete patching must stay at or below 10 % of the total pavement area unless cost effectiveness can be demonstrated. These inspections and tests serve as acceptance criteria for material condition, preparation depth, installation limits, and completed work quality. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

A satisfactory Maintenance Cycle is defined by several interrelated criteria. The RMS network indicator must correctly reflect the surface type and functional class—using “H” for High Level Asphalt or “L” for Low Level Asphalt—and the prescribed treatment must be performed within the timeframes defined in the cycle tables; pavements that have not been treated according to those intervals are classified as “out of cycle,” and indicators cannot be manually changed to or from “C” or “U,” while districts may manually edit the L/H designations based on actual usage. Additionally, a satisfactory cycle requires confirming that no pavement miles are classified as “out of cycle” under the prescribed treatment cycles and achieving Overall Pavement Index thresholds in which more than 85 % of the mileage is in Excellent or Good condition and less than 75 % may be in Poor condition, with any interim maintenance work clearly recorded so it does not obscure underlying needs. For preservation treatments, flexible‑pavement overlays must stay at or below two inches unless a rutting correction calls for milling and overlay depths of one‑and‑a‑half inches or two inches, which in turn require coring to verify existing pavement depth and integrity; micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are only acceptable when applied early in the cycle before advanced distresses emerge; cold‑in‑place recycling is limited to a maximum four‑inch base depth with an asphalt overlay not exceeding one‑and‑a‑half inches of 60 lb/yd² scratch course; for rigid pavements, scratch overlays may not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches when using a Superpave 9.5 mm mix or five inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix; and any concrete patching must be ten percent or less of the total area unless cost effectiveness is demonstrated, with all failed joints repaired and verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 191–193), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 219–220)

Performance and service life

The maintenance cycle improves pavement condition and functional performance across a range of treatments. For jointed plain concrete pavements that incorporate tied plain cement‑concrete shoulders, the added stiffness of the shoulders reduces stresses at the outer edge of the rigid slab, which in turn lowers deflection magnitudes; this stress mitigation together with the limitation of differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation enhances overall condition and functionality while reducing maintenance requirements. Because these durability benefits are integral to Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects, the first scheduled maintenance cycle can be postponed relative to conventional concrete pavements, thereby further extending the expected service life. More generally, adherence to the prescribed treatment cycles described in section 11.2 keeps pavements in better condition and preserves functional performance because regular interventions delay the onset of major rehabilitation or reconstruction; compliance with the defined timeframes therefore enhances durability and lengthens the anticipated service life, whereas pavements that are not maintained according to those schedules cannot be expected to achieve comparable lifespans. Similarly, when preservation treatments such as micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course are applied early in the cycle—i.e., before advanced distresses appear—they restore skid resistance, fill ruts and improve ride quality, which maintains functional performance. Because these thin, non‑structural layers are identified as cost‑effective in PennDOT’s Pavement Management System, they can be implemented without a full structural design, helping to preserve the existing structure’s durability and postponing more invasive reconstruction. The design guidelines further restrict flexible pavement overlays to thicknesses that do not exceed 2 inches, ensuring that preservation treatments remain thin and continue to extend the expected service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166), Ch. 11 (pp. 191–193), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Recurring distresses that can appear during a maintenance cycle depend on pavement type and the adequacy of structural restraint. For jointed plain concrete pavements, differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation at the pavement‑shoulder interface may arise when edge stresses are not adequately restrained or when the pavement lacks sufficient stiffness; the use of tied plain cement concrete shoulders increases edge stiffness, thereby reducing these distresses and extending service life. Within flexible pavements, rutting, loss of skid resistance, degraded ride quality, cracked or faulted joints, spalls and failed patches often become evident as the maintenance cycle progresses, especially when rutting or problematic materials require milling depths beyond the usual 1½‑ to 2‑inch limit. For rigid pavements, cracks, joint failures or spalling that push patching quantities toward the 10 % threshold may develop; eligibility for preservation treatments is limited to projects where total patching percentages stay less than 10 % unless cost effectiveness can be shown, and structural soundness must be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before thin‑asphalt overlays or polymer‑modified emulsified systems are applied. Micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses should occur early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge, and coring of existing pavement is required to determine the depth and integrity needed for corrective milling. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Monitoring and follow-up

In the maintenance cycle, inspection‑detected rutting or other identifiable material problems trigger a response that may require milling and overlay to a depth of 1 ½ inches or 2 inches, and if those distresses have progressed beyond the early stage the use of micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is no longer appropriate because those treatments must occur early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge. For concrete pavements, joint failures confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing that would require patching of more than 10 % of the total area are not eligible for preservation treatment unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is shown. Likewise, any overlay on flexible pavement that exceeds 2 inches is not eligible for consideration as pavement preservation, indicating that a more extensive rehabilitation is required. Additionally, when a segment’s Pavement Surface Age is identified as “out of cycle” or is forecast to become out of cycle in the coming years, the mileage is flagged for additional work; a Pavement Structure Age that reaches the point defined as a trigger for rehabilitation or reconstruction also signals the need for repair or rehab. Finally, an Overall Pavement Index that falls into Poor condition—defined as less than 75 % of the mileage—is another performance trend indicating that further maintenance is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 219–220)
