Air Content

Part of: Air Entrainment is-subtopic-of
Air Content is a topic under Air Entrainment.
air-content
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Air Content, often called plastic air, is the measured percentage of air voids present in a concrete mix while it is still fresh and later retained after hardening, determined from a single sample tested according to AASHTO T 152 for stone‑and‑gravel or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight aggregates [2]. It is created by introducing microscopic bubbles through an air‑entraining admixture—or, alternatively, by using suitable lightweight aggregate—and is controlled to meet design targets of 6 % (standard cement) or 7 % (AAAP, slip‑form and form paving) in the plastic state with a tolerance of ±1.5 %, while hardened concrete must retain total air between 3.5 %–7.5 % (or 4.5 %–8.5 % for AA mixes) and exhibit a spacing factor near 0.008 in and specific surface about 630 in²/in³ [2]. Maintaining these air‑void characteristics is essential because the specified void system provides durability benefits such as freeze‑thaw resistance, and acceptance testing treats plastic‑air results as a key quality‑control parameter—trucks outside the limits are rejected and the mix must be adjusted accordingly [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Air Content, referred to in the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications as plastic air, is the measured percentage of air voids present in a concrete mix or in fresh concrete and is determined from a single sample tested according to AASHTO T 152 for stone and gravel or AASHTO T 196 for slag coarse aggregate. In SECTION 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements the pavement construction quality program treats air content as a key acceptance parameter; the Representative evaluates the average sublot result against specification limits using a lower‑quality index that subtracts 5.0 % from the measured value, and concrete is accepted for air content only when the percentage of material within limits (PWL) is 55 % or greater. In SECTION 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement the quality‑control plan must require sampling the first truck of each lot for plastic air and trucks that do not meet the requirement of 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 % will not be incorporated; this acceptance criterion ensures that only concrete mixes with the specified air content are placed, providing a control mechanism to maintain the intended performance of the pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 380–437)

Composition and characteristics

Air content in pavement mixtures is defined by the volume of air voids present both while the mix is plastic and after it has hardened, comprising entrapped and entrained air that is introduced via an air‑entraining admixture solution or, alternatively, through a suitable lightweight aggregate incorporated into the blend of cement, flyash, slag cement, fine and coarse aggregates, bottom ash, water and other admixtures. For cement concrete the design target is 6.0% air in the plastic state; for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes the target is 7.0% in the plastic state, measured with AASHTO T152 or T196 within a tolerance of ± 1.5%. In hardened concrete the total air must fall between 3.5% and 7.5%, inclusive (or 4.5% to 8.5% for those mixes), and the air‑void system is characterized by a maximum spacing factor that shall be approximately 0.008 in and a minimum specific surface that shall be approximately 630 in²/in³. For pervious concrete, air content is expressed as the void structure measured on hardened sections in accordance with ASTM C 1754; the specification requires a void structure of 20% ± 5%, and the core void content must fall within ± 5% of the average voids determined from the accepted test sections. Within long‑life concrete pavement construction, air content is expressed as the measured plastic‑air percentage of each truck load, and the quality‑control plan requires that the first truck in every lot be sampled and only trucks whose plastic air falls within the requirement of 7.0%, plus or minus 1.5% may be used for placement, with subsequent loads required to achieve three consecutive passing plastic‑air tests before they are accepted. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–437), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), adding macrofibers to a concrete overlay changes the mixture’s air voids, so variations in fiber type or dosage can alter the measured air content and the mix designer must modify the amount of air‑entraining admixture during trial batches to achieve the required fresh‑property specifications [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications state that for standard cement concrete the plastic‑state air content is fixed at 6.0 % and for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes it is fixed at 7.0 %, therefore any change in aggregate source, mix composition or processing that would alter entrapped air must be compensated by adjusting the dosage of the air‑entraining admixture so that the measured plastic air content stays within ±1.5 % as determined by AASHTO T 152 or T 196, and the mix design shall be based on the actual materials to be used; if the specified range cannot be met, alternative acceptable materials or a modified mixing procedure shall be employed [2]. For hardened concrete the total air content must remain between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (or 4.5 %–8.5 % for AAAP‑type mixes), ensuring that variations in proportioning do not cause the air‑void system to fall outside these limits [2]. In flowable backfill, when the cement type is changed—such as using Type IP or IS—the quantities of fly ash or slag cement must be adjusted, which requires a revised mix design that includes testing for air content; similarly any change in material source triggers a new mix design because the resulting air voids may differ [2]. Incorporating an air‑entraining admixture, as required for flowable backfill, directly alters the air content and can lower the density values of the mixture [2]. Consequently the specification mandates that all composition, source, or proportioning variations be reflected in a submitted mix design with measured air content before construction [2]. Plant‑mixed concrete specifications require air content to be tested at the beginning, midpoint, and end of each batch, and any processing inconsistency that causes the measured air content to vary by more than 1.5 % must trigger a shutdown of the mixer or agitator until the condition is corrected, showing that mixing variations directly affect air‑void uniformity [2]. Cement, aggregates, water and admixtures must be proportioned in accordance with AASHTO M 157 for batching plants, which underpins consistent mixture quality including air content [2]. In long‑life concrete pavement production, variations in aggregate proportioning can affect the air voids, so any change that also alters cementitious material or water content must trigger a new mix design and trial batches; daily sieve analysis of plant‑stockpiled aggregate is used to keep gradations within PTM No. 529 limits, helping maintain consistent air content [2]. The quality‑control plan requires sampling the first truck of each lot for plastic air and excludes trucks whose measured air content falls outside the required 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 %; deviations from the gradation limits must be reported to QA, ensuring that air content remains within specification [2].

Key material properties

The design basis for cement‑concrete air content requires a plastic‑state air content of 6.0% (7.0% for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving) and a hardened‑state total air content that must be between 3.5% and 7.5% (4.5%–8.5% for those mixes), thereby defining the physical volume of voids that must be retained. The geometry of the air‑void system is controlled by a maximum spacing factor of approximately 0.008 in and a minimum specific surface of roughly 630 in²/in³, which together govern void distribution for durability. Air‑content testing is performed together with concrete temperature, slump and density; when a slump falls outside the target range an immediate air‑content and temperature test must be performed, and verification may not differ by more than 1.0% while temperature differences greater than 5 °F trigger corrective action. For Class AA concrete the acceptance criteria link air‑content performance to a 28‑day compressive strength lower quality index that requires an average exceeding 3,300 psi and to air‑content quality indices that require the average air content to be greater than 5.0% and less than 8.5%, with Percent Within Limits of at least 55%. Long‑life concrete pavement mandates measured entrained air content between 3.5% and 8.5%; values outside this range trigger a written evaluation, and the slab may not be opened to traffic until it reaches at least 3,000 psi compressive strength. Each sublot’s plastic air content must fall within the specification limits, otherwise trucks whose plastic‑air results are outside those limits shall be rejected. Self‑consolidating concrete mixes are required to have a plastic air content of 7% ± 2%, and mechanical performance is verified by confirming both the minimum curing strength and the 28‑day minimum concrete strength for each lot. Chemical consistency influences air voids because the mix must be placed with a water‑to‑cement ratio between 0.37 and 0.42, which must agree with verification testing within ±0.03, and the aggregate mineral filler must meet ASTM C1797 chemical and physical requirements so that its composition does not compromise stability of the air content. Thermal durability is addressed by a freeze‑thaw resistance test that must achieve a minimum durability factor of 80. In pervious concrete pavement systems the physical void structure must be about 20% ±5% as measured by ASTM C1754 because this air‑void volume controls permeability; the required infiltration rate of more than 200 inches per hour confirms that the target voids have been achieved. The hardened density is limited to a maximum of 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ±5 lb/ft³, ensuring sufficient strength while preserving void space. Placement may not occur when projected air temperatures fall below 40°F, and the pavement cannot be opened to traffic until it has cured for at least seven days with ambient temperature exceeding 55°F. For stone‑matrix asphalt mixtures bulk specific gravity of specimens is measured in accordance with AASHTO T 312 and used to compute air voids (V_a) and Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) at the design density; these values must fall within the tolerances set out in Table E. At least 95.0% of aggregate surfaces must be coated, a condition that helps maintain the target air content by preventing stripping during placement and compaction. Air‑void performance is also tied to measured air voids at the design maximum specific gravity and mixture density obtained from core density testing; three pavement cores are tested for density and a set of specimens for air voids. Placement temperature limits for asphalt paving mixtures state that they must not be placed when the air or surface temperature is 50°F or lower, because low temperatures can impair compaction and void retention. The apparent moisture content of plant‑mixed asphalt mixtures must not exceed 0.5%, and aggregate gradation together with bulk and theoretical maximum specific gravities are measured so that air voids can be calculated within the defined production temperature range, because temperature influences compaction and the achieved air voids. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 254–289), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Air content in fresh cement concrete is measured with air meters that conform to AASHTO T 196 or T 152 and are calibrated in the presence of the Inspector no more than two weeks before placement and recalibrated every two weeks during placement; initial testing is performed on the first three trucks and thereafter at a minimum frequency of one test per 50 cubic yards (or per 30 minutes for slip‑form paving), and any difference greater than 1.0 % between duplicate measurements triggers immediate review and corrective action. For standard cement concrete the required plastic‑air content is set at 6.0 %, while AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, and AA Form Paving mixes are required to achieve 7.0 % with an allowable deviation of ± 1.5 %; the target is maintained by adjusting the quantity of air‑entraining admixture according to AASHTO T 152 for stone‑and‑gravel mixes or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregate, and no aggregate correction factor is applied. After curing, the hardened‑concrete air‑void system is evaluated in accordance with ASTM C457; total hardened‑concrete air content must be between 3.5 % and 7.5 % inclusive for regular mixes and between 4.5 % and 8.5 % inclusive for AAAP‑type mixes, and additional parameters such as a maximum spacing factor of approximately 0.008 in. and a minimum specific surface of approximately 630 in²/in³ may be recorded; entrained air can also be measured by the unit‑weight method per ASTM C138. For Class AA concrete one sample from each sublot is tested according to AASHTO T 152 or T 196, the resulting percentage constitutes the air‑content value, and the average of these sublot results (X̄₄) is used in quality‑index equations with a lower limit Q_L calculated as (X̄₄ − 5.0 %)/s and an upper limit Q_U as (8.5 % − X̄₄)/s; acceptance of the measurement is achieved when the Percent Within Limits (PWL) is 55 % or greater. In Long‑Life Concrete Pavement a plastic‑air sample from the first truck of each lot must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 %; any truck whose result falls outside this range is rejected and subsequent trucks are retested until three consecutive passing results are recorded, and cores taken from the pavement are submitted to the LTS for an entrained‑air evaluation per PTM No. 623, with values below 3.5 % or above 8.5 % requiring a written evaluation and proposed solution within five days. Precast concrete products have air content measured together with slump and temperature tests in accordance with Section 704 and Publication 145 at the frequencies specified in Section 704; for self‑consolidating concrete the plastic‑air content must be within 7 % ± 2 %. Plant‑mixed concrete is tested by taking individual mixed samples approximately at the beginning, midpoint, and end of each batch and testing them according to AASHTO M 157; if the air‑content variation exceeds more than 1.5 %, the mixer or agitator must be discontinued until the condition is corrected. In pervious asphalt mixtures air voids are quantified by first determining the bulk specific gravity of compacted specimens in accordance with AASHTO T 312, then calculating air voids (V_a) at the design density using the procedures in AASHTO R 35 and Bulletin 27, and compliance is judged by averaging the results and checking them against the tolerances set out in Table B (or Table C). Stone matrix asphalt follows a similar volumetric analysis: at least two specimens are prepared per AASHTO T 312, bulk specific gravity is averaged, and V_a and VMA are calculated at N_design using the equations in AASHTO R 35 as referenced in Bulletin 27; compliance is determined by averaging all specimen results and comparing them with the tolerances listed in Table E. For flowable backfill air content is measured according to AASHTO T 152 (or alternatively T 196) without applying an aggregate correction factor, and these tests are performed together with slump and yield measurements as part of the quality‑control plan. In volumetric mixed cement concrete air content is quantified by performing an air‑content test prescribed in PTM No. 601 after mixing and before batch acceptance, providing a direct measurement of the volume of entrapped air voids required for quality control. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

The Pavement Policy Manual notes that in fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the presence of macrofibers alters the volume of entrained air voids, so the resulting air content is governed by this mechanical interaction between fibers and the cement paste. Consequently, the chemical air‑entraining admixture must be adjusted in trial batches to achieve the specified fresh‑mix air content when macrofibers are used. The Pennsylvania DOT specifications describe that air content in concrete is controlled by an interplay of chemical, physical and mechanical mechanisms whose relative importance varies with design stage and production method. Chemically, the primary mechanism is the addition of a solution of an air‑entraining admixture, which creates and stabilizes microscopic bubbles; dosage is adjusted according to AASHTO test methods so that the target plastic‑state air content—6.0 % for general mixes and 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes—is maintained within a tolerance of ± 1.5 %. Physically, the plastic mix contains both entrapped and entrained air that together provide the required level of voids. In plant‑mixed cement concrete the dominant physical mechanism is the mechanical mixing condition; the type of mixer (inclined‑axis or horizontal‑axis revolving‑drum), the prescribed range of revolutions, speed and mixing time create conditions that trap or release air, while maintaining concrete temperature between 50 °F and 90 °F further influences void stability. Chemical control in plant‑mixed concrete is provided by admixtures such as an AEA, which deliberately generate stable microscopic bubbles. Continuous testing requires that if the measured air content varies by more than 1.5 %, the mixer or agitator must be stopped until the condition is corrected, thereby ensuring that both mechanical and chemical mechanisms remain within acceptable limits. For pervious concrete pavement systems the intended void structure of 20 % ± 5 % (Void Content – ASTM C 1754) is preserved by mechanical actions during construction; smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode for one to four passes are required, pneumatic tire rollers are prohibited, and a final surface finish may be achieved with a maximum 2‑ton roller or steel troweling only if the surface voids remain open. The mixing speed is limited to a minimum of 70 to 100 revolutions of the drum and placement must occur within 90 minutes after the final introduction of mix water, both measures helping maintain the intended air‑void network. Curing begins within 20 minutes of concrete discharge and the pavement is covered with polyethylene sheeting for at least 7 days, preventing moisture loss that could collapse voids. [1][2]

Durability and deterioration

Under the long‑life concrete pavement (LLCP) construction criteria, concrete is considered defective when surface defects from rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, or honeycombing are present, and when edge slumping does not meet the requirements specified in Section 501.3(g)5; these damage processes can compromise the intended air‑void structure. Acceptance testing also treats plastic air content as a durability parameter, so trucks selected for acceptance testing with plastic air results that are outside specification limits will not be placed, indicating that insufficient entrapped air is a durability concern. After depth cores are taken from hardened slabs, the entrained air content is checked; if the entrained air content is below 3.5 % or above 8.5 %, it is treated as a durability problem that must be documented and a written evaluation of the problem and proposed solution must be provided to the Department within five days, and failure to take positive action may lead the District Executive to require that paving operations be stopped until an acceptable solution is agreed upon. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, a lot is deemed defective when the Percent Within Limits for plastic air content is less than 55 %, reinforcing that insufficient air voids fails to meet specification limits; the same list of surface defects from rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, or honeycombing also renders pavement defective. For pervious concrete pavement systems, the durability of the air‑void network is threatened by any process that blocks or seals the voids, such as accumulation of fine particles from saw‑cut joints which must be removed by vacuum to avoid clogging, and finishing operations that could close surface voids unless steel troweling is used only while keeping the voids open; rainfall on freshly placed concrete may cause loss of mortar at the surface and requires evaluation and possible repair, and opening the pavement to traffic before the concrete has cured for at least seven days with ambient temperature above 55 °F may damage the void structure. Pneumatic‑tire rollers are prohibited because they tend to seal the voids, and smooth steel‑wheeled rollers must be used in static mode with limited passes. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441)

Air Content is most vulnerable when the plastic air content of concrete falls below the specification limits; low‑air mixes are therefore discarded as defective. Maintaining proper curing procedures and protecting fresh pavement from rain helps prevent surface defects that could compromise the air void system, and complying with the required water‑to‑cementitious ratio of 0.37 to 0.42 is part of the acceptance criteria that supports adequate air content. For Class AA paving, the specification reduces the risk that the required air voids deteriorate by demanding durable aggregates that are free from slate texture or cleavage planes and thoroughly washed, prohibiting blast‑furnace slag aggregate, limiting reactive aggregates to those whose 14‑day expansion is less than 0.1 % (rejecting anything above 0.30 %), requiring blended hydraulic cement to keep mortar expansion at or below 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks, restricting the water‑cement ratio to not exceed 0.42 lbs/lbs (target 0.40–0.38), and mandating an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at placement; using non‑durable stone, unwashed or slate‑textured aggregate, excessive expansion, or exceeding the water‑cement or cement‑content limits would increase susceptibility to deterioration. Self‑consolidating concrete mixes are required to have a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 % and must also pass the ASTM C666 freeze‑thaw resistance test with a minimum durability factor of 80; satisfying both the air‑content target and the freeze‑thaw durability requirement is intended to limit deterioration of the air void system under freeze‑thaw exposure. In pervious concrete, keeping the surface voids open by using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers rather than pneumatic tire rollers, limiting the final finish to a maximum 2‑ton roller, and allowing steel troweling only when the voids remain open reduces susceptibility of air content to deterioration, while exposure to low temperatures or rain increases it; concrete must not be placed if air temperatures are projected to fall below 40 °F any time during the 24‑hour period after placement, rainfall before final finishing and curing can cause loss of mortar at the surface, and curing must begin within 20 minutes and be maintained under polyethylene sheeting for a minimum of 7 days. In Stone Matrix Asphalt mixes, air content is more vulnerable when the mix contains excess moisture or unburned fuel oil because the specification requires both aggregates and the finished mixture to be free of those contaminants; placement is prohibited when pavement surfaces are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower, and seasonal weather limits bar SMA placement from October 1 to March 31 in designated districts, while storing stabilizer material in a dry environment further reduces the risk of deterioration. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 287–289), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–455), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 693)

Material interactions and compatibility

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the inclusion of synthetic or steel macrofibers alters the mix’s entrained‑air volume, requiring designers to modify the air‑entraining admixture during trial batching to meet specified air content and fresh‑property criteria while maintaining durability goals; this adjustment compensates for the fibers’ influence on workability and slump. For Class AA Slip‑Form Paving and AA Accelerated Patching concrete used in bonded concrete overlays, the specification calls for an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at placement, and this target must be achieved concurrently with a cement factor that provides at least 400 lb/yd³ Portland cement, limits slag cement to no more than 30 %, and observes a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 (target 0.40–0.38). The same overlay specification also requires that any potentially reactive aggregate be paired with an approved cement‑supplementary blend capable of limiting 14‑day expansion to below 0.1 %, demonstrating coordination between air content, cement type, supplementary material proportion, water‑cement ratio, and aggregate reactivity for durability. In the flowable backfill specification, air content is introduced through an air‑entraining admixture sourced from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, and this admixture is considered together with cement type, fly ash or slag adjustments, and aggregate selection during mix design. The required mix design for flowable backfill calls for testing density, strength, and air content, acknowledging that the presence of an air‑entraining agent may reduce the achieved density and strength values, and permitting the use of a suitable lightweight aggregate as an alternative means to obtain the needed air voids. Influence of production and placement

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the presence of macrofibers can alter the entrained‑air volume, so the mix designer must adjust the dosage of air‑entraining admixture during trial batches to achieve the specified air content. [1] Consistent mixing is required to maintain target air content because the specification mandates testing air content at the beginning, middle and end of each batch and requires a shutdown of the mixer or agitator if variation exceeds 1.5 %. [2] Accordingly, plant mixers must operate for not less than 50 seconds and not more than 90 seconds for normal‑strength concrete, or for at least 70 seconds for high‑early‑strength (HES) concrete. [2] Truck‑drum mixers must complete between 70 and 125 revolutions at speeds of 6–18 rpm, followed by a post‑mix speed of 2–6 rpm, with a total revolution ceiling of 300. [2] For stone matrix asphalt, the plant is required to add stabilizer in the weigh hopper and increase both dry and wet mixing times so that the additive is uniformly distributed before binder injection, which assists in controlling the void structure of the compacted mix. [2] Prior to placement, concrete must be agitated for at least 20 revolutions immediately before discharge, and concrete that has been idle for more than 45 minutes without agitation must not be used. [2] Placement of pervious concrete must occur within 90 minutes after the final introduction of any mix water, and it may not be laid on a wet surface or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower, conditions that could trap excess moisture and raise air content. [2] Acceptance testing performed before laying ensures that only material satisfying the plastic‑air criteria contributes to the finished pavement’s durability. [2] Compaction of pervious concrete is limited to smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode for one to four passes, with pneumatic‑tire rollers prohibited; this practice preserves the target void content measured by ASTM C1754 within its ±5 % tolerance. [2] Compaction of stone matrix asphalt must be carried out with a minimum of three steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing at least 10 tons, and pneumatic‑wheeled rollers are prohibited; vibratory rolling may be used only after a satisfactory demonstration that no aggregate breakage or binder flushing occurs. [2] The final surface finish for pervious concrete may be achieved only with a maximum 2‑ton roller so that seams are eliminated while the surface voids remain open. [2] Improper final finishing is listed among defect conditions, and when the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content falls below 55 %, the lot is considered defective, indicating that poor finishing may compromise the performance benefits associated with proper air content. [2] Curing of pervious concrete must begin within 20 minutes of discharge, the surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting, and the curing period must be maintained for a minimum of 7 days to protect the air‑void network from premature drying or clogging. [2] Improper curing procedures are identified as defect conditions that can degrade the intended air‑void structure and lead to premature deterioration. [2] For long‑life concrete pavement, the curing plan must specify methods to cure and protect joints during the curing period and to prevent moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints during and following joint sawing. [2]

The long‑term behavior of air content is most strongly influenced by construction‑stage variations that cause the measured air voids to depart from specified limits. For cement concrete, air meters conforming to AASHTO T 196/T 152 must be calibrated in the presence of the Inspector no more than two weeks before placement and recalibrated every two weeks; if a test exceeds the upper or lower action points, testing frequency must increase to every truck until material control is reestablished, and concrete that does not meet specification requirements at placement must be rejected. The quantity of air‑entraining admixture used during mixing must maintain the plastic‑state air content within a tolerance of ± 1.5%, and any variation that causes the hardened concrete total air content to fall outside 3.5%–7.5% (or 4.5%–8.5% for AAAP mixes) is identified as a defect that can affect durability. Uniformity of plastic‑air across each sublot is required; any sublot whose measured result falls outside the specified lower 5 % and upper 8.5 % limits reduces the percent within limits (PWL), and when PWL drops below 55 % the concrete shall be deemed defective for air‑content compliance. Mixing practices also govern long‑term air content: mixing time must be at least 50 seconds and no more than 90 seconds for normal strength concrete (or at least 70 seconds for HES), temperature after mixing must be maintained between 50°F and 90°F for general concrete and between 50°F and 80°F for bridge decks, and if the air content varies by more than 1.5% the mixer or agitator shall be taken out of service until corrected. In addition, cement batching tolerances must be observed—cement weight 0 to +4 %, fine and coarse aggregates each ±2 %, admixtures ±3 %, water ±2 %—and drive‑shaft speed must be maintained within 50 rpm of the set operating speed; departures beyond these limits can alter entrapped air volume. For plant‑mixed asphalt courses, apparent moisture content must not exceed 0.5%; volumetric properties must conform to the tolerances of Table B, and a shift in theoretical maximum specific gravity of 0.030 or more from the previous test or JMF value shall trigger immediate notification; asphalt‑content results must remain within 0.2 percentage points for at least 90 % of a day’s production, and any specimen that falls outside Table B tolerances is a primary variation that can change air voids. In stone matrix asphalt mixture production, construction‑related variations that cause the mixture’s volumetric properties to fall outside the tolerances in Table E, aggregate gradation or asphalt content averages to fall outside Table D, draindown results not meeting Table D limits, or percent coated particles below 95.0 % directly influence the final air‑void content. Long‑life concrete pavement construction requires that the plastic‑air value of each truck be 7.0 % ±1.5%; trucks failing this requirement shall not be incorporated and must be retested until three consecutive passes are obtained. For pervious concrete, equipment that compresses or seals pores—such as pneumatic tire rollers or steel trowels—must not be used, the final finish roller may not exceed a 2‑ton weight, and rolling before sufficient strength is attained shall be avoided because these actions reduce the intended void content; curing must begin within 20 minutes of discharge and a polyethylene cover must be maintained for at least 7 days, and construction joints or saw‑cuts that leave fines without vacuum removal shall be prevented to avoid pore clogging. Finally, surface defects arising from rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, honeycombing, or unsealed surface voids are listed as conditions that can also alter the intended air‑void structure. Selection, specification, and improvement

The air‑content criteria for pavement materials are defined by the design class of the mix and by the performance limits established in the applicable sections. For regular cement concrete the design calls for an air content of 6.0 % in the plastic state and the hardened concrete must have a total air content between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive. For AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes the design calls for an air content of 7.0 % in the plastic state with an allowable tolerance of ± 1.5 % at the time and point of placement; after hardening the total air content must lie between 4.5 % and 8.5 %. The required quantity of air‑entraining admixture is that which maintains the specified plastic‑state air content, determined according to AASHTO T 152 for stone and gravel or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregate, without applying an aggregate correction factor. For Class AA rigid‑pavement concrete acceptance requires that the percentage within limits (PWL) be 55 % or greater and that a single sample tested according to AASHTO T152 or T196 and averaged for sublots give an air content between 5.0 % and 8.5 %. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement under Section 530 specifies that the measured entrained air content must be between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; any value outside this interval triggers a written evaluation and corrective action before paving may continue, and the lot is defective when the PWL for plastic air content falls below 55 %. In addition, LLCP quality control samples the first truck of each lot for plastic air and excludes any truck whose measured plastic air does not fall within 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 %; trucks that do not meet the requirement of 7.0 %, plus or minus 1.5 %, will not be incorporated, and after a failing test trucks must be retested until three consecutive passing results are obtained. When directed, air content of individual mixed concrete samples taken approximately at the beginning, midpoint, and end of each batch shall be tested, and if the air content varies by more than 1.5 % the use of the mixer or agitator must be discontinued until the condition is corrected. For pervious concrete pavement the void structure is measured according to ASTM C 1754 and a target of 20 % ± 5 % is required; this void‑content requirement is coupled with an infiltration performance criterion that test sections must achieve an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 inches per hour, and the accepted void‑content values serve as the basis for acceptance of the remainder of the project. Plant‑mixed thin asphalt overlay courses must produce a mixture whose air‑void content (Va) satisfies the volumetric tolerances listed in Table C of Section 1.d.5, with bulk specific gravity measured per AASHTO T 312 and Va and VMA calculated at Ndesign according to AASHTO R 35 and Bulletin 27; any mixture not meeting those limits must be corrected before acceptance. Stone matrix asphalt mixes are controlled by meeting the volumetric tolerances set out in Table E, with bulk specific gravity determined per AASHTO T 312 and air voids calculated at design density according to AASHTO R 35 and Bulletin 27, and compliance is established by comparing the average of all specimen results to the multiple‑specimen tolerances. Flowable backfill mixes are required to use an air‑entraining admixture listed in Bulletin 15; the mix design, including measured air‑content results together with density and strength data, shall be submitted at least three weeks before construction, and the design must incorporate either a suitable lightweight aggregate or the approved air‑entraining agent to satisfy the prescribed strength and density limits. Self‑consolidating concrete mixtures are specified to have a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 %; this requirement appears in the mixture specification prior to production and is verified by the routine slump, air and temperature tests prescribed in Section 704. Because macrofibers alter the amount of entrapped air in a concrete overlay, designers treat air content as a controllable fresh‑property by varying the dosage of an air‑entraining admixture during trial mixes of fiber‑reinforced concrete; the trial batches are required to verify that all fresh‑property limits, including air content, are satisfied. In addition, water‑reducing admixtures may be added to offset the slump loss caused by the fibers, thereby helping maintain workability and finishability while allowing the targeted air‑entraining dosage to be achieved. To meet the required air‑content performance the mix design must incorporate an air‑entraining admixture whose dosage is adjusted until the plastic concrete reaches a target of 6.0 % for standard cement concrete or 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes, with a tolerance of ± 1.5 % as measured by AASHTO T 152 or T 196. Portland cement may be partially replaced by fly ash or slag cement provided that at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement remains, the total cementitious material stays within Table A limits, and slag cement does not exceed 30 % by weight of the total cementitious material. The water‑cement ratio shall be verified in the field and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb (target 0.40 lb/lb) for standard mixes, while long‑life concrete pavement requires a water‑to‑cement ratio between 0.37 and 0.42. For flowable backfill an air‑entraining admixture listed in Bulletin 15 may be used together with a suitable lightweight aggregate; the mix design, including density, strength and air‑content test results, must be submitted at least three weeks before construction and certified according to Section 106.03(b)3. Air content shall be measured with AASHTO T152 (without an aggregate correction factor) or T196 and the mix adjusted on‑site until Table A criteria are satisfied; water may be added during placement to keep slump within the required range, which helps maintain consistent entrapped air. Placement shall be avoided when material temperature is below 50 °F or when the temperature of either the air or the surface is 40 °F or lower, and the in‑place backfill must be maintained at no less than 50 °F for a minimum of 24 hours. For long‑life concrete each sublot core shall be evaluated for entrained air content in accordance with PTM No 623 and the measured value must fall between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; if the measured air content is outside this range the contractor must provide a written evaluation of the problem together with a proposed solution within five days, and failure to take positive action may result in halting paving operations. Continuous moisture monitoring with calibrated meters or automatic moisture‑compensating probes and verification of aggregate and cement weights on scales checked per PTM No 410 are required to reduce water variability that can upset air entrainment. Air content shall be measured on samples taken at the beginning, midpoint and end of each batch, and if the variation exceeds 1.5 % the mixer or agitator shall be stopped until corrected. Inclined‑axis or horizontal‑axis revolving‑drum mixers are required, and concrete temperature shall be maintained between 50 °F and 90 °F (or 50 °F–80 °F for bridge decks). The first truck of each lot shall be sampled for plastic air; trucks whose measured air is outside the requirement of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % shall be rejected and retested until three consecutive passing results are obtained, and daily monitoring of aggregate gradations by sieve analysis allows mix proportions to be adjusted promptly. For self‑consolidating concrete mixes a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 % is required; maintaining slump within the prescribed ranges—no more than 5 in for mixes without water‑reducing admixtures, 6½ in with water‑reducing admixtures, or 8 in with high‑range water‑reducers—helps achieve proper air entrainment, and a small portion of aggregate mineral filler may be used as a fine‑aggregate replacement to aid optimization. Volumetric mixed cement concrete plants must be inspected and listed, calibrated at least annually (or when aggregate or cement sources change), with gate openings and meters adjusted to tolerances of 0 to +4 % for cement weight, ±2 % for fine and coarse aggregates, ±3 % for admixtures, and ±2 % for water. During mixing the drive‑shaft speed shall stay within 50 rpm of the set point and flow meters for water and additives shall be coordinated with aggregate and cement feeds; each batch is required to undergo slump and air‑content testing per PTM No 601 to verify that the produced concrete meets the design air‑void specifications. Plant‑mixed asphalt mixtures shall be subjected to daily volumetric analysis of compacted specimens so that bulk specific gravity can be used to calculate air voids (Va) and VMA at N_design; results must conform to the tolerances of Table B, and calibrated measuring and conveying devices, a certified Level 1 plant technician on‑site and an on‑call Level 2 technician are mandated. Approved warm‑mix asphalt technologies—including the specified additive or modifier and, when required, an anti‑strip additive—may be incorporated under the dosage and blending procedures outlined in the specification to improve workability and moisture resistance, thereby supporting consistent air content. For stone matrix asphalt mixtures the plant may be modified to incorporate warm‑mix additives, modifiers, or foaming processes so that a uniform blend of the additive is produced; a stabilizer (fiber) supply system shall meter fibers within ±10 % of the required weight and provide low‑level and no‑flow indicators, feed‑rate printouts and a transparent pipe for visual inspection, while increased dry and wet mixing times together with dry storage of the stabilizer help ensure uniform distribution before binder is introduced. A paving operation quality‑control plan shall include a pre‑placement meeting, documented construction equipment and methods, and a trial demonstration strip in which loose plant samples and pavement cores are tested for air voids at N_design(Va) and maximum specific gravity; testing shall be performed by an accredited HOLA laboratory with calibrated equipment and regular calibrations to the Representative’s satisfaction. For pervious asphalt pavement systems air‑void structure may be improved by using approved polymer additives or ground tire rubber in the binder, limiting recycled asphalt pavement to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture, adding an anti‑strip additive at a dosage not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, and incorporating hydrated lime when moisture susceptibility testing indicates need; if testing per AASHTO T 305 shows a stabilizer is required, cellulose fibers shall be added at a rate between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix. For pervious concrete pavement systems the contract calls for test sections whose void content is measured by ASTM C 1754 and must be 20 % ±5 %; hardened density is accepted only if it stays within a tolerance of ±5 pounds per cubic foot of the test‑section average and does not exceed 130 pounds per cubic foot, and ongoing quality controls include unit‑weight testing on every truck (ASTM C 1688), core sampling for void content, and infiltration testing that must exceed 200 inches per hour. Construction practices that protect the void structure are specified: wetting the subgrade before placement, using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode with one to four passes (and not using pneumatic tire rollers), limiting mixing and hauling to within 90 minutes after water addition, beginning curing within 20 minutes while completely covering the surface with polyethylene sheeting for a minimum of seven days, and avoiding placement when projected air temperatures are below 40 °F or drop lower any time during the first 24‑hour period after placement. Quality‑control actions common across materials include calibrating all air meters a maximum of two weeks before beginning concrete placement in the presence of the Inspector, recalibrating them every two weeks during placement, providing sufficient thermometers and backup equipment to ensure no tests are missed, and increasing testing frequency to every truck when a QC test fails to conform to the specified requirements or exceeds the upper or lower action points. The Representative accepts concrete on a lot‑by‑lot basis when the percentage of air content meets the Class AA limits expressed by Q_L = ( X̄₄ – 5.0 % )/s and Q_U = ( 8.5 % – X̄₄ )/s with a Percent Within Limits (PWL) of 55 % or greater; trial mixtures are prepared, the plastic‑state air‑content test is performed on each batch, and if deficiencies are suspected the hardened concrete air‑void system is evaluated by ASTM C457 to verify that the maximum spacing factor is approximately 0.008 in and the minimum specific surface is about 630 in²/in³; the total hardened‑state air content must then fall between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % for AAAP‑type mixes). Performance evaluation and implications

Air content in fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays is evaluated through laboratory trial batches in which the air‑entraining admixture is adjusted and the resulting mix is checked against fresh‑property specifications; the source does not describe any field or long‑term performance evidence for air content. [1] Air content in cement‑concrete mixes is evaluated on the job site by testing fresh concrete with calibrated air meters that meet AASHTO T 152 or T 196 during quality‑control sampling, acceptance testing, verification testing and QA inspections, and a result that differs by more than 1.0 % from the reference triggers a review of procedures and equipment (Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE, 704.1(d)). In addition, three QA cylinders are sent to Laboratory Technical Services for hardened‑state air content determination in accordance with PTM No. 623, providing laboratory evidence of retained air voids after curing (same section). The plastic‑state air percentage is also measured using AASHTO T 152 for stone‑and‑gravel mixes or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregates with a tolerance of ± 1.5 % as specified in Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE, 704.3. When hardened concrete appears deficient, the air‑void system is examined with ASTM C457 to verify that the spacing factor does not exceed approximately 0.008 in and that the specific surface is at least about 630 in²/in³, and the total hardened‑state air content must be between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive (same section). For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements a single plastic‑air sample taken from each sublot is tested according to AASHTO T 152 or T 196; the measured air content is compared with specification limits of 5.0 % as the lower bound and 8.5 % as the upper bound, and acceptance requires a Percent Within Limits for air content of at least 55 % (Section 506—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, 506.3(u)). Long‑life concrete pavement uses the plastic‑air content on samples taken from each sublot before placement as field acceptance evidence; any truck whose result falls outside the specified limits must be discarded and subsequent trucks retested, with compliance determined by the Representative (Section 530—LONG‑LIFE CONCRETE PAVEMENT, 530.3(t)). Precast concrete products are evaluated in the laboratory for slump, air content and temperature at frequencies set by Publication 145; self‑consolidating concrete is required to have a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 % (Section 714—PRECAST CONCRETE PRODUCTS, 714.7), and long‑term performance is demonstrated by a freeze‑thaw resistance test performed in accordance with ASTM C666/C666M Procedure A or B that must achieve a minimum durability factor of 80 (same section). Plant‑ and truck‑mixed cement concrete requires testing of individual mixed samples taken approximately at the beginning, midpoint and end of each batch; if the measured air content varies by more than 1.5 % the mixer or agitator must be stopped until the condition is corrected (Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE). The quality‑control plan may also require that the first truck in each lot be sampled for plastic air with a requirement of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %; trucks failing this requirement are not incorporated and are retested until three consecutive passing results are obtained (Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE). Superpave plant‑mixed asphalt mixtures are evaluated in the laboratory by preparing compacted specimens according to AASHTO T 312, determining bulk specific gravity as specified in AASHTO T 312 and calculating air voids (V_a) and Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) at N_design using AASHTO R 35 and Bulletin 27; for daily production of each job‑mixing facility greater than 150 tons the mixture is sampled and volumetric analysis performed at least once each day, and the results must conform to the tolerances of Table B (Section 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, STANDARD AND RPS CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED ASPHALT COURSES). Thin asphalt overlay courses follow a similar procedure with a minimum of two compacted specimens from each sample tested according to AASHTO T 312 and compliance judged against the tolerances of Table C (Section 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED 6.3 mm THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY COURSES). Stone‑matrix asphalt mixtures are evaluated in a HOLA laboratory that has demonstrated proficiency through an AASHTO re:source On‑Site Laboratory Assessment; air voids at N_design and the mixture’s maximum specific gravity are measured, and field evidence consists of extracting three pavement cores from the demonstration placement or from the first 100 tons of production and measuring their density to verify compliance (Section 419—STONE MATRIX ASPHALT MIXTURE DESIGN). For pervious asphalt pavement systems air voids are determined in the laboratory by measuring bulk specific gravity using any method listed in Table B, gyrating two specimens according to AASHTO T 312, averaging the results and calculating air voids; verification testing may be performed by LTS at the Department’s discretion and material certifications for asphalt content, gradation and air voids provide additional evidence of compliance (Section 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM). Pervious concrete pavement systems are evaluated in the laboratory for void structure according to ASTM C1754 with a target of 20 % ± 5 %; field evidence includes constructing two test sections that meet the same tolerance, performing infiltration testing on three areas per 10 000 ft² in accordance with ASTM C1701 and requiring an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 inches per hour, and coring the installed pavement for oven‑dry density and void content determination according to ASTM C1754, accepting the results if core void content is within ±5 % of the average voids measured in the test sections (Section 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM). Flowable backfill mixes are evaluated in the laboratory for air content using AASHTO T 152 or T 196 without applying an aggregate correction factor; the mix‑design submission must include test results for density, strength and air content if applicable and be provided to the Representative at least three weeks before construction (Section 220—FLOWABLE BACKFILL). Air Content is a prescribed mix‑design parameter for cement concrete. For standard cement concrete the mix must achieve an air content of 6.0 % in the plastic state, while AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes are required to target 7.0 % in the plastic state, and this level is maintained by dosing an air‑entraining admixture according to AASHTO T 152/T 196 with a tolerance of ± 1.5 % during placement. After hardening the concrete’s total air content must fall within defined limits—3.5 % to 7.5 % for regular mixes and 4.5 % to 8.5 % for the specified AA mixes—and the air‑void system must meet a maximum spacing factor of about 0.008 in and a minimum specific surface of about 630 in²/in³, ensuring that the pavement attains the durability criteria embedded in the specification. During LLCP construction each sublot must include a test for the percentage of plastic air content; any truck whose plastic air result falls outside the specified limits is rejected and cannot be placed, and low‑air‑content trucks must be discarded with subsequent trucks retested. In the same LLCP specifications entrained air content of the concrete slab is monitored through core or nondestructive testing; the acceptable range is defined as at least 3.5 % and not more than 8.5 %. When measured air content falls below 3.5 % or exceeds 8.5 %, the contractor must submit a written evaluation of the problem and a proposed solution within five days, and failure to take positive action can lead the District Executive to halt paving operations until an acceptable remedy is agreed upon. The specification requires that air content be measured on individual mixed concrete samples taken near the beginning, midpoint, and end of each batch; if the air content varies by more than 1.5 % the mixer or agitator must be discontinued until the condition is corrected, preserving uniformity for intended performance and durability. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements a lot is considered defective when the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content is less than 55 %; when this occurs the specification mandates removal and replacement of the pavement segment, making maintenance dependent on maintaining air‑content within prescribed limits. Air content in flowable backfill is controlled by incorporating an air‑entraining admixture that must be sourced from a listed manufacturer for this application; the mix design therefore has to include measured air content along with density and strength results, and because entrained air can lower the achieved density and strength designers must account for these reductions when meeting the specified performance criteria, ensuring that the final backfill still satisfies the required durability and structural requirements. For pervious concrete the required void structure of 20 % ± 5 % according to ASTM C 1754 is the primary control on permeability; the design calls for an average infiltration rate that must exceed 200 inches per hour, so maintaining the specified void content ensures adequate drainage and thus pavement performance. During construction the void content of each core must be within ±5 % of the average voids measured in the approved test sections, providing a check on the achieved air content before acceptance, and finishing operations such as steel troweling are permitted only if the surface voids remain open, because closed voids would reduce permeability and compromise durability. In stone matrix asphalt mix verification air voids measured at the design density (N_design (V_a)) must fall within prescribed tolerance limits; if the measured air voids—or any other parameter—fail to meet those limits the contractor must repeat the demonstration strip before proceeding, underscoring that proper air content is essential for achieving target density and overall mixture quality that support pavement performance.
