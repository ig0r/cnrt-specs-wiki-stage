Air Content

Part of: Air Entrainment is-subtopic-of
Air Content is a topic under Air Entrainment.
air-content
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Air Content is the percentage of air voids—both entrapped and intentionally entrained—present in a plastic concrete mix, measured as the proportion of entrained air during routine slump, air and temperature testing of fresh concrete [2]. It is created and stabilized by adding an air‑entraining admixture that generates microscopic bubbles, providing the chemical mechanism needed to achieve the specified void structure for durability and performance [2]. The specification requires a target plastic‑state air content of 6.0 % for standard cement concrete or 7.0 % for AAAP, slip‑form, and fixed‑form mixes, each held within a ±1.5 % tolerance, with hardened concrete subsequently required to retain total air between 3.5 %–7.5 % (or 4.5 %–8.5 % for the latter mixes) to meet durability criteria [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Air Content is defined as the percentage of air voids—both entrapped and entrained—present in the plastic concrete mix, which is measured as the proportion of entrained air voids in fresh concrete during routine slump, air and temperature testing. In Section 704—Cement Concrete, the mix design requires that the air content be achieved by adding an air‑entraining admixture and be kept within a tolerance of ± 1.5%; the specification calls for 6.0% air content for standard cement concrete and 7.0% for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes, a critical parameter that must be controlled to meet the pavement’s performance criteria. In Section 714—Precast Concrete Products, the specification calls for a plastic air content of 7% ± 2% for self‑consolidating concrete mixtures, a control that supports meeting durability criteria such as the freeze‑thaw resistance requirement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

Composition and characteristics

Air content is the volume of air voids present in a concrete mix, consisting of both entrapped air and intentionally entrained air introduced through an air‑entraining admixture solution, and it is expressed as a percentage of the total mix volume while the concrete is in the plastic state. For standard cement concrete the design target is 6.0 % in the plastic state, whereas for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving the target is 7.0 %. In long‑life concrete pavement mixes the plastic‑air percentage must be 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 %, with any truck that fails this test excluded until three consecutive passing results are obtained. After hardening, the total air content must fall between 3.5 % and 7.5 % inclusive for general mixes, or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % inclusive for the specified mixes. The air‑void system is further characterized by a maximum spacing factor of approximately 0.008 inch and a minimum specific surface of approximately 630 in²/in³, and measurement of air content must be within a tolerance of ±1.5 %. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 448–449), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) section 10.10, adding macrofibers changes the mixture’s air voids; thus variations in fiber type or dosage can alter the measured air content of a concrete overlay, and the mix designer must modify the amount of air‑entraining admixture during trial batches to achieve the required fresh‑property specifications [1]. When the type of coarse aggregate is changed, the quantity of air‑entraining admixture must be adjusted so that the plastic concrete air content meets the specified target of 6.0 % for ordinary cement concrete or 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving; the dosage is determined according to AASHTO T 152 for stone and gravel or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregate and must be kept within a tolerance of ± 1.5 % during work [2]. After hardening, the total air content must fall between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving) [2]. For long‑life concrete pavement, any truck whose measured plastic air content falls outside the specification limits will not be placed, and a low plastic air result triggers testing of consecutive trucks as required in Section 704.1(d)4.a.; the quality‑control plan must require that the first truck in each lot be sampled for plastic air and state that trucks that do not meet the requirement of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % will not be incorporated, with one set of compressive‑strength specimens together with a test for percentage of plastic air content made on samples from each sublot [2]. If air‑content measurements taken at the beginning, midpoint and end of a batch show a variation of more than 1.5 %, the mixer or agitator must be discontinued until the condition is corrected; adjustments to aggregate proportions that also require changes in cementitious material or water content will require the development of a new mix design and trial concrete batches because such composition shifts directly influence whether the air‑content requirement is met [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 448–453), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–681)

Key material properties

Air‑content performance is governed by several interrelated properties that are defined for each pavement material. For cement concrete, the design basis in Section 704—Cement Concrete requires a plastic air content of 6.0 % (or 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes) measured with a tolerance of ± 1.5 %, while hardened concrete must retain total air between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (4.5 % to 8.5 % for those mixes); the air‑void system shall exhibit a maximum spacing factor of approximately 0.008 inch and a minimum specific surface of approximately 630 in²/in³, achieved by adding an air‑entraining admixture in accordance with AASHTO T 152 or T 196. Acceptance testing for cement concrete evaluates both the mechanical property of 28‑day compressive strength and the percentage of plastic air content on each sublot, and a lot is approved only when the Percent Within Limits reaches at least 55 % (Section 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements). In Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, the first truck of each lot must meet a plastic air requirement of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %, and placement temperatures are limited to 80 °F to prevent loss of entrained air; trucks whose plastic‑air results fall outside specification limits are rejected (Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement). For pervious concrete, the void structure shall be about 20 % ± 5 % as measured by ASTM C1754 and the hardened density shall not exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³; the resulting air‑void network must support an average infiltration capacity greater than 200 inches per hour, and placement is prohibited when projected air temperatures are below 40 °F. Flowable backfill mixes must report density, compressive strength and measured air content, and chemical durability criteria require a resistivity greater than 2000 ohm‑centimeters after a 60‑day cure, a pH between 5.0 and 9.5, and chlorides below 200 ppm to ensure that entrained air does not compromise corrosion resistance (Section 220—Flowable Backfill). In Superpave and related asphalt mixtures, the physical capacity to retain air is expressed through bulk specific gravity measured per AASHTO T 312 together with theoretical maximum specific gravity; these values are used to calculate air voids (Va) and Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) at design density, and both must fall within the tolerances of Table C or Table E depending on the mixture. The apparent moisture content of the mix shall not exceed 0.5 %, and any variation of theoretical maximum specific gravity of 0.030 or more from the previous test or from the JMF value triggers immediate notification; asphalt content, aggregate gradation and draindown are monitored because they control mixture density and VMA, influencing how much air can be accommodated (Sections 413 and 412). Stone Matrix Asphalt mixtures also rely on bulk specific gravity to compute Va and VMA, with volumetric properties required to conform to Table E; proper compaction, aggregate cleanliness, and avoidance of placement when surfaces are wet or the air/surface temperature is 50 °F or lower are essential for preserving the intended air‑void structure (Section 419). Finally, self‑consolidating concrete mixes must maintain a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 % and achieve a freeze‑thaw durability factor of at least 80 to protect the mixture during thermal cycling (Section 714—Precast Concrete Products). [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–187), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 248–297), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 389–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

Air content of fresh cement concrete is measured on‑site with air meters that conform to AASHTO T 196 or T 152; the meters must be calibrated no more than two weeks before placement and recalibrated in the presence of the Inspector every two weeks, with initial testing on the first three trucks and thereafter at a minimum frequency of one test per 50 cubic yards (or per 30 minutes for AA Slip Form or Fixed Form paving). The same calibrated meters are used for acceptance, verification and QA sampling, and any difference greater than 1.0 % in air‑content results between duplicate tests requires the Representative to immediately review testing procedures, equipment and personnel. For standard cement concrete the plastic‑state air content is targeted at 6.0 %, while AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes are targeted at 7.0 % with a tolerance of ± 1.5 %; the dosage of air‑entraining admixture is determined according to AASHTO T 152 for stone and gravel or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregate, without applying an aggregate correction factor. The hardened‑concrete air‑void system is characterized by testing per ASTM C457, which specifies a maximum spacing factor of approximately 0.008 inch and a minimum specific surface of approximately 630 in²/in³; the total air content in hardened concrete must be between 3.5 % and 7.5 % inclusive (or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % inclusive for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving). Entrained‑air content may also be determined by the unit‑weight method per ASTM C138 related to results from ASTM C173. For Class AA concrete the air‑content property is measured on a plastic sample according to AASHTO T 152 for stone and gravel or AASHTO T 196 for slag coarse aggregate; the average result for each sublot (\overline{X}_4) is used in a quality‑index calculation where Q_L = (\overline{X}_4 – 5.0 %)/s and an upper limit may be expressed as (8.5 % – \overline{X}_4)/s, and acceptance requires the percent‑within‑limits to be 55 % or greater. For a concrete pavement lot air content is measured as required by Section 506.3(u)2 and expressed as a characteristic percentage (Pa) obtained from Table B; this percentage is used together with other characteristic percentages in the multiple‑characteristic formula that determines lot payment. In Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (Section 530.3) entrained air content is measured by taking information cores—the first core in a sublot and every tenth thereafter—and submitting them to the LTS for evaluation according to PTM No. 623; the resulting percentage must be at least 3.5 % and not more than 8.5 %, with any out‑of‑range result triggering a written problem‑solution report. In Section 506.3 Construction air content is similarly assessed by submitting information cores from each sublot to the LTS for entrained‑air evaluation per PTM No. 623; values outside 3.5 %–8.5 % also require a written problem evaluation and proposed solution within five days. In Section 714.7 air content on fresh concrete is measured in accordance with the procedures of Section 704 and Publication 145 at the frequencies established there; for self‑consolidating concrete mixtures the required plastic air content is 7 % ± 2 %, which must be verified during production. In Section 704.2, when directed, air content may be tested on individual mixed‑concrete samples taken approximately at the beginning, midpoint and end of a batch; if the measured air content varies by more than 1.5 % the mixer or agitator must be discontinued until corrected. In Section 506.3(t) Defective Work plastic air content is expressed through the Percent Within Limits (PWL) metric; under Section 506.3(u)2 a lot is classified as defective when the PWL for plastic air content falls below 55 %. For pervious asphalt pavement (Section 420.2) air voids are measured by first determining the bulk specific gravity of two gyrated specimens in accordance with AASHTO T 312, averaging those values, and then applying any method from Table B to calculate air voids; this determination is required during mixture design. For pervious concrete (Section 520.3) void content is quantified using the ASTM C1754 test, which determines oven‑dry density and calculates the void structure; the specification calls for a void structure of 20 % ± 5 %, with three 4‑inch diameter cores taken per 5,000 sq ft required to show a core void content within ± 5 % of the average voids measured in approved test sections. For flowable backfill (Section 220.3) air content is measured by testing a sample with AASHTO T 152 (no aggregate correction factor) or, as required, AASHTO T 196; the resulting quantitative value of air‑void volume is used for acceptance of the backfill material. For stone matrix asphalt mixtures (Sections 419.1 and 419.3) air content is quantified as air voids (Va) by preparing compacted specimens in accordance with AASHTO T 312, determining each specimen’s bulk specific gravity, and calculating Va at the design density using AASHTO R 35; the calculated Va values are compared to the multiple‑specimen tolerances specified in Table E, and during trial demonstration air voids are measured from pavement cores and must satisfy both single‑sample and multiple‑sample tolerance limits before mix acceptance. Finally, for cement concrete (Section 704.3) air content is quantified by performing an air‑content test as prescribed in PTM No. 601 after mixing, together with slump, unit weight and other standard evaluations to verify that the batch meets the specified tolerances. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the presence of macrofibers alters the volume of entrained air voids, so the resulting air content is governed by a mechanical interaction between fibers and the cement paste. Because the fibers tend to affect the air, the chemical air‑entraining admixture must be adjusted in trial batches to achieve the specified fresh‑mix air content. In cement concrete the addition of an air‑entraining admixture creates and stabilizes microscopic bubbles, providing the chemical mechanism that governs air content. The plastic mix contains both entrapped and entrained air, and the amount incorporated is controlled by using the quantity of admixture required to achieve the target air content as determined in accordance with AASHTO T 152 (or T 196) within a tolerance of ± 1.5 %. For plant‑mixed concrete the uniformity of air content is primarily controlled by the mechanical mixing actions and the presence of chemical air‑entraining agents; measurement at the beginning, midpoint and end of a batch and a variation greater than 1.5 % mandates discontinuation of the mixer or agitator until corrected, indicating that mixing intensity and consistency directly control entrapped air. Maintaining concrete temperature after mixing between 50 °F and 90 °F for general concrete (or between 50 °F and 80 °F for bridge‑deck concrete) and adhering to prescribed mixing times—at least 70 and no more than 125 truck‑drum revolutions at a speed not less than 6 rpm nor more than 18 rpm—provides the physical conditions that influence bubble stability. In pervious concrete pavement systems the target void structure of 20 % ± 5 % is governed primarily by mechanical and physical handling during construction; mixing at the manufacturer‑specified speed of 70 to 100 drum revolutions, placing the mix within 90 minutes after water addition, and wetting the subgrade so it is saturated but free of standing water all help retain the intended voids. Mechanical preservation of the air space is further ensured by using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes and avoiding pneumatic‑tire rollers, while placement must not occur below 40 °F and a minimum curing period of seven days with ambient temperatures exceeding 55 °F prevents premature loss of voids. For flowable‑backfill mixes air content is chiefly controlled by the inclusion of air‑entraining admixtures, which chemically create and stabilize the required air voids; the overall mix design—including proportions of cement, fly ash, slag, aggregates, water and other admixtures—determines the density and strength at which that air content is measured. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–187), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–681)

Durability and deterioration

In self‑consolidating concrete mixes the specification in Section 714—Precast Concrete Products requires a plastic air content of 7% ± 2%; if this target is not achieved and the Percent Within Limits for plastic air content falls below less than 55%, the pavement lot is classified as defective under Section 506.3(u)2, which triggers replacement actions because the low PWL indicates that the measured air content does not meet the required limits. Additionally, the mix must satisfy a Freeze‑Thaw Resistance test (ASTM C666/C666M, Procedure A or B) with a minimum durability factor of 80, and inadequate air voids can jeopardize performance under freeze‑thaw cycling. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 706–707)

For pervious concrete pavements, keeping the designed void structure of about 20 % ± 5 % and a hardened density that does not exceed 130 lb/ft³ helps preserve the air‑void network and reduces susceptibility to deterioration; exposure conditions such as projected air temperatures below 40°F during or within the first 24 hours after placement, or rainfall before final finishing and curing, can cause loss of mortar at the surface and increase the risk of void clogging. Using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode rather than pneumatic tire rollers and avoiding any finish that seals the surface voids further protect air content, while sealing would raise deterioration potential. Curing must begin within 20 minutes and be maintained for a minimum of 7 days with polyethylene sheeting to retain the intended void system. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421)

Material interactions and compatibility

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, adding synthetic or steel macrofibers changes the mix’s entrained‑air volume, so designers must modify the air‑entraining admixture during trial batching to achieve the required air content and fresh‑property specifications; this adjustment compensates for the fibers’ influence on workability and slump while preserving durability goals. For cement concrete, air content is obtained by adding a solution of an air‑entraining admixture, and the quantity needed to maintain the plastic‑state air level depends on aggregate type: stone or gravel aggregates are evaluated with AASHTO T 152 while slag or lightweight coarse aggregates use AASHTO T 196, with no aggregate correction factor applied. The design calls for a plastic‑state air content of 6.0% for ordinary cement concrete and 7.0% for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes; the hardened concrete must then have total air between 3.5% and 7.5%, or between 4.5% and 8.5% for those specific mixes. Accordingly, air content must be managed in relation to both the aggregate characteristics and any macrofibers incorporated into the mix, requiring dosage adjustments of the air‑entraining admixture as indicated by the respective specifications. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

The only compatibility risk noted for air‑content concrete in the specification concerns potentially reactive aggregates; such aggregates may be used only if their 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10% and 0.30% and an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend can reduce the resulting expansion to less than 0.1%, while any aggregate showing more than 0.30% expansion must be rejected, and blended hydraulic cements are limited to a maximum mortar expansion of 0.02% at 14 days and 0.06% at 8 weeks. No compatibility issues related to sulfates, organics, clays, contaminants, dissimilar materials, corrosion, shrinkage restraint, or chemical attack are addressed in the source. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466)

Influence of production and placement

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays (Pavement Policy Manual, section 10.10) macrofibers may change the entrained‑air volume, so the mix designer must adjust the air‑entraining admixture during trial batches to obtain the required air content; the source provides no information on how placement, compaction, finishing, or curing practices influence air content [1]. Consistent air content in cement concrete (SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE) depends on strict mixing controls; the plant must test air content of individual mixed concrete samples taken approximately at the beginning, the midpoint, and the end of each batch, and if the air content varies by more than 1.5% the mixer or agitator must be discontinued until the condition is corrected [2]. Mixing times are limited to not less than 50 seconds and not more than 90 seconds for normal‑strength concrete, and not less than 70 seconds for HES concrete, while truck‑drum mixing must stay within 70 to 125 revolutions at 6–18 rpm and never exceed 300 revolutions [2]. Temperature after mixing must remain between 50°F and 90°F for general concrete (and between 50°F and 80°F for bridge deck concrete), and the mixed concrete must be delivered and discharged within 1½ hours, with at least 20 revolutions of agitation immediately before placement to preserve the intended air‑void structure [2]. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (SECTION 506) a lot is deemed defective when the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content is less than 55%, indicating that inadequate control of air content during mixing and placement compromises durability [2]. Surface defects from rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, or honeycombing are also listed as causes of defectiveness, showing that poor curing and finishing can degrade the pavement’s performance even if other parameters meet specifications [2]. For pervious concrete (SECTION 520) mixing must be done with truck mixers operating at the manufacturer‑designated speed for a minimum of 70 to 100 revolutions, and the concrete must be placed within 90 minutes after the final introduction of any mix water so that the designed void structure is preserved [2]. Placement requires the subgrade or subbase to be saturated but free of standing water and the concrete to be deposited uniformly without segregation [2]. Compaction is limited to smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode with one to four passes, and pneumatic tire rollers must not be used so that surface voids are not sealed [2]. The final surface finish may use a maximum 2 ton roller or steel troweling only provided the surface voids remain open and the average density requirement and permeability values are met, thereby supporting the target void content of 20% ± 5% [2]. Curing must begin within 20 minutes of discharge; the pavement surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting, and that cover must be maintained for a minimum of 7 days to protect moisture and prevent loss of the intended air‑void network [2]. In long‑life concrete pavements (SECTION 530) the mixing stage is limited to plant‑mixed concrete with daily aggregate gradation checks so that the blend stays within prescribed limits before placement, and any needed adjustments trigger a new mix design [2]. During placement a concrete temperature control plan must be activated when concrete temperatures exceed 80°F at the point of placement, and the quality‑control plan requires sampling the first truck in each lot for plastic air, rejecting trucks that do not meet the requirement of 7.0% ± 1.5% until three consecutive passes are obtained, thereby directly controlling the final air‑void content [2]. The curing plan must include methods to cure and protect joints during the curing period to prevent moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints during and following joint sawing, which helps preserve the intended air‑void structure through the early hardening period [2].

In the cement‑concrete provisions of Section 704.1(d) any departure from the prescribed air‑meter testing regime strongly influences long‑term air‑content performance; the specification requires that air meters be calibrated no more than two weeks before concrete placement and recalibrated in the presence of the Inspector every two weeks, and using uncalibrated or malfunctioning meters can produce inaccurate measurements. Tests must follow AASHTO T 152 (or T 196 for certain mixes) without applying an aggregate correction factor, and if the measured air content differs by more than 1.0 % between acceptance, verification or QA samples the Representative will immediately review the testing procedures, equipment and personnel. The construction practice that most strongly influences long‑term air content is the control of the air‑entraining admixture dosage; the plastic‑state air content must be kept within a tolerance of ± 1.5 % during placement, otherwise the hardened concrete can fall outside the required total air‑content limits of 3.5 % to 7.5 % inclusive for standard mixes or 4.5 % to 8.5 % inclusive for AAAP and related pavings, which is treated as a defect affecting durability. In the plain‑or‑reinforced concrete pavement provisions of Section 506.3(u) lack of uniformity in measured plastic air content across sublots directly degrades durability because sublot acceptance test results that fall outside specification limits are included in the determination of the percentage within limits (PWL), and the Representative will only accept the concrete for air‑content performance when the PWL reaches at least 55 %. During plant or truck mixing, Section 704.2 requires testing air content of samples taken approximately at the beginning, midpoint and end of each batch, and if the air content varies by more than 1.5 % the mixer or agitator must be stopped until the condition is corrected, because such variation directly alters the volume of air voids that govern long‑term durability. For long‑life concrete pavement, Section 530.3(u) specifies that any truck whose plastic‑air test result falls outside the allowable limits must be rejected and not placed, and loads discarded for low plastic air content trigger retesting of subsequent trucks as specified in Section 704.1(d)4.a., so acceptance sampling performed before placement directly controls whether the pavement achieves the intended durability. Both Sections 530.3(s) and 506.3 require that when measured entrained air content in hardened concrete falls below 3.5 % or rises above 8.5 %, a written evaluation of the problem and proposed solution must be submitted within five days; failure to act can lead to a stop‑work order, and the District Executive may require paving operations to be halted until an acceptable solution is agreed upon. In the Superpave plant‑mixed asphalt provisions of Section 413.2, exceeding the allowable apparent moisture content of 0.5 % or allowing the theoretical maximum specific gravity to shift by 0.030 or more from its prior value can alter calculated air voids and degrade long‑term performance; similarly, any asphalt‑content or gradation result that falls outside the tolerances set in Table A changes mixture density and VMA, and when volumetric analysis of compacted specimens shows air‑void values that do not meet the Table B limits, corrective actions and possible suspension of production are required to protect durability. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements under Section 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, a lot is deemed defective when the Percent Within Limits for plastic air content is less than 55 %, when the average pavement depth is less than the design thickness (D) minus 1/2 inch, or when surface problems such as rain damage, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking or honeycombing are present; these conditions can alter the void structure and impair the intended air‑void system during freeze–thaw cycles. For pervious concrete pavement systems under Section 520—Pervious Concrete Pavement System, long‑term performance of the air‑void system depends on achieving a void structure of 20 % ± 5 %, avoiding finishing operations that seal surface voids, prohibiting pneumatic tire rollers, limiting the final roller to a maximum 2‑ton unit, permitting steel troweling only when surface voids remain open, and requiring saw‑cut joints to be vacuumed free of fines, because failure to meet these requirements can reduce infiltration below the required minimum average rate of 200 inches per hour and degrade air‑content durability. For long‑life concrete pavements under Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, variations in plastic‑air content are controlled by a quality‑control plan that samples the first truck in each lot for plastic air, with a requirement of 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 %; non‑conforming trucks are excluded and subsequent trucks are tested until three consecutive passing results are obtained, otherwise excess or insufficient air would compromise long‑term air‑content performance. Selection, specification, and improvement

In Section 704.1(c) the design of standard cement concrete calls for a plastic‑state air content of 6.0 % and, after hardening, the total air must lie between 3.5 % and 7.5 % inclusive. AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes are designed for a plastic‑state air content of 7.0 % with a tolerance of ± 1.5 %, and their hardened total air must be between 4.5 % and 8.5 % inclusive, together with the specified spacing factor and specific surface requirements. Section 704 requires that each sublot be tested for plastic‑air content before placement; any truck whose test falls outside the limits is rejected and all subsequent concrete from that truck must be discarded. For Class AA Slip‑Form Paving and AA Accelerated Patching concrete (Section 545) the design air content shall be 7.0 % with a ± 1.5 % tolerance measured at the time and point of placement per Section 704.1(c)3. Under the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications (Section 530.3(s)) the hardened entrained air content must remain between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; values outside this interval trigger a written evaluation and corrective action within five days, and failure to act may permit the District Executive to halt paving. The plain or reinforced cement concrete pavement specification (Section 506.3) mirrors this range, requiring hardened entrained air not below 3.5 % nor above 8.5 %, with the same reporting and possible stoppage provisions. Acceptance of each lot is performed on a sublot basis according to Sections 105 and 106; the Representative will accept concrete when the 28‑day compressive strength is adequate and, for air content, when the percentage of material within specification limits reaches at least 55 %, while Class AA air‑content quality indices are calculated using a 5.0 % lower reference and an 8.5 % upper reference. Payment for concrete pavement incorporates air‑content performance through a characteristic percentage (Pa) obtained from Table B and applied in the multiple‑characteristic formula of Section 506.4(a)3. For Superpave thin‑asphalt overlays (Section 412.2) the specified air voids must satisfy the tolerances listed in Table C; air voids are computed from bulk specific gravity of compacted specimens per AASHTO T 312 and evaluated at design density according to AASHTO R 35 and Bulletin 27, with any result outside Table C prompting corrective action before production continues. In self‑consolidating concrete (SCC) mixtures the specification designates a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 % as the primary criterion for selecting the mix’s air content; traditional non‑self‑consolidating mixes are not assigned an explicit target and their air content is governed by other performance requirements. Where air‑content testing is mandated, samples taken at the beginning, midpoint, and end of each batch serve as a selection criterion because any variation greater than 1.5 % triggers a stop‑use order for the mixer or agitator until the condition is corrected. In rigid‑pavement specifications air content is evaluated through the Percent Within Limits (PWL) for plastic air content, and a lot is deemed defective when that PWL falls below 55 %; consequently the specification must ensure the measured PWL meets or exceeds this threshold. For pervious concrete pavement systems the acceptable air (void) content is controlled by requiring a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % as measured by ASTM C1754 on each test section, and this void requirement together with an infiltration performance that must exceed 200 inches per hour constitutes the primary criterion for specifying the mix’s air content. In flowable backfill selection of air content is governed by the requirement to use an approved air‑entraining admixture from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, and the mix design must include measured air‑content results when applicable; the mix design, together with density and strength data, must be submitted to the Representative at least three weeks before construction to verify any specified air content through testing. For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement the quality‑control plan specifies that plastic air content shall be controlled by sampling the first truck in each lot, and only trucks whose measured plastic air falls within 7.0 % ± 1.5 % are permitted for placement; any truck that fails this requirement must be retested until three consecutive passing test results are obtained. In stone matrix asphalt (SMA) mixtures the specification of air content is governed by the requirement that the mixture’s volumetric properties—including calculated air voids (Va) and Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA)—must fall within the tolerances set out in Table E of Section 419.1, using bulk specific gravity determined per AASHTO T 312 and the design density (Ndesign) defined by AASHTO R 35; compliance is demonstrated through both single‑sample and multiple‑sample averages. The air‑content of an SMA mix is further governed by the design air‑void target, such that a trial strip must produce volumetric specimens whose measured Air Voids equal the Ndesign (Va) value and whose maximum specific gravity also complies with limits; the demonstration requires placing a minimum of 100 tons, sampling loose mixes for asphalt content, gradation and draindown, and coring the pavement to check density before confirming the air‑void result, and if any measured parameter falls outside the specification tolerances the mix must be re‑demonstrated. Because macrofibers can affect the entrapped air in a concrete overlay, designers should treat air content as a controllable fresh‑property by adjusting the dosage of an air‑entraining admixture during fiber‑reinforced concrete trial mixes, and such trial batches are required to verify that the mixture satisfies all fresh‑property limits, including air content. Water‑reducing admixtures may be added to offset slump loss caused by fibers, helping maintain workability and finishability while allowing the targeted air‑entraining dosage to be achieved. For standard cement concrete the mix shall incorporate a solution of an air‑entraining admixture, with the dosage calibrated according to AASHTO T 152 (or T 196 for slag or lightweight aggregate) so that the plastic concrete contains 6.0 % air and the required air content must be maintained within a tolerance of ± 1.5 % during placement. For AAAP, AA Slip Form and AA Fixed Form mixes the target is 7.0 % air with the same tolerance. Supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash or slag may be used provided that at least 400 lb per cubic yard of Portland cement remains in the mix, slag cement does not exceed 30 % of the total cementitious material and the total cementitious content does not exceed the limits in Table A. Approved warm‑mix asphalt technologies, including manufacturer‑specified additives, modifiers or processes, and compatible heat‑stable amine‑based anti‑strip liquids may be blended at the dosage rates given by the manufacturers to adjust mixture viscosity and improve compaction consistency. To keep air content within specification the contractor shall develop a reviewed quality‑control plan that defines testing frequencies and corrective‑action points, assign a trained concrete technician and a PennDOT‑certified field‑testing technician, calibrate all air meters no later than two weeks before placement and recalibrate them in the inspector’s presence every two weeks, and measure fresh‑concrete slump, air, density (for AAAP LW and AA LW) and temperature on the first three consecutive trucks; after material control is established testing may be reduced to at least one test per 50 cubic yards or per 30 minutes whichever is greater, but any failure shall trigger an increase to testing each truck until control is regained. Lot‑by‑lot quality control shall be applied by having the Representative locate plastic‑concrete samples according to PTM No. 1, conduct one compressive‑strength test and one AASHTO‑specified air‑content test for each sublot, and accept concrete only when the percentage of material within limits is 55 % or greater; compliance shall also be monitored by calculating lower quality indices from the average sublot results. For long‑life concrete pavement each sublot must have a plastic‑air‑content test performed before placement and any truck whose result falls outside the specified limits shall not be placed and all failing concrete shall be discarded; a testing station shall be provided within one mile of the final placement location unless an alternate is approved, and compressive‑strength cylinders and plastic‑air specimens shall be taken from each sublot in accordance with PTM procedures. Hardened entrained air shall be evaluated on information cores and if the measured air content is below 3.5 % or above 8.5 % a written evaluation of the problem and a proposed solution must be submitted to the Department within five days; core‑hole repair may be performed using either the same mixture used in construction or a premixed non‑shrink grout as an alternative material. Moisture control shall be strict: any coarse aggregate with water absorption greater than 2.0 % shall trigger additional sampling, the produced mixture shall maintain an apparent moisture content not to exceed 0.5 %, and for daily production of each JMF larger than 150 tons the asphalt content, gradation, theoretical maximum specific gravity and a volumetric analysis of compacted specimens shall be determined at least once each day so that air voids meet the Table B tolerances. The water‑cement ratio shall be verified in the field using batch tickets for cementitious content determination and AASHTO T 318 for water‑content determination to help control mix consistency. Incorporating a freeze‑thaw durability test (ASTM C666) with a minimum durability factor of 80 provides an additional quality check that confirms the entrained‑air system can resist freezing cycles, and replacing a small portion of fine aggregate with Aggregate Mineral Filler may help optimize the mix and promote consistent air‑void distribution. For plant‑ and truck‑mixed cement concrete the design should include an air‑entraining admixture together with any required water reducers or retarders, and the plant must perform air‑meter tests on samples taken at the beginning, midpoint and end of each batch; if the measured air content varies by more than 1.5 % the mixer or agitator must be stopped until corrected. Maintaining concrete temperature after mixing between 50 °F and 90 °F for general concrete and using retarding admixtures in hot weather further helps preserve uniform entrained air. In pervious asphalt mixtures designers may use polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber in a PG‑64 binder and limit recycled asphalt pavement to no more than ten percent of the mixture; when moisture susceptibility is a concern an anti‑strip additive blended with the cement at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt—or hydrated lime—can be incorporated, and cellulose fibers may be added as a stabilizer in a dosage between 0.2 % and 0.4 % of the total mix. The mixture design must determine air voids using any method listed in Table B for bulk specific gravity, and a quality‑control plan that verifies asphalt content, gradation and air‑void measurements is required before production. For pervious concrete pavement systems two test sections are produced and checked for a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % using ASTM C1754 and for an average infiltration rate that must exceed 200 inches per hour as measured by ASTM C1701; consistent quality is further ensured by limiting hardened density to within ±5 lb/ft³ of the test‑section average and not exceeding 130 lb/ft³, testing unit weight on every truck in accordance with ASTM C1688, and coring and evaluating void content after placement. Construction practices that protect the air voids include wetting the subgrade to saturation without standing water, using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes, avoiding placement on frozen material, and initiating curing within 20 minutes with a polyethylene sheet that remains in place for at least seven days. For flowable backfill a dedicated air‑entraining admixture from a Bulletin 15 manufacturer shall be used; when Type IP cement is used the fly ash quantity should be adjusted and when Type IS cement is used the slag cement amount should be adjusted. Maintaining material limits—fly ash with a maximum loss on ignition of 16 % and aggregates limited to a 20 % loss in the soundness test—helps maintain the intended void structure for reliable air entrainment, and submitting a mix design that includes density, strength and air‑content testing at least three weeks before construction and obtaining certification provides the required quality control. Under long‑life concrete pavement construction requirements a quality‑control plan must mandate sampling the first truck in each lot for plastic air and reject any truck that does not meet the requirement of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %; trucks that fail are retested until three consecutive passing tests are obtained. Daily aggregate gradations shall be verified by sieve analysis and adjusted as needed to stay within the limits defined in PTM No. 529, and a concrete temperature control plan must be included to keep temperatures from exceeding 80 °F at placement, both of which help maintain consistent air content. Performance evaluation and implications

Air content in fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays is evaluated through laboratory trial batches, where the air‑entraining admixture may be adjusted and the resulting mix is checked against fresh‑property specifications; trial batches are always recommended to confirm that the FRC mixture can meet all fresh property specifications. [1] For cement concrete, fresh air content at placement is measured on each truck load with calibrated air meters conforming to AASHTO T 152 or T 196 (without an aggregate correction factor); tests are required at least once per 50 cubic yards, the meters must be calibrated in the presence of the Inspector no more than two weeks before placement and recalibrated every two weeks, primary and backup meters are used and any result that differs by more than 1.0 % prompts a review of procedures. [2] Laboratory determination of air content on fresh concrete also follows AASHTO T152 for stone‑and‑gravel mixes or AASHTO T196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregates, and the measured value must remain within ± 1.5 % of the target during placement. [2] When hardened concrete is suspected of deficiency, an air‑void system analysis per ASTM C457 is performed to verify spacing factor, specific surface and to confirm that total air content falls between 3.5 % and 7.5 % inclusive (or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % for AAAP mixes). [2] Field sampling of each sublot provides additional evidence: one concrete sample per sublot is taken, molded into acceptance cylinders at the designated location and tested in the laboratory with AASHTO T152 or T196; the result is combined with the sublot plastic‑air‑content test to compute a Performance Weighted Level (PWL) and the Representative accepts the concrete when the PWL is 55 % or greater. [2] Long‑term field evidence for entrained air in both LLCP and other cement concrete pavements is obtained by extracting an information core from the first sublot and every tenth sublot, submitting those cores immediately to the Laboratory Testing Services for evaluation of entrained air content according to PTM No. 623, and comparing the measured value with limits of 3.5 % minimum and 8.5 % maximum; any result outside that range requires a written evaluation and proposed corrective action within five days. [2] The air content of precast concrete is verified by laboratory testing of fresh mixes in which plastic concrete slump, air and temperature are measured at the frequencies prescribed in Section 704 and Publication 145; each batch must also undergo an air‑meter test that confirms a plastic air content of 7 % ± 2 % before production. Air content is a controlled parameter in pavement concrete mix design. Regular cement concrete must be designed for an air content of 6.0 % in the plastic state, while AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes must be designed for an air content of 7.0 % in the plastic state, and this level must be maintained within a tolerance of ± 1.5 % during placement by adjusting the dosage of an air‑entraining admixture as prescribed by AASHTO test methods. After hardening, the concrete’s total air content must fall between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive, to satisfy the specified air void system; for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes the acceptable hardened‑state range is 4.5 % to 8.5 % inclusive, and meeting these limits ensures compliance with the design criteria that govern durability and performance of the pavement. In Long‑Life Concrete Pavement construction, the first truck in each lot must be sampled for plastic air and trucks that do not meet the requirement of 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 % will not be incorporated; any truck whose plastic‑air result falls outside specification limits will not be placed, and all sampling and testing must take place before the concrete in the truck being tested is placed on grade. If a truck is discarded because of low plastic air content, consecutive trucks must be tested as specified, and each truck after any failing plastic‑air test will be tested until three consecutive passing test results are obtained. A testing station must be provided within 1 mile of the final concrete placement location unless an alternate location is approved by the Representative. During construction, cores are taken from each sublot and submitted for an entrained‑air‑content evaluation; the entrained air content of each core must fall between 3.5 % and 8.5 %. If the entrained air content is below 3.5 % or above 8.5 %, a written evaluation of the problem and proposed solution must be provided to the Department within five days, and if positive action is not taken to address hardened entrained air results below 3.5 % or above 8.5 % within five days, the District Executive may require that paving operations be stopped until an acceptable solution is agreed upon. The lot is defective if the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content is less than 55 %, which triggers mandatory removal and replacement of the affected pavement segment, underscoring that adequate air content is required to avoid defect classification and associated maintenance. In pervious concrete pavement the void structure is specified as 20 % ± 5 %, a condition that directly controls the mixture’s permeability and therefore its ability to meet the required infiltration performance. The infiltration test performed on test sections according to ASTM C1701 must exceed 200 inches per hour, a result that depends on maintaining both the prescribed void content and the hardened density tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³ and not to exceed 130 lb/ft³. If core testing shows void content outside the allowed ± 5 % of the average test‑section voids, the placement is rejected and the concrete may have to be removed or replaced, highlighting that proper control of air content is essential for durable, functional pervious concrete.
