Cracking

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Cracking is a topic under Distress.
cracking
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

Cracking is a pavement distress that appears as fissures or breakage on the surface or within the structural layers of both asphalt and concrete pavements, described in concrete as slab fracture that produces free‑moving pieces at joints, cracks and punchouts [1]. It develops when tensile stresses exceed material capacity—such as from improper slabjacking (Section 4.6), low‑temperature effects, moisture intrusion with freeze‑thaw cycling, plastic or drying shrinkage, or inadequate subbase material—and propagates upward as reflective cracking under new overlays or laterally as alligator‑type cracking caused by age oxidation [1][2]. For performance monitoring the extent of cracking is quantified as the percentage of pavement surface that is fissured, a metric used in FHWA condition ratings and associated with differential slab movement, faulting and reduced service life [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Cracking” also include the following subtopics: Longitudinal Crack and Transverse Crack.

Longitudinal Crack and Transverse Crack are specific orientations of the fissures that constitute cracking in concrete pavements. [1][2] Longitudinal Crack runs parallel to the centerline within a traffic lane or at joint interfaces, while Transverse Crack appears perpendicular to the roadway centerline where subgrade saturation reduces load support. [1][2]

Description and identification

Cracking is a pavement distress that manifests as fissures or breakage either on the surface or within the structural layers of the pavement. In concrete pavements it is described as slab fracture that renders the pavement too deteriorated for economical repair; the distress appears as fissures and breakage at existing joints, cracks and punchouts that produce free‑moving slab pieces. When an asphalt overlay is placed over a previously cracked roadway, those underlying fissures can propagate upward, producing reflective cracking, while age‑related oxidation may generate an alligator pattern of interconnected surface cracks. For performance monitoring the presence of such fissures on the pavement surface is quantified by measuring the percentage of the surface that is cracked, a metric that contributes to the good‑fair‑poor condition rating used in FHWA reporting. Annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys also record cracking as fissures or breaks observable on the surface and may include premature mid‑slab cracking that can be caused by inadequate subbase material. Routine maintenance addresses these manifestations: cyclic crack sealing of asphalt surfaces should be performed every 3 to 5 years, and joint sealing of concrete pavements should be done every 5 years. In latex‑modified mortar or concrete wearing surfaces cracking manifests as fissures or surface tears that become visible on the overlay; these non‑structural cracks—such as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related movement, or finishing‑induced tears—are identified by measuring their width, depth and length relative to permanent reference points, and when a crack exceeds a depth greater than 1/4 inch and has a width between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch it is classified as a repairable defect. In polyester polymer concrete overlays cracking is any fissure or surface tear that appears on the finished wearing course and may extend into the slab; non‑structural cracks are treated when the visible surface crack or tear exceeds a depth of 1/8 inch and falls between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch in width. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements cracking appears as fissures or breaks—often described as cracks or spalls—that can be observed on the surface as longitudinal cracks or develop within a slab, and a longitudinal crack occurring within a lane triggers removal and replacement of the affected slabs. In bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements cracking refers to fissures that open in the existing asphalt surface, appearing as visible gaps larger than three‑quarters of an inch; such cracks must be cleaned and filled before the overlay is placed. When existing concrete slabs are fractured for rehabilitation cracking denotes the intentional full‑depth fracture that must run continuously across the full width of the slab and remain visible on the top surface. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs cracks are measured by their width and depth relative to the slab thickness D; cracks wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than D/3 require complete slab replacement, cracks wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than D/3 are repaired according to ACI 224R, and cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are sealed with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate sealant. On asphalt‑concrete curbs cracking is a visible distress that may appear on the surface after placement; if the crack cannot be satisfactorily repaired, the curb must be removed and replaced. In the Pavement Policy Manual, cracking is first recognized by examining its visual pattern and the surrounding context; reflective cracking is noted when a new asphalt overlay is placed over an existing cracked roadway, while alligator‑type cracking that extends across the entire surface of the pavement and is not confined to wheel paths indicates age oxidation rather than a structural deficiency. The manual further states that these visual distinctions guide the selection of interlayer systems described in Section F for treating those specific crack forms. For performance measurement, cracking is quantified by measuring the proportion of the pavement surface that exhibits fissures and reporting this as a cracking percentage, which is treated separately from other distress components such as International Roughness Index, rut depth, and faulting elevation differences. Under the FHWA performance framework a segment is rated poor when two or more of its three distress components—including cracking—receive a poor rating, and the overall interstate network must keep poor‑condition lane‑miles below 5 percent.

In latex‑modified mortar or concrete wearing surfaces (Section 1042.3 Construction), any fissure or surface tear is taken as the initial indication of cracking; the Representative then measures the width, depth and length of each crack, locates its ends with respect to permanent reference points, and may require coring if a mechanical probe cannot accurately determine depth. The specification distinguishes non‑structural cracks—such as those caused by plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature effects or finishing/texturing tears—from structural cracks, and requires repair at no additional cost only for cracks whose depth exceeds ¼ inch and whose width lies between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch. In polyester polymer concrete overlays (Section 1047.3 Construction), cracking is likewise first identified by visual observation; when depth cannot be gauged a core is submitted for laboratory analysis, and non‑structural cracks that exceed a depth greater than ⅛ inch and have a width between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch are repaired with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate penetrating crack sealer at no additional cost. For unbonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548.3 Construction), cracking is identified by the presence of fissures that occur outside the intended saw‑cut joints; such panels are considered defective, whereas regular joint lines created by sawing are intentional, and if a crack occurs at or within 1.5 feet of a planned joint before or during sawing the specification requires omitting the saw cut and addressing the condition according to Section 590. In bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540.3 Construction), any fissure wider than three‑quarters inch is classified as a crack that must be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or other Department‑approved material before the overlay is placed, and the same rule for omitting sawing applies when a crack is found at or within 1.5 feet of a planned joint location. In slab‑fracturing work on existing concrete pavements (Section 522.3 Construction), cracking is identified when continuous full‑depth fissures are plainly visible on the top surface, and wetting the pavement with water may be used to assist visual inspection; this type of cracking is distinguished from other distresses by prohibiting equipment that would cause extensive surface spalling, punched holes or shattering, which indicate a different damage mode. The slab‑stabilization specifications (Section 679.4 Measurement and Payment) identify radial cracking by its linear extent, reducing the cement‑grout pay item by one bag for each five linear feet of radial cracking measured, thereby separating radial cracking from other incidental items such as drilled holes or grout material. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529.3 Construction), cracks are identified by measuring fissure width and depth relative to the slab thickness D; a crack wider than 0.007 inch and extending deeper than one‑third of the slab depth (greater than D/3) triggers a full‑slab replacement at no extra cost, while a crack wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than D/3 requires repair in accordance with the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R), and cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate—distinguishing these cracking conditions from spalls, which are addressed separately. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 506.3 Construction), cracking is listed as a surface defect that places the lot in the defective category when it cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative; the defect criteria enumerate cracking separately from rain damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, final‑finish problems and honeycombing. For asphalt‑concrete curbs (Section 636.3 Construction), cracking is recognized by visual signs of fissures or breaks on the completed surface, while sagging (vertical deformation) and distortion (misalignment) are listed separately, allowing the presence of cracking to be distinguished from those other conditions. In long‑life concrete pavements (Section 530.3 Construction), cracking is identified as a surface defect that renders a lot defective when present on the finished pavement and not repairable to the Representative’s satisfaction; the specification lists cracking alongside rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, finish problems and honeycombing, providing a separate classification that distinguishes it from those similar distresses. In shotcrete (Section 1043.3 Construction), the Representative visually inspects the hardened surface for evidence of excessive plastic or drying‑shrinkage cracking; such cracks are recorded separately from tears, featheredging or sloughs, and to differentiate cracking from delamination the Representative sounds the material and, if delaminations are suspected, conducts bond pull‑off tests in accordance with ASTM C 900. In asphalt‑concrete pavements covered by the 60‑month warranty (Section 496.3 Construction), cracking is identified through automated distress surveys performed in accordance with Publication 336; each fissure is measured and classified by severity, and a medium‑severity crack is treated as a deviation affecting six inches on either side of the crack for its entire length, thereby distinguishing it from other distresses such as flushing or potholes that are evaluated by manual surveys. Where it occurs

In section 4.6 Slabjacking, cracking is reported on Portland cement concrete pavement slabs when slabjacking is performed improperly; the procedure can introduce additional damage including cracking, tilting and faulting, especially if grout extrudes from joints, cracks or the pavement edge before the target elevation is reached. [1] Section 4.9 Rehabilitation treats cracking on existing concrete pavements—specifically Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) and Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP)—when a flexible asphalt overlay is to be placed; the cracking/breaking and seating process reduces the pavement to 12‑ to 24‑inch pieces, rolls them into the base or subbase, thereby reducing differential movement at cracks, joints and punchouts and lessening reflective cracking through the new overlay, and these operations are not required for unbonded concrete overlays. [1] In section 5.2 Seal coats, slurry seals, and surface treatments, minor cracking is identified as a deficiency that can be corrected with an asphalt surface treatment or a scratch‑course/seal‑coat combination, indicating occurrence on asphalt pavements where such treatments are applied. [1] Section 5.12 Overlays reports reflective cracking in new asphalt overlays placed over already cracked roadways and states that alligator cracking may appear across the surface of existing pavements regardless of material; interlayer systems are intended to mitigate such cracking. [1] In section 10.6 Bonded PCC Overlay of PCC Pavement, cracking is encountered on existing Portland cement concrete pavements considered for a bonded PCC overlay, and bonded overlays should only be used where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking and/or faulting because significant distress would be reflected through the overlay if not repaired. [1] In the latex‑modified mortar or concrete wearing surface specifications, cracking is observed in overlays that exhibit nonstructural cracks—plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related cracks, or surface tears from finishing—and the specification requires investigation of length, width, depth and location, and mandates repair with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate (HMWM) penetrating crack filler/sealer for cracks deeper than ¼ inch and between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch in width. Causes and mechanisms

Improper slabjacking is identified as a construction‑related deficiency that may cause additional damage to the slab such as cracking, tilting, or faulting, and the guidance recommends performing slab lifting in increments to minimize slab stresses and avoid cracking. [1] Low‑temperature effects are noted as a climate‑related cause of fatigue and low‑temperature cracking. [1] The use of an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is said to reduce oxidation and weathering, thereby helping the pavement remain resilient to such cracking. [1] Material aging, specifically age oxidation, is linked to alligator‑type cracking that extends across the entire surface. [1] Moisture intrusion into a pavement’s structural section and freeze‑thaw cycling are identified as major contributors to crack propagation. [1] Interlayers are intended to address cracking that is not associated with a lack of structure, indicating that structural or design deficiencies are not emphasized in this context. [1] Premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material, reflecting a materials or construction deficiency. [1] Cracking is consistently linked to material‑related phenomena such as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑induced movements, and alkali‑silica reaction in reactive aggregates. [2] Shrinkage that develops when chemically stabilized material attains an unconfined compressive strength greater than 900 psi may result in cracking. [2] Moisture exposure, for example rain affecting the surface before proper curing, is also cited as a cause of cracking. [2] Construction deficiencies including improper joint construction, improper finishing or curing procedures, and inadequate compaction of asphalt mixtures where roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern or size are not adjusted to eliminate cracking may lead to cracking if the mixture is not compacted correctly. [2] In the latex‑modified mortar overlay specification, non‑structural cracks that manifest as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related cracking or surface tears from finishing are described as defects in materials or workmanship. [2] The polyester polymer concrete overlay specification adds rain, improper finish, improper cure and honeycombing to the list of causes for non‑structural cracking. [2] The long‑life concrete pavement defective‑work clause similarly lists rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures and improper final finish as sources of cracking. [2] None of the documents examined includes a cause‑classification table for cracking; the Pavement Policy Manual discusses low‑temperature cracking as a climate factor but does not present it in tabular form, and the Pennsylvania specifications do not provide such a table. Cracking in concrete pavements develops when tensile stresses generated by external actions exceed the material’s capacity; during slabjacking, if the lift is not performed incrementally the imposed tensile stresses can surpass the slab’s capacity and create fissures, a situation that is further aggravated by lifting too quickly or without careful control and by high‑temperature conditions that cause slab expansion and place joints in compression [1]. In overlay applications, reflective cracking initiates when a new asphalt layer is placed over an existing cracked roadway and propagates along the underlying cracks, while alligator cracking may arise from age oxidation of the pavement; water intrusion into the structural section promotes crack propagation and freeze‑thaw cycles cause expansion and contraction of moisture within the pavement, with progression accelerated by the presence of widespread existing cracks, inadequate drainage that allows moisture infiltration, and harsh environments with repeated freezing and thawing [1]. Premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements can be caused by an inadequate subbase material that permits slab movement and joint opening, leading to fissure formation within the slab body [1]. In latex‑modified mortar wearing surfaces, cracking develops through plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related stresses, or surface tears introduced by finishing and texturing; cracks that exceed a depth greater than ¼ inch and have a width between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch must be repaired [2]. In polyester polymer concrete overlays, cracking also originates from plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related stresses, and surface tears caused by finishing and texturing; progression is evaluated by measuring each crack’s width from 0.007 inch to 0.016 inch and depth greater than ⅛ inch, and accelerated cracking occurs under adverse ambient or substrate temperature and moisture conditions, rain exposure, improper finish or cure, and honeycombing that impair bonding [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, cracking is tied to stress conditions that exceed the concrete’s capacity and may be premature when panel dimensions are larger than 18 times the overlay thickness or when the length‑to‑width ratio exceeds 1.5; a temperature change greater than 40 °F within any one‑hour period is considered defective, indicating rapid thermal swings accelerate crack formation [2]. In stream‑bed cement concrete pavements, shrinkage of the curing concrete creates tensile stresses once set; if contraction joints are not cut promptly, these stresses open fissures known as shrinkage cracks, and the specification directs that saw cuts be made as soon as possible after the concrete sets sufficiently, with contraction joints 3/16 inch wide and 2 inches deep to control where shrinkage stresses are relieved [2]. In cement‑concrete structures subject to alkali‑silica reaction, cracking develops when expansive alkali‑silica gel generates internal pressure that initiates microcracking which can coalesce into surface fissures, and progression is accelerated whenever aggregates classified as potentially reactive are combined with cement containing sufficient alkali and without adequate supplementary cementitious material or mitigation admixtures [2]. In concrete median barriers, cracking primarily results from shrinkage when contraction joints are not provided early enough; conditions that accelerate this distress include any factor that produces surface blemishes larger than ½ inch, which must be corrected within 30 linear feet [2]. In long‑life concrete pavement, surface defects caused by exposure to rain, improper joint construction, inadequate curing procedures, or an improper final finish initiate cracking and these conditions accelerate crack initiation and propagation, rendering the lot defective when such surface defects cannot be satisfactorily repaired [2]. In shotcrete applications, cracking originates from plastic shrinkage and later drying‑shrinkage as the material hardens; the tendency to crack is intensified when moisture is not retained during curing or when temperature requirements are not met, requiring water cure for seven days with air temperatures of at least 50 °F for the first four days and above 40 °F for the last three days, and any occurrence of curing temperature below 35 °F renders the work unsatisfactory [2]. In full‑depth reclaimed bases, shrinkage cracking develops when the chemically stabilized layer reaches an unconfined compressive strength greater than 900 psi; this excessive stiffness and associated volume change are triggered by over‑stabilization or curing practices that drive the material’s strength beyond the specified upper limit, placing the base at risk for shrinkage cracking [2].

Severity levels and rating

In the Pavement Policy Manual, micro‑surfacing project‑selection criteria address cracking only by requiring a “low severity cracking” surface condition and provide no further severity categories, numeric thresholds, counts or dimensional limits for that level; recommended treatments such as Type A crack sealing are predicated on the pavement meeting this low‑severity condition. Within Section 1042 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, non‑structural surface cracks in latex‑modified mortar or concrete wearing surfaces that are deeper than one‑quarter inch and whose width lies between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch must be repaired with a high molecular weight methacrylate penetrating crack filler/sealer at no additional cost to the Department. If such cracks or tears exceed a depth of one‑quarter inch and a width greater than 0.016 inch, they are classified as defective work and require removal and replacement of the wearing surface. In Section 1047 covering polyester polymer concrete overlays, non‑structural cracks or surface tears that are deeper than one‑eighth inch and whose width is between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch must be repaired using high molecular weight methacrylate penetrating crack sealer at no additional cost. Cracks or tears in the overlay that are greater than one‑quarter inch deep and wider than 0.016 inch trigger removal and replacement of the wearing surface as specified in Section 501.3(o). For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, cracks wider than 0.007 inch and with a depth greater than one‑third of the slab thickness (D) require full‑depth slab replacement at no additional cost to the Department. Cracks that are wider than 0.007 inch but have a depth less than one‑third of D must be repaired in accordance with the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R). Cracks that are 0.007 inch wide or less are sealed with high molecular weight methacrylate following the prescribed cleaning, mixing and sand‑blasting procedure. Under the sixty‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, cracking is classified only as low or medium severity; a low‑severity crack contributes to the trigger that after year 5, if thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more distresses with low severity, microsurfacing or an equivalent remedial treatment must be applied. A medium‑severity crack is counted as a deviation affecting six inches on either side of the crack for its entire length when determining whether combined deviations exceed twenty percent of the segment area, which would require full‑depth removal and replacement. Measurement, survey and extent

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, cracking is quantified by determining the percentage of the pavement surface that is cracked; this measurement consists of a surface‑area assessment expressed as a percent of the total pavement area [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for latex‑modified mortar or concrete wearing surfaces (Section 1042.3), when cracks or surface tears are observed the investigator must measure each crack’s width, length, and depth and locate the ends relative to permanent reference points; depth is normally obtained with a mechanical probe, and if the probe cannot determine depth accurately coring of the pavement is performed [2]. The same measurement protocol is required for polyester polymer concrete overlays (Section 1047.3(j)), where each crack’s width, length, and depth are recorded, end locations are referenced to permanent points, depth is normally taken with a mechanical probe, and when the probe is insufficient two cores are taken for laboratory analysis [2]. For slab‑stabilization work (Section 679.4), cracking is quantified solely by its linear extent; the contractor simply assesses the total linear footage of radial cracks and the contract provides a reduction of one bag of cement for each 5 linear feet of radial cracking as defined in Section 679.3(a)6, without prescribing a specific tool or test [2]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement (Section 496.3(c)), cracking is measured during performance surveys by identifying each crack and estimating its extent; the recorded quantity is the area affected, calculated from the crack length plus a standard lateral influence of six inches on either side for medium‑severity cracks, expressed as a percent of the segment’s surface area, and the measurement is carried out with an automated survey system (or manual survey when necessary) in accordance with the techniques, rating procedures, and extent‑estimation methods set forth in Publication 336 [2].

Effects on performance

In rehabilitation where a cracked concrete pavement is broken, the slab is reduced to 12‑ to 24‑inch pieces that are rolled into the base or subbase before an overlay; this provides a level of support greater than a conventional subbase layer and therefore adds structural capacity. The reduction in size of free‑moving slabs minimizes differential movement at cracks, joints and punchouts, which reduces the occurrence and severity of reflective cracking through the new overlay, thereby improving durability and extending service life. Conversely, when an existing concrete pavement exhibits a great extent of cracking or faulting, a bonded PCC overlay should be avoided because the underlying distress will be reflected up through the new slab if it is not repaired, compromising the composite structural capacity and shortening the service life of the overlay; consequently, significant cracking reduces durability of the rehabilitated section by allowing existing flaws to continue influencing performance. No ride‑quality or safety effects are noted for the described rehabilitation method. For chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed material, if compressive strength exceeds 900 psi, shrinkage cracking may develop; the specification requires rework, indicating that such cracking compromises structural integrity and durability of the base. When cracks are left untreated in concrete pavements, the slabs can become free‑moving, which leads to slab rocking, pumping and faulting and causes differential movement at existing cracks, joints and punchouts [1]. That differential movement then manifests as reflective cracking in any later overlay [1]. The progression may require more intensive rehabilitation such as cracking/breaking and seating, a process that reduces the pavement to 12‑to‑24‑inch pieces and rolls them into the base or subbase before an overlay, or even rubblizing if conditions are severe [1]. Untreated surface cracks also permit water to infiltrate the pavement’s structural section, which is identified as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw‑induced deterioration [1]. When an overlay is later placed over such cracked pavement, the existing fissures commonly generate reflective cracking in the new wearing surface, further reducing the effectiveness of the rehabilitation [1]. Bonded PCC overlays are designed to function as a single monolithic slab and should only be used where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if significant distress remains unrepaired it will be reflected up through the bonded overlay, causing the same cracks and related damage to appear in the new layer [1]. Under Section 1042—Latex Modified Mortar or Concrete Wearing Surface, any observed cracking or surface tears must first be investigated; when the cracks are classified as non‑structural and exceed a depth of 1/4 inch with a width between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch they may be repaired with HMWM penetrating crack filler at no additional cost [2]. If the cracks are deeper than 1/4 inch and wider than 0.016 inch they are deemed defective work and require removal and replacement of the wearing surface [2]. Under Section 506.3(t) for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, cracking that cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative classifies the lot as defective; a defective lot must be removed and replaced, with at least a minimum of 10 feet of pavement between transverse joints for reinforced cement concrete or an entire panel for plain cement concrete being replaced [2]. Section 530.3(t) for Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifies that cracking which cannot be satisfactorily repaired causes the lot to be classified as defective, and the specification then requires removal and replacement of at least an entire panel of LLCP pavement [2]. In Section 1043—Shotcrete, excessive plastic or drying‑shrinkage cracking left unrepaired may progress into additional defects such as tears, featheredging, sloughs and delaminations; these deficiencies are listed among the causes for shotcrete rejection, prompting remedial work while the material is still plastic and, if delamination is detected, bond pull‑off testing must be performed to verify adhesion before acceptance [2]. The 60‑month warranty in Section 496.3(c) treats untreated cracks as low‑severity distresses; when a year‑5 performance survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more such distresses, microsurfacing or another approved treatment is required, and if the combined deviations from all distress limits—including medium‑severity cracks considered to affect a zone six inches on each side of the crack for its full length—exceed 20 % of the segment’s surface area, removal and replacement of the entire segment is mandated [2].

Repairs and treatments

Slabjacking, described in Section 4.6, is used to fill voids beneath concrete slabs and raise them to the proper profile, thereby restoring structural integrity; it may be applied when correcting an extensive profile deficiency and is only practical when the slab is in compression rather than during high temperatures that cause expansion. The method must be carried out in incremental lifts to keep slab stresses low and avoid inducing new cracking, and dowel‑bar retrofits are normally paired with slabjacking to reestablish load transfer on settled slabs that have lost support, which can reduce faulting and associated cracking. Cracking/breaking and seating an existing concrete pavement, presented in Section 4.9, attempts to achieve a stable, uniform level of support by breaking the slab into 12‑to‑24 inch pieces and rolling them into the base; it is applied when the concrete pavement is distressed to such an extent that patching is no longer economical (Section E) and therefore addresses extensive cracking, joints and punchouts while reducing differential movement and reflective cracking in a subsequent overlay. Rubblizing, also in Section 4.9, reduces the concrete to 8‑ or 12‑inch pieces before rolling them into the base and is prescribed for badly distressed pavements—often with poor subgrade or moisture problems—and thus addresses severe cracking by providing a uniform support layer for an overlay. Seal coats, slurry seals, and other surface treatments listed in Section 5.2 are intended to correct minor cracking (along with minor rutting and loss of fine aggregate); they may be used on non‑Interstate roads but shall not be placed on any pavement in need of structural upgrading, and seal coats, slurry seals, and surface treatments must not be used on the Interstate system. Micro‑Surfacing Type A, described in Section 5.4, is the only treatment in that section that directly targets cracking; it will be used to seal cracks, fill voids, and shallow (less than 1/2 inch) ruts, and a roadway may be chosen for a micro‑surfacing project only when the surface exhibits low severity cracking. The Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course (UTWC) in Section 5.5 is applied to address low temperature cracking; by sealing the pavement it reduces oxidation and weathering of the surface, and the oxidation reduction will allow the pavement to remain resilient to fatigue and low temperature cracking, so the treatment applies when a surface requires protection from oxidation‑induced cracking. An asphalt paving interlayer—offered as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids—in Section 5.12 is specified to mitigate reflective cracking that occurs when a new overlay is placed over an existing cracked roadway and to prevent alligator‑type age‑oxidation cracking from propagating through the new wearing surface; it may be applied only when the cracking present is not associated with a lack of structural capacity, and selection should also consider moisture, climate, and traffic conditions as outlined in the interlayer guidance. A bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlay, covered in Section 10.6, addresses cracking in an existing concrete pavement by being applied only where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if there is a significant amount of distress it must be repaired before the overlay because otherwise the distress will propagate through the new slab. Routine‑maintenance crack sealing for asphalt‑surfaced pavements and joint sealing for concrete pavements are listed in Section 13.7; crack sealing should be done every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing every 5 years, and both are classified as actions intended to repair or retard existing distress. Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual recommends that incremental slabjacking lifts be used so that slab stresses are limited and cracking is avoided, but it cautions that such lifts shall not be attempted when pavement temperature is high because thermal expansion places joints in compression (section 4.6 Slabjack) [1]. It also specifies bonded jointed plain‑concrete‑pavement (JPCP) overlays as a design measure that adds structural capacity and eliminates surface distress, including cracking, for pavements that are in good to fair condition with a stable subgrade and functional drainage (section 4.9 C 1) [1]. When an existing concrete pavement is so distressed that patching is uneconomic, the manual prescribes a cracking‑or‑breaking technique that reduces slabs to 12‑24 inch pieces, thereby limiting differential movement at cracks, joints and punchouts and reducing the occurrence and severity of reflective cracking in the subsequent overlay (section 4.9 E) [1]. For asphalt overlays placed over concrete, saw‑cutting and sealing all underlying transverse and patch joints is required as a preparation that mitigates reflective cracking through the new layer (section 4.9 D) [1]. Seal coats, slurry seals and other surface treatments are identified as measures that can mitigate minor cracking, but they must not be used on the Interstate system and shall only be applied where the pavement does not require structural upgrading; eligibility is decided according to Table 5.1 (section 5.2) [1]. Micro‑surfacing projects are limited to pavements exhibiting low‑severity cracking; a double application is recommended unless the road surface is in excellent condition, and on Interstate routes a double application of Type A SRL‑E is advised even when the surface is excellent, with the mix sealing cracks, filling voids and shallow ruts less than ½ inch (section 5.4) [1]. An ultra‑thin bonded wearing course—comprised of a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane overlaid with a thin hot‑mix asphalt concrete in one pass—will seal the pavement, reduce oxidation and weathering, and thereby keep the surface resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking; this measure applies only when the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is placed as described (section 5.5) [1]. Asphalt paving interlayers such as a geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid or composite reinforcing grid are prescribed to mitigate reflective cracking by retarding crack reflection and providing tensile reinforcement; they are intended for overlays over pavements that exhibit alligator cracking caused by age oxidation rather than structural deficiency, and they also act as a moisture barrier when surface or subsurface drainage is good or will be upgraded (section 5.12) [1]. A bonded PCC overlay forms a monolithic slab that prevents upward transmission of cracks; it should be used only where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking and/or faulting, because significant distress would otherwise be reflected through the overlay (section 10.6) [1]. Routine maintenance design measures include crack sealing of asphalt‑surfaced pavements on a cyclic basis every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every 5 years; both activities receive emphasis in the Interstate system treatment matrices (section 13.7) [1].

The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that transverse contraction joints and tied longitudinal contraction joints be saw‑cut as soon as the concrete is sufficiently hardened to allow sawing without excessive raveling, a measure intended to prevent random cracking (section 501.3(i)2) [2]. The same specification mandates eliminating one tie bar in each slab adjacent to any joint where a crack has opened at least 1/8‑inch and also eliminating one tie bar adjacent to dominant joints when paving between October 1 and April 1, measures aimed at reducing shear cracking (section 501.3(i)) [2]. Proper installation of load transfer units is addressed by staking dowel locations in advance, aligning saw cuts over the centre of dowel baskets and applying bond‑breaker lubricant at least one hour before concrete placement, which addresses joint cracking caused by inadequate load transfer (section 501.3(i)) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, pre‑overlay repairs such as slab stabilization, slabjacking or flexible base replacement are required when existing slabs move visibly, to eliminate non‑uniform support that would otherwise cause cracking (section 548.3(e)1) [2]. The specifications also require filling surface defects larger than 4 sq in and deeper than 1 in before overlay placement—especially when a geotextile interlayer is used—to stop crack initiation (section 548.3(e)1.f) [2]. When joints are faulted more than ¼‑inch, a geotextile interlayer must be placed over the joints; if faulting exceeds ½‑inch, an asphalt interlayer with a scratch course or milled joint is required, both acting as debonding layers that reduce reflective cracking (section 548.3(e)3.a‑b) [2]. Joint layout controls limit panel aspect ratio to not exceed 1.5, restrict panel size to no more than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and prohibit longitudinal joints in wheel paths, all intended to prevent premature slab cracking (section 548.3(i)6‑7) [2]. Saw cuts at least D/3 within 12 in of edges and at least D/4 elsewhere must be made as soon as the concrete can be sawn without excessive raveling, and insulating blankets must be provided when air temperature is 40°F or less or surface temperature change does not exceed 40°F per hour; these measures avoid random and thermal cracking (section 548.3(l)) [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete, control joints shall be saw‑cut to a depth of one‑quarter of the compacted slab with cutting begun no later than 18 hours after placement, a requirement that limits random cracking (section 502.3(i)3). The same section mandates maintaining a moist surface on fresh and cold joints and providing continuous curing for a minimum of 72 hours after final rolling using water, sheet or compound to keep the pavement surface moist, thereby reducing shrinkage cracking (section 502.3(j)) [2]. Placement shall not occur when ambient air temperature falls to 40°F or rises above 85°F with mixture temperatures exceeding 90°F, and evaporation rates greater than 0.20 lb/ft²·h must be mitigated because rapid drying can induce cracking (section 502.3(c)1‑3) [2]. A final compaction greater than 96 % of the maximum wet density for all lift thicknesses is required to help avoid cracking due to insufficient consolidation (section 502.3(h)2) [2]. In bonded concrete overlays, panel dimensions are limited by the same eighteen‑times‑thickness rule and aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, early saw‑cutting of joints is mandated to prevent random cracking, and longitudinal joints in wheel paths are prohibited; insulating blankets are required when air temperature is 45°F or less and surface temperature change does not exceed 40°F per hour, a curing measure that prevents thermal cracking (section 540.3(i) and 540.3(l)) [2]. The use of micro synthetic fibers—100 % virgin polypropylene, fibrillated, rough‑textured, no longer than 2 inches and with tensile strength of at least 32 ksi—is specified to prevent or reduce plastic shrinkage cracking (section 708.1(b)) [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, a crack wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness must be replaced at no additional cost; cracks wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than that depth are to be repaired according to the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R) recommendations, and cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are to be sealed with high molecular weight methacrylate after cleaning and sanding before traffic returns (section 529.3 11.a) [2]. In stream‑bed pavements, contraction joints 3/16‑inch wide and 2 inches deep shall be formed or sawed as soon as the concrete has set sufficiently to avoid raveling and before any shrinkage cracking occurs, a measure intended to prevent shrinkage‑related cracking (section 667.2) [2]. For asphalt‑concrete curbs, if placement is interrupted and hot mix temperature drops below 275°F, a vertical joint shall be cut and immediately painted with a thin, uniform coat of hot Asphalt Cement before work resumes, a treatment intended to prevent cracking at the joint caused by low temperature; any completed curb exhibiting sagging, cracking or distortion that cannot be satisfactorily repaired must be removed and replaced, thereby reducing existing cracks (section 636.3) [2]. Chemical admixtures called out in Section 711.3 shall be furnished to mitigate alkali‑silica reaction (ASR)–induced expansion and microcracking for all cement concrete structures (section 704.1(c)) [2]. Supplementary cementitious materials are permitted under the cement/SCM requirements to reduce ASR gel formation that leads to cracking, with an exemption possible only when a documented service record of non‑reactivity covering at least ten structures over ten years is accepted (section 704.1(g)3.c) [2]. Up to 25 % extra cement may be used in place of the standard anti‑washout admixture, and that extra cement may be replaced with other cementitious material and water up to 50 % of the mix water dose; this provision is intended to improve mixture performance and thus curb cracking when the anti‑washout admixture is not used (section 704.1(h)) [2]. Concrete median barriers shall have contraction joints formed by hand or saw that are 3/16‑inch wide and 2 inches deep at maximum 20‑foot intervals, with the joint sawed as soon as the concrete has set sufficiently to preclude raveling but before any shrinkage cracking occurs, a measure intended to control shrinkage cracking (section 623.2(c)) [2]. Shotcrete must be water cured with double‑thickness burlap or fogging for seven days while keeping the material fully saturated, and surface temperature shall be maintained at 50°F or greater for the first four days and above 40°F for the final three days of curing; these conditions are intended to prevent low‑temperature cracking (section 1043.3(e)) [2]. Chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed material is limited to an unconfined compressive strength below 900 psi, because strengths greater than 900 psi must be reworked as they may cause shrinkage cracking (section 344.3(g)) [2]. Finally, during compaction of asphalt mixtures the contractor shall adjust roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and roller size to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage, a measure that applies during compaction to meet density acceptance requirements (section 413.3(h)) [2].

In slabjacking operations the lift shall be performed in increments so that slab stresses are minimized and cracking, tilting or faulting is avoided; this incremental lift applies throughout the slab‑jacking operation itself and must follow the Manual and Publication 408 Specification Section 681 [1]. When a jointed plain concrete pavement is so distressed that patching is uneconomical, the existing slabs may be fractured into 12‑ to 24‑inch pieces and rolled into the base or subbase before placing an asphalt or flexible overlay; this cracking/breaking and seating step attempts to reduce differential movement of free‑moving slabs and thereby lessen the occurrence and severity of reflective cracking in the new overlay [1]. Seal coats, slurry seals and other surface treatments may be used to correct minor cracking (as well as minor rutting and loss of fine aggregate), but they shall not be used on the Interstate system, may only be applied where permitted by Table 5.1, and shall not be placed on a pavement that requires structural upgrading [1]. Applying an Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course—a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane immediately overlaid with a thin hot‑mix asphalt concrete layer in one pass—seals the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering; this reduction allows the surface to remain resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking whenever the UTWC is used as described in Section 5.5 [1]. Installing an asphalt paving interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid between the existing pavement and a new overlay mitigates reflective cracking that can occur when a new overlay is placed over an old cracked roadway; the interlayer is intended for alligator‑type age‑oxidation cracking that spans the surface, must be compatible with the asphalt mix, thermally stable and durable, and is applied under the conditions described in Subsection F of Section 5.12 [1]. A bonded PCC overlay should only be utilized where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if significant distress is present, the distress must be repaired or corrected before the bonded overlay is placed so that cracks are not reflected up through the overlay, as required in Section 10.6 [1]. Routine‑maintenance actions on the Interstate system require cyclic crack sealing of asphalt‑surfaced pavements every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every 5 years to repair and retard existing cracking; additionally, providing adequate subbase material is identified as a material requirement to prevent premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements [1]. For latex‑modified mortar or concrete wearing surfaces prepared by scarification, a vertical pull bond test shall be performed between 24 and 72 hours after curing and the overlay must achieve at least 250 psi bond strength; this measure targets debonding‑related cracking and applies to each span or day’s placement of scarified surfaces as stated in Section 1042.3(c) [2]. Heavy equipment and vehicular traffic are prohibited on fresh latex until the period shown in Table A has elapsed and the minimum strength described in Section 1042.3(d) is reached, a restriction intended to prevent load‑induced early cracking [2]. Saw‑cutting of transverse joints as soon as the concrete is hardened enough for cutting without excessive raveling, and tied longitudinal contraction joints likewise sawed at that early stage, are prescribed to prevent random cracking (Section 501.3(i) and Section 501.3(i)2) [2]. To limit shear cracking, one tie bar shall be eliminated in each slab adjacent to any joint where a crack of at least 1/8 inch has opened, and the elimination is also required for slabs placed between October 1 and April 1 that lie next to dominant joints (Section 501.3(j)) [2]. When new pavement abuts existing concrete, transverse or contraction joints must be aligned with existing joints or cracks and dominant joints marked within 48 hours so that dominant‑joint development controls cracking (Section 501.3(i)) [2]. The temperature change of the slab during curing may not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period, a limit intended to prevent thermal cracking (Section 501.3(l)) [2]. Prior to placement of an unbonded concrete overlay, any unstable slabs or slab fragments must be stabilized or repaired so that non‑uniform support does not cause cracking, and surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep must be filled with an approved patching material; the fabric temperature shall not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement (Section 548.3(e)1 and Section 548.3(e)3.a) [2]. All transverse and longitudinal joints must be sawed to a depth of at least D⁄3 within twelve inches of edges or at least D⁄4 elsewhere as soon as the concrete can be cut without raveling, a practice that limits random cracking (Section 548.3(i)6‑9) [2]. Panel aspect ratios shall not exceed 1.5 and longer dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness in order to reduce premature cracking (Section 548.3(i)5‑7) [2]. Curing of the overlay must begin within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and insulating blankets are to be removed so that surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F per hour when air temperature is ≤40 °F, thereby preventing thermal cracking during early strength gain (Section 548.3(l)) [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, control joints shall be saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted slab behind the rollers no later than eighteen hours after placement in order to prevent random cracking (Section 502.3(i)4) [2]. The pavement surface must be kept continuously moist for at least seventy‑two hours after final rolling by water cure, polyethylene sheeting or curing compound until a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi is attained, a requirement that mitigates shrinkage‑related cracking (Section 502.3(j)) [2]. For fresh horizontal joints, the surface shall be maintained moist to avoid excess moisture loss and thereby reduce the potential for joint cracking (Section 502.3(i)1) [2]. Before placement of a bonded concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, the milled surface must be cleaned and any cracks larger than three‑quarters inch filled with non‑shrink grout, eliminating existing fissures that could propagate (Section 540.3(e)) [2]. The joint layout shall avoid longitudinal joints in wheel paths and panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness nor a length‑to‑width ratio of 1.5; these measures are intended to reduce stress concentrations that lead to reflective or transverse cracking (Section 540.3(i)) [2]. Sawing of joints as soon as the concrete is hardened enough to permit cutting without excessive raveling is required to prevent random cracking (Section 540.3(i)) [2]. Curing materials shall be applied promptly and insulating blankets used when air temperature is 45 °F or less; blankets are removed only when the surface temperature change rate does not exceed 40 °F per hour, a practice that mitigates thermal cracking from rapid cooling (Section 540.3(l)) [2]. During slab rehabilitation, impact equipment capable of delivering a variable force must be used to create continuous full‑depth cracks while preserving aggregate interlock, and tools that cause surface spalling, punched holes or shattering are prohibited; this control prevents uncontrolled cracking (Section 522.3(a)) [2]. All existing slabs shall be pre‑fracturing saw‑cut to a maximum length of twenty feet (approximately one inch deep for two‑thirds of the depth) so that crack segments remain within prescribed dimensions, thereby controlling crack segment size (Section 522.3(b)) [2]. In areas adjacent to culverts, utilities, bridge approaches or other fixed objects, full‑depth sawcut panels of five by six feet shall be used instead of standard fracturing to limit cracking in those zones (Section 522.3(b)) [2]. The fractured slab must be seated with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller, applying at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat and vibratory rollers are prohibited; this ensures the broken pieces settle without generating new cracks (Section 522.3(d)) [2]. Concrete mixes shall incorporate 100 % virgin polypropylene micro‑fibers graded to a maximum length of two inches and possessing at least a tensile strength of 32 kips per square inch, conforming to ASTM C1116 Type III and accompanied by an ICC‑ES AC32 report; these fibers are intended to mitigate plastic shrinkage cracking (Section 708(b)) [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, any crack wider than 0.007 inch that extends deeper than one‑third of the slab depth shall be addressed by replacing the entire slab at no additional cost, eliminating severe cracking (Section 529.3(11.b)) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inch but with a depth less than one‑third of the slab depth shall be repaired in accordance with the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures recommendations, thereby addressing moderate cracking (Section 529.3(11.b)) [2]. Cracks that are 0.007 inch wide or less shall be sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after approximately twenty minutes before traffic is permitted, a step intended to prevent propagation of fine cracks (Section 529.3(11.b)) [2]. Section 711.3 requires monofilament or collated fibrillated synthetic fiber complying with ASTM C1116 Type III and an ICC‑ES AC32 test report; the mix procedure must ensure full dispersion without clumps to mitigate plastic shrinkage cracking (Section 711.3) [2]. Contraction joints of 3/16 inch width and 2 inches depth shall be formed or sawed, and sawing performed as soon as concrete has set sufficiently to preclude raveling and before any shrinkage cracking occurs, preventing shrinkage cracks during early curing; reinforcement must be placed as indicated and held firmly while the concrete is placed, and expansion joint filler of ½‑inch thickness shall be installed at not more than 100‑foot intervals, cut to be ½ inch less than pavement depth, mitigating thermal or movement stress cracking (Section 667.2) [2]. Similar requirements apply to concrete median barriers: contraction joints 3/16 inch wide and 2 inches deep shall be formed by hand or saw at a maximum spacing of 20 feet and sawn as soon as the concrete has set enough to preclude raveling before shrinkage cracking, preventing shrinkage cracking (Section 623.2(c)); a six‑inch strip beside the longitudinal joint of damaged precast barrier sections shall be cut so that cracked pavement can be removed and patched, thereby preventing further crack propagation during repair (Section 623.2(g)) [2]. Improper joint construction, improper curing procedures or an improper final finish are treated as surface defects that make a lot defective; executing those practices correctly is required to avoid cracking under the “Other Conditions” clause whenever such defects exist (Section 506.3(t)) [2]. Chemical admixtures specified in Section 711.3 shall be furnished to limit alkali‑silica reaction–induced cracking, and aggregates identified as potentially reactive may be exempt from cement or SCM requirements only when a documented service record of nonreactivity covering at least ten structures older than ten years, including visual cracking reports and petrographic analysis, is provided; this exemption is permitted per Section 704.1(g)2 (Section 704.1(g)) [2]. Water curing for seven days with double‑thickness burlap or fogging while keeping the surface fully saturated shall be performed to limit excessive plastic or drying‑shrinkage cracking (Section 1043.3(i)) [2]. Shotcrete surfaces shall be maintained at a temperature of 50 °F or greater for the first four days and above 40 °F for the final three days of cure, rejecting work if temperatures fall below 35 °F, as a condition to control shrinkage cracking (Section 1043.3(j)) [2]. Construction joints shall be formed with a 45° tapered edge and feather‑edging prohibited; continuous removal of rebound and overspray during placement is required to avoid weak zones that could develop into cracks, addressing joint‑related cracking (Section 1043.3(h)) [2]. Chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed material shall not exceed an unconfined compressive strength of 900 psi because higher strengths may result in shrinkage cracking and trigger rework, thereby preventing shrinkage cracking (Section 344.3(g)) [2]. During compaction of asphalt mixtures the contractor shall adjust roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage; this practice applies whenever the mixture is being compacted to meet density‑acceptance requirements (Section 413.3(h)1.i) [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

In Section 1042—Latex Modified Mortar or Concrete Wearing Surface, any surface crack or tear whose depth exceeds 1/4 inch and whose width is between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch must be repaired at no additional cost to the Department, with compliance determined by measuring the width, depth and length of each crack relative to permanent reference points. Under the Immediate Corrective Work provisions of Section 1047—Polyester Polymer Concrete Overlay, visible non‑structural surface cracks or tears greater than 1/8 inch deep and between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch wide must be repaired with the specified high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate penetrating crack sealer at no additional cost, while non‑structural cracks or tears exceeding 1/4 inch depth and greater than 0.016 inch width trigger removal and replacement of the wearing surface. In the rigid‑pavement specifications of Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, when cracks have opened 1/8‑inch or greater at longitudinal joints one tie bar must be eliminated in each slab adjacent to the joint to control shear cracking, a condition that governs compliance with the rigid‑pavement warranty. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, any cracking that appears outside the prescribed sawed joints is deemed defective and fails acceptance; if a crack occurs at or within 1.5 feet of a joint location before or during sawing, the contractor must omit the sawing operation and address the condition as required in Section 590. The slab‑stabilization warranty in Section 679 ties payment for cement grout work to radial cracking, reducing the pay item by one bag of cement for each 5 linear feet of radial cracking observed. Under Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, cracks wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth (D/3) must be replaced at no additional cost; cracks wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than D/3 are repaired in accordance with the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R) after acceptance of the repair procedure, and cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate following specified cleaning, mixing, squeegee and sand‑blasting steps before traffic is allowed. Section 711—Concrete Curing Material and Admixtures requires fibers for plastic shrinkage cracking to be monofilament or collated fibrillated synthetic fiber complying with ASTM C1116, 4.1.3‑Type III, with a test report meeting ICC‑ES AC32 Acceptance Criteria Section 3.2.1 from an independent certified laboratory, and the mix design must provide a method of adding the fibers so that they are dispersed during mixing and no clumps remain at the end of a mix cycle. Finally, in Section 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, cracking is listed among surface defects that make a lot defective when the cracks cannot be satisfactorily repaired, a determination made by the Representative; thus the acceptance limit for cracking in this specification is a repair‑satisfaction judgment rather than a numeric value.
