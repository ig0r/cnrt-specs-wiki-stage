Cracking

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Cracking is a topic under Distress.
cracking
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-27

Cracking in both flexible (asphalt) and rigid (concrete) pavements is the visible manifestation of a loss of structural integrity, indicating that the pavement’s ability to bear traffic loads has been compromised[1][2]. It develops when interrelated mechanisms—such as moisture infiltration, inadequate support layers, material aging, heavy traffic loading and construction deficiencies—reduce the structure’s capacity to distribute stresses, causing fissures that range from superficial surface fissures to deep fractures[1][2]. The distress is characterized by measurable width and depth limits (e.g., cracks wider than 0.007 in to 0.016 in and deeper than one‑quarter inch are considered structural in overlays, while narrower or shallower cracks are classified as non‑structural) that dictate whether the fissure can be sealed, repaired, or requires removal of the wearing surface[2].

PennDOT publications covering “Cracking” also include the following subtopics: Longitudinal Crack and Transverse Crack.

Longitudinal Crack and Transverse Crack represent specific orientations of fissures that signal compromised structural integrity in rigid pavements. [1][2] Longitudinal Crack runs parallel to the lay-down direction and permits slab migration and loss of aggregate interlock, while Transverse Crack appears perpendicular to travel due to inadequate subsurface drainage and saturated subgrades during spring thaw. [1][2] These distinct distress patterns dictate whether treatment focuses on restoring load transfer or necessitates full-slab replacement when stabilization efforts have failed. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Cracking in both flexible and rigid pavements is identified as the visible manifestation of loss of structural integrity, indicating that the pavement’s ability to bear loads has been compromised [1][2]. In asphalt surfaces, minor or low‑severity cracks appear as superficial fissures that can be sealed with surface treatments such as seal coats, slurry seals, or a Type A micro‑surfacing scratch course, which fill voids and shallow ruts less than one‑half inch deep, restore skid resistance, and arrest further propagation [1]. Routine maintenance crack sealing on the Interstate system is typically scheduled at intervals of approximately three to five years to retard progression of these fissures [1]. In concrete pavements, cracking—ranging from non‑structural plastic or drying shrinkage cracks to structural fractures that penetrate a significant portion of slab thickness—signals loss of bond performance and threatens slab stability [2]. Non‑structural surface cracks exceeding a depth of one‑eighth inch and having widths between 0.007 in and 0.016 in are sealed with a high‑modulus wet‑mix penetrating filler, while very fine cracks of 0.007 in or less are treated with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after cleaning and sanding [2]. When crack depth exceeds one‑quarter inch or width surpasses 0.016 in, the wearing surface is removed and replaced because such dimensions compromise bond integrity [2]. Structural cracks that extend deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness, even when only 0.007 in wide, require full‑depth slab replacement; shallower structural cracks are repaired in accordance with ACI 224R recommendations [2]. Longitudinal cracks occurring within a lane trigger removal and replacement of the entire slab, whereas radial cracks are stabilized at prescribed linear foot intervals using cement grout [2]. Extensive alligator‑type cracking associated with age‑related oxidation in asphalt indicates fatigue and low‑temperature distress; an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course can seal the surface, reduce oxidation, and maintain resilience against further fatigue cracking while also correcting raveling and moderate rutting [1]. For concrete pavements exhibiting extensive cracking accompanied by faulting, joint movement, or punchouts, a cracking/breaking and seating process is employed to reduce the size of free‑moving slabs, create a uniform stable base, and limit reflective cracking that would otherwise propagate through an overlay, thereby improving performance and service life [1]. The overarching objective of all crack‑related treatments across both pavement types is to restore or preserve smoothness, flatness, and load‑bearing capacity, prevent further deterioration such as reflective cracking, raveling, or premature failure, and meet acceptance criteria for structural performance [1][2].

The primary aim of cracking treatment in pavement engineering is to re‑establish a sound structural and functional condition that will reliably support traffic loads and thereby extend the pavement’s service life. To achieve this, large free‑moving concrete slabs are deliberately fragmented and seated so that a uniform support layer is created, which reduces differential movement at joints and punchouts and consequently lessens reflective cracking in any subsequent overlay. For low‑severity fissures in asphalt surfaces the guides prescribe the application of a micro‑surfacing sealant—typically a Type A mix—to close the cracks and fill voids, directly repairing the damage and restoring surface continuity. When cracks exceed one‑quarter inch in depth and fall within a width range of 0.007 in to 0.016 in, a high‑molecular‑weight penetrating filler or methacrylate resin is used to seal them, thereby restoring integrity and preventing further deterioration. Non‑structural surface cracks deeper than one‑eighth inch are also sealed with the same manufacturer’s penetrating resin and finished with abrasive sand after an approved corrective action plan, a step that both repairs the defect and slows its propagation. Structural cracks are removed and replaced, and concrete slabs that exceed critical width or depth limits are substituted at no additional cost, actions intended to eliminate the source of distress and maintain long‑term performance. Surface defects such as spalls or localized loss of material are patched with pre‑approved asphalt‑based or cementitious repair materials before overlay placement, ensuring a continuous load‑bearing surface. Routine crack‑sealing operations are scheduled cyclically every three to five years for asphalt pavements, providing periodic repair of existing distress and retarding further propagation, which contributes to service‑life extension. The distress survey that documents crack patterns is used to identify underlying mechanisms so that rehabilitation measures can be designed to prevent those same mechanisms from recurring, effectively halting additional deterioration. Across the guides, the consistent objective is therefore to repair damage—through sealing, patching, or replacement—while simultaneously preventing recurrence of the causative mechanisms, slowing crack growth, and ultimately extending the pavement’s useful life. Distress characterization and causes

The cracking distress is evaluated within each defined segment, a roughly half‑mile stretch of the state route. Its extent is quantified by the percentage of the segment area that exhibits low‑severity cracks (e.g., if 30 % of the segment shows two or more low‑severity distresses after the fifth‑year survey) or by treating a medium‑severity crack as a deviation covering six inches on each side along its full length. Severity is classified as low or medium, and the warranty program monitors these conditions annually; when the measured extent exceeds the prescribed thresholds, remedial actions such as microsurfacing or segment replacement are required. If the condition is judged potentially harmful or unsafe, immediate emergency repairs may be ordered to protect safety and maintain traffic operations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Cracking in both concrete and asphalt pavements is driven by a suite of interrelated mechanisms that reduce the structure’s ability to distribute stresses, including moisture infiltration, drainage deficiencies, inadequate structural capacity, weak support layers, material aging, environmental exposure, heavy traffic loads, and construction shortcomings [1][2]. Moisture that enters through surface fissures or stands on milled surfaces penetrates into the base and subgrade, where excess water weakens support and promotes crack propagation; when drainage is insufficient this water also participates in freeze‑thaw cycles that expand trapped moisture and accelerate fissuring [1][2]. The presence of standing water after milling, as well as rain or flowing water reaching fresh concrete before it attains minimum strength, further compromises bond integrity and initiates early fissures [2]. Insufficient support—whether from low subgrade stiffness, thin pavement sections, or inadequate subbase material—creates stress concentrations under anticipated traffic, especially heavy vehicles, leading to premature mid‑slab cracking and the development of free‑moving slab fragments that rock visibly under normal loads [1][2]. Structural deficiencies are amplified when panel dimensions exceed recommended limits; the length‑to‑width aspect ratio should not surpass 1.5 and panel lengths greater than eighteen times the overlay thickness generate excessive tensile stresses, particularly where longitudinal joints intersect wheel paths [2]. Material aging also contributes markedly: oxidative ageing of asphalt binders reduces fatigue resistance and low‑temperature resilience, producing alligator cracking unrelated to structural capacity, while aggregate‑related reactions such as alkali‑silica reaction (ASR) and the presence of reactive Vanport Limestone create expansive gels that generate internal pressure, microcracking, and joint expansion when moisture is present [1][2]. Construction practices introduce additional pathways for distress; rapid slabjacking or lifting at high temperatures imposes tensile forces that can crack slabs, whereas proper incremental lifting with controlled grout flow mitigates this risk [1]. Plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, and temperature‑induced movements in latex and polymer‑modified concrete overlays produce non‑structural cracks that reveal material defects or workmanship errors, especially when curing temperatures fall below 50 °F during the first four days or below 40 °F for the final three days, or when temperature changes exceed 40 °F within a single hour [2]. Improper joint construction, delayed sawing of contraction joints, inadequate reinforcement placement, and failure to protect newly placed pavement from the elements until specified strength is achieved further allow shrinkage stresses and moisture intrusion to develop unchecked, accelerating crack formation [2][1]. The guides therefore emphasize that addressing each mechanism—through adequate subdrainage, proper design of support layers, selection of durable materials, control of environmental exposure, realistic traffic loading estimates, and meticulous construction and curing practices—is essential to limit the initiation and propagation of cracks and to extend pavement service life.

Applicability and treatment selection

Routine crack sealing is employed on both asphalt‑surfaced and jointed concrete pavements that exhibit visible cracking, typically on a three‑to‑five year cycle for asphalt and about every five years for concrete joints, without reference to specific traffic or environmental limits. Full‑depth fracturing, often termed cracking, is reserved for rigid concrete pavements after removal of any asphalt overlay and loose joint sealants, and it requires that the subgrade be stable so that sonic vibratory equipment will not cause soil liquefaction. The operation must be carried out with traffic stopped on the affected segment, because movement would interfere with the fracturing and subsequent seating of the slab. Ambient conditions for full‑depth fracturing are limited to temperatures between roughly 45 °F and 90 °F and to pavement that is not frozen at the time of work. The treatment cannot be performed within ten feet horizontally or vertically of culverts, cross‑pipes, utility sleeves, nor within forty feet of bridge approaches, tunnel portals, or other structures, and a minimum three‑foot clearance from fixed objects such as manholes must be observed. Site factors that may prompt crack sealing include an inadequate subbase, which can lead to premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements and therefore warrant sealant repair. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Cracking treatments such as seal coating or routine crack sealing are only appropriate for low‑severity surface cracks, and they are expressly prohibited on Interstate highways and whenever the pavement requires a structural upgrade [1]. When cracks become moderate or severe, or when they coexist with other distresses—such as medium‑to‑high raveling, deep rutting, significant friction loss, moisture infiltration, extensive faulting, or inadequate subbase material—the use of surface treatments like micro‑surfacing is no longer suitable and a more substantial preservation, rehabilitation, or reconstruction method must be selected [1]. Likewise, if the amount of patching needed exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement area, routine crack sealing does not satisfy treatment criteria and a higher‑level intervention is required [1]. For concrete pavements, any extensive cracking or faulting that would allow distress to propagate through a bonded overlay precludes the use of such overlays; instead an unbonded overlay, repair of the cracks, or another rehabilitation technique should be employed [1]. When the existing slab is so deteriorated that patching is no longer economical, the practice of cracking and seating the slab becomes unsuitable, and an unbonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlay can provide needed support at lower cost [1]. Full‑depth reclamation, whose purpose is to improve support layers, is inappropriate for surface‑related cracking and should be replaced by a surface preservation strategy [1]. Crack sealing is only permissible when the fissure is narrow (approximately 0.007 in to 0.016 in wide) and shallow (no deeper than one‑quarter inch); any crack exceeding either of these limits, or associated with bond failure, honeycombing, unsound concrete, excessive air content, or low compressive strength, must not be sealed but instead the affected area must be removed and replaced [2]. Structural cracks are never candidates for sealant; they require removal of the damaged portion and replacement of the pavement [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 in but shallower than one‑third of the slab thickness must be repaired according to the latest ACI 224R recommendations rather than simply sealed, whereas cracks wider than 0.007 in that penetrate deeper than one‑third of the slab depth mandate full‑depth slab replacement at no additional cost to the agency [2]. If a representative determines that cracking is so extensive it cannot be satisfactorily repaired, the pavement segment is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced rather than patched or preserved [2]. Performance surveys further limit crack sealing; when a five‑year survey shows low‑severity distresses covering thirty percent or more of a segment’s area, microsurfacing or another approved treatment replaces routine sealant, and if the combined deviations—counting each medium‑severity crack as a twelve‑inch wide strip—affect more than twenty percent of the segment surface, the entire segment must be removed and replaced [2]. During any warranty period the agency does not perform routine crack sealing, so any cracking that exceeds the prescribed depth, width, or area thresholds triggers the prescribed remedial actions rather than sealant application [2]. Full‑depth fracturing (cracking) is also prohibited in locations where it could jeopardize nearby infrastructure; it may not be used within ten feet horizontally or vertically of culverts, cross‑pipes, utility sleeves, nor within forty feet of bridge approaches, tunnels, or other structures, and it cannot be employed where the subgrade is prone to liquefaction—in those zones sawcutting the concrete into small panels and repairing them is required instead [2].

Condition assessment and timing

The process begins with a systematic visual examination of the pavement surface to locate all fissures, tears, joint expansions, and other distresses, recording their presence, type, and severity in a distress survey that complies with PennDOT manuals and FHWA guidance. [1][2] For each identified crack the inspector measures width, depth, and length, establishing end‑point locations relative to permanent reference markers so that an exact location map can be produced. [2] Width measurements are taken with a calibrated gauge; cracks wider than 0.007 in but less than 0.016 in and deeper than 1⁄8 in are classified as non‑structural and slated for sealing with a high‑modulus water‑borne penetrant, whereas cracks exceeding 0.75 in in width must be cleaned and filled with an approved non‑shrink grout before any further work. [2] Structural cracking is identified when depth exceeds 1⁄4 in or greater than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3), triggering more extensive actions such as full‑depth slab replacement. [2] When a mechanical probe cannot reliably determine depth, coring is performed; two cores are extracted at each questionable location—one sent to an independent laboratory for detailed geometric analysis and the other retained for on‑site evaluation by the Representative or Laboratory Technical Services. [2] The visual survey also records accompanying distresses such as rutting, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling, and faulting, and quantifies the percentage of surface that is cracked for each pavement segment. [1] The crack‑percentage measurement is entered together with International Roughness Index (IRI) values and rutting or faulting data into the Pavement Condition/Treatment Matrices; any segment meeting matrix thresholds for cracking initiates routine crack sealing on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle. [1] Segments are classified as being in poor condition when two or more of the three distress components (cracking, rutting, IRI) receive a poor rating, and FHWA policy limits a state to no more than five percent of its Interstate lane‑miles in that category. [1] In addition to manual inspection, an annual automated pavement condition survey (videologging) conducted under the STAMPP program captures crack extent, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting; ambiguous automated results are supplemented with a supplemental manual visual inspection. [1][2] For radial or fatigue cracking that may indicate sub‑base problems, strategically drilled inspection holes and falling weight deflectometer (FWD) testing are employed to obtain subgrade stiffness values and elastic pavement modulus, with the FWD back‑calculation informing whether the underlying structure can support the proposed repair. [1][2] When micro‑cracking associated with alkali‑silica reaction is suspected, representative core samples are subjected to petrographic analysis per ASTM C856 and aggregate mineralogy verification per ASTM C295 to confirm the presence of ASR gel formations before any treatment decision. [2] The compiled distress inventory is compared against performance thresholds such as a 30 percent segment‑area coverage by two or more low‑severity cracks in the year‑5 survey, which triggers remedial microsurfacing; medium‑severity cracks are treated as affecting a corridor six inches on either side of the crack for extent calculations. [2] Finally, before any rehabilitation is executed, in‑situ strength testing—including FWD measurements, dynamic cone penetrometer readings, or lightweight deflectometer assessments—provides subgrade stiffness values needed to verify that the pavement structure can accommodate the selected repair technique. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 10 (p. 183), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–448), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (p. 592), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 664–665), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1110)

Cracking interventions are triggered at distinct stages of pavement deterioration, ranging from early surface fissures to severe slab fragmentation [1][2]. As soon as any visible crack or tear appears, an investigation confirms its nature and, if it is a non‑structural defect, immediate repair with a high‑molecular‑weight penetrating filler or sealer is required; for latex overlays the depth threshold is one‑quarter inch and the acceptable width lies between 0.007 in and 0.016 in, while polymer‑modified concrete overlays use a reduced depth trigger of one‑eighth inch with the same width limits [2]. Structural cracks are excluded from sealing and must be removed and replaced following an approved corrective action plan [2]. When existing concrete pavements have deteriorated beyond economical patching—evidenced by free‑moving slabs, faulted joints, or pronounced differential movement—the cracking/breaking and seating method is employed, breaking the slab into pieces roughly 12 to 24 inches long and rolling them into the base or subbase before an overlay is placed [1]. For pavements that remain structurally sound but exhibit low‑severity cracking, surface treatments such as asphalt scratch‑course/seal‑coat combinations, Type A micro‑surfacing mixes, or other sealants are applied to seal cracks, fill voids, and address accompanying raveling, friction loss, or moisture infiltration [1]. Existing pavements with cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch must have the openings cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or another department‑approved bonding material prior to any overlay placement, and similarly, cracks wider than 0.007 in and deeper than one‑third of slab thickness (D/3) require full‑slab replacement, whereas those wider than 0.007 in but shallower than D/3 are repaired according to ACI 224R procedures after departmental approval [2]. Cracks that are 0.007 in or narrower are sealed with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate sealer and sanded before traffic returns, forming part of routine maintenance activities that are scheduled cyclically—typically every three to five years for asphalt surfaces and every five years for concrete joints—once surface cracks are identified in periodic condition surveys [1]. Additionally, crack sealing may be employed when a crack reaches at least medium severity, defined as a deviation extending six inches on either side of the crack for its entire length, provided the combined affected area does not exceed 20 % of the segment; exceeding this threshold triggers more extensive remediation such as removal and replacement [2].

Materials and repair details

The initial step in addressing pavement cracking is to measure crack width, depth and length so that the appropriate repair method can be selected based on established thresholds[2]. Cracks no wider than 0.007 in are sealed with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate sealer that is mixed according to the manufacturer’s directions, poured and squeegeed into a cleaned, dry crack, and after roughly twenty minutes covered with commercial‑grade dry‑blast sand before traffic is allowed[2]. Non‑structural surface cracks in latex or polymer‑modified concrete overlays that are deeper than one‑eighth inch (or one‑quarter inch where specified) and between 0.007 in and 0.016 in wide are filled with a high‑modulus, high‑workability penetrating filler supplied by the overlay manufacturer; the cracks must be cleaned, kept dry, completely filled flush with the surface and immediately coated with surface‑finishing sand to provide an abrasive finish[2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 in but whose depth is less than one‑third of the slab thickness are repaired in accordance with ACI 224R using appropriate sealants or patching materials approved by the Representative[2]. When a crack exceeds one‑third slab thickness, is deeper than one‑quarter inch, wider than 0.016 in, or otherwise classified as structural, the wearing surface must be removed and the affected slab replaced; replacement slabs are saw‑cut to panels no longer than twenty feet, with joint depths of one‑third slab thickness and a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, panel dimensions not exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio below 1.5, full‑width slabs placed by slip‑forming at least ten feet between transverse joints, dowel bars field‑coated with bond‑breaker lubricant as required, and new joints cut with a green‑cut saw to the prescribed depth and width[2]. Cracks greater than three‑quarters of an inch are cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or another Department‑approved material to secure bonding before a concrete overlay is placed[2]. For fracture repair, panels are saw‑cut to a depth of about one inch within two‑thirds of the slab thickness and the fractured slabs are seated using a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller equipped with four to seven rubber wheels inflated to at least 100 psi, making three passes for crack‑and‑seat and five passes for break‑and‑seat operations[2]. Voids created by the fracture are backfilled with Subbase No. 2A and topped with Flexible Base Replacement; the surface is then swept, tack‑coated and an asphalt leveling course placed within one week while protecting it from freezing[2]. When conventional patching is no longer economical, the crack‑and‑seat method fractures the existing concrete slab into pieces roughly twelve to twenty‑four inches long and rolls each fragment into the underlying base or subbase to create a uniform support before an overlay is applied; any cracks that are patched in advance of this operation must be filled with a flexible‑base course replacement rather than a conventional concrete patch, ensuring compatibility with the seated slab[1][2]. Surface‑level cracking and shallow depressions up to one‑half inch deep are treated with a micro‑surfacing treatment using a Type A mix that seals cracks, fills voids, addresses shallow ruts and provides a scratch or surface course; a double coat is recommended unless the pavement condition is judged excellent, and on Interstate projects a double application of Type A SRL‑E is required even when the surface appears excellent[1]. An alternative mitigation strategy employs an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (UTWC) that combines a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane laid by a self‑priming paver with an ultra‑thin hot‑mix overlay; three aggregate gradations are available—Type A, B or C—with nominal maximum sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively, and the finished thickness is set at one to one‑and‑a‑half times the largest aggregate diameter, which translates to about a half‑inch for Type A and three‑quarters of an inch for Types B and C; this system reduces oxidation, enhances resistance to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking while also correcting raveling and moderate rutting and is expected to perform for eight to ten years without traffic restrictions[1]. To prevent reflective cracking when a new asphalt overlay is placed over an existing cracked surface, the design calls for an asphalt paving interlayer positioned between the old pavement and the new wearing course; acceptable interlayers include needle‑punched geotextile fabric, high‑strength fiberglass mesh grids or composite reinforcing grids, each of which must be compatible with the asphalt mix and applied with a suitable tack coat as directed by the supplier[1]. When a geotextile interlayer is used, any surface defect larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be patched with an approved asphalt‑based, cementitious or comparable repair mix and compacted to a flat, tight surface before the fabric is laid; the geotextile edges are overlapped by approximately eight inches (± 2 in) and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three layers of fabric are permitted[2]. The overlay incorporating the interlayer should have a minimum thickness of one‑and‑a‑half inches to ensure adequate support for milling and bonding[1]. Radial cracks may be stabilized by drilling holes at the crack locations and filling those holes with bags of cement grout, optionally using an accelerator; contract pricing is adjusted by reducing one bag of cement for every five linear feet of radial cracking[2]. Joint detailing to control future cracking includes contraction joints formed or sawed to a width of three‑sixteenths of an inch and a depth of two inches, with construction joint edges rounded to a quarter‑inch radius; premolded half‑inch expansion joint filler is placed at intervals not exceeding one hundred feet, set a half‑inch shy of the pavement depth and sealed as specified[2]. Curb cracking is treated similarly: any curb segment that cannot be repaired is removed and fully replaced, and if placement of a curb is interrupted and the mix cools below 275 °F, a vertical joint is cut and a thin uniform coat of hot asphalt cement (Class PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28) applied before work resumes, after which the new curb is compacted and cured[2]. Medium‑severity cracks are considered to affect six inches on each side along their entire length, and when spaced less than one hundred feet apart they are repaired as a single continuous patch using square or rectangular sections of material that meet the specification requirements; full removal and replacement may employ plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another approved material is specified[2]. When low‑severity distresses exceed prescribed thresholds, remedial actions such as microsurfacing or other Department‑approved treatments are required, with microsurfacing employing a Type A mix as described for surface‑level cracking[1][2]. Finally, the specification mandates the use of 100 % virgin polypropylene microfibers that are fibrillated, rough‑textured and interconnected, with a maximum length of two inches and a minimum tensile strength of 32 kips per square inch; these fibers must meet ASTM C1116 Type III requirements and be accompanied by an independent laboratory test report satisfying ICC‑ES AC32 acceptance criteria[2].

The performance of crack‑mitigation treatments on both concrete and asphalt pavements is governed by a set of material characteristics, compatibility constraints, and the prevailing condition of the existing structure. Low‑viscosity expanding polyurethane grouts, such as high‑density two‑component systems, provide superior void penetration and resistance to water and chemical attack, thereby reducing stress concentrations that could initiate new cracks [1]. Latex cementitious overlays must attain a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi before testing and develop a bond with the underlying slab of at least 250 psi to ensure adequate load transfer [2]. Reinforcing fibers used to control plastic‑shrinkage cracking are required to be 100 % virgin polypropylene, fibrillated, rough‑textured, and interconnected, with a maximum length of two inches and a minimum tensile strength of 32 kip/in², conforming to ASTM C1116 Type III and documented by an ICC‑ES AC32 report [2]. Concrete mixes employed in overlay systems must meet a Percent Within Limits of at least fifty‑five percent for both compressive strength and plastic air content, while aggregate reactivity, cement alkali content, and supplementary cementitious material dosage are evaluated because they influence susceptibility to alkali‑silica reaction [2][1].

Compatibility between the treatment materials and the existing pavement is equally critical. Sealants used for surface crack repair should be supplied by the same manufacturer as any polymer‑modified cementitious overlay, and reinforcement bars must be free of rust, dirt, oil, grease, or other foreign substances that could impair steel‑concrete bond [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is placed, no more than three layers may be used at any location, edges must overlap by eight inches plus or minus two inches, and the fabric temperature must not exceed 120 °F before concrete placement; similar temperature restrictions apply to asphalt interlayers, which are required to have a nominal depth of one inch and be bonded with a compatible hot‑mix asphalt cement such as PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28 over a clean tack coat [2]. Polymer interlayers beneath asphalt overlays must be thermally stable, physically durable, and chemically compatible with the asphalt so that a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond is formed, and the overlying Superpave 6.3 mm wearing course should be placed only on sound pavements where structural integrity has been confirmed and patching does not exceed two percent of the surface area [1][2].

The condition of the existing pavement dictates whether a particular treatment will succeed. A firm, uniformly compacted subgrade free of unsuitable material provides the stable base required for any overlay, and slabs must be stable; surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be patched with an approved asphalt, cementitious, or equivalent material and compacted to a flat, tight surface before treatment [2]. For concrete pavements, bonded jointed plain‑concrete (JPCP) overlays are appropriate only when the substrate is in good or fair condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage functions properly, allowing the overlay and slab to act as a monolithic unit that resists further cracking; unbonded overlays may be used on moderately to severely deteriorated pavements where bonding is not required, but prior to any bonded overlay existing slabs should be cracked or broken at joints to reduce the size of free‑moving panels and limit differential movement that could cause reflective cracking [1]. Visible cracks deeper than one‑eighth inch and between 0.007 in and 0.016 in wide are eligible for sealant repair, whereas cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch must be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout before overlay placement; cracks exceeding 0.007 in in width and deeper than one‑third the slab thickness require full‑depth slab replacement [2]. After milling operations, standing water must be eliminated and the milled surface temperature kept below 120 °F to protect bond quality [2].

Micro‑surfacing treatments are effective when low‑severity cracking, modest raveling, limited weathering, and minimal moisture infiltration are present; selection of Type A for shallow cracks and ruts less than one‑half inch deep, Type B for moderate distress, or Type RF for deeper faults, together with a double coat unless the surface is in excellent condition, ensures material compatibility with the observed damage level [1]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses rely on a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane that must seal the surface to limit oxidation and weathering, preserving fatigue resistance and reducing low‑temperature cracking; thicknesses are limited to one‑half inch for Type A and three‑quarters of an inch for Types B and C, requiring precise control of aggregate size [1]. Reclaimed pavement mixes introduce additional considerations: the reclaimed material’s strength potential must be balanced against brittleness, as excessive chemical stabilizer content (cement, lime, fly ash) raises stiffness but may promote cracking, especially when the existing subgrade is moist or drainage deficiencies exist; therefore a thorough distress survey to identify current cracking severity, moisture levels, and drainage problems must be completed before constructing the reclaimed layer [1]. Inherent material issues such as inadequate subbase that can trigger mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements, or problematic aggregates like Vanport limestone or susceptibility to alkali‑silica reaction, may not be captured by automated surveys and should be detected through field inspection prior to selecting any crack‑related treatment [1].

Treatment procedures

When cracking in a pavement overlay is observed, the guides require an initial joint investigation with the responsible representative to determine crack type, source, and extent. [2] Each fissure is then measured for width, depth, and length, and its termini are located relative to permanent reference points; if probe measurements cannot establish depth, two cores are extracted for laboratory analysis. [2] Non‑structural cracks are prepared by cleaning the surface and keeping it covered and dry until the sealing operation is performed. [2] For cracks whose depth exceeds one‑quarter inch in latex overlays or one‑eighth inch in polymer‑modified concrete overlays, yet whose width lies between 0.007 in. and 0.016 in., a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate penetrating filler is mixed according to the manufacturer’s data sheet, applied flush with a squeegee or by pouring, and immediately thereafter the wetted surface is coated with a surface‑finishing sand to create an abrasive finish. [2] When the crack width is 0.007 in. or less, the same methacrylate sealant is mixed per manufacturer directions, poured into the fissure and forced in with a squeegee; after roughly twenty minutes the cured sealant is covered with commercial‑grade dry‑blast sand before traffic is restored. [2] All sealing actions must be accompanied by a Corrective Action Plan that includes the material data sheet and specifies ambient temperature, substrate temperature, and moisture conditions for approval. [2] Structural cracks or those exceeding the prescribed depth or width limits are removed and replaced; removal involves cutting a minimum of ten feet of concrete between existing transverse joints for reinforced cement concrete, or removing an entire panel for plain cement concrete, discarding any intersecting joint and reconstructing it in kind according to standard joint‑construction drawings. [2] Where an asphalt overlay is present, the overlying asphalt is stripped, joint seals are removed, and slabs longer than twenty feet are saw‑cut to about one inch depth (approximately two‑thirds of slab thickness) before fracturing. [2] A test section at least one thousand feet long is fractured with variable‑force equipment to establish the optimal fracture pattern, after which the full production fracture proceeds while keeping all cracks visible. [2] The fractured pavement is then seated by rolling a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller over the surface, making at least three passes for the crack‑and‑seat method or five passes for the break‑and‑seat method, with tires inflated to roughly one hundred psi; seating is considered satisfactory when visual inspection shows no substrate movement. [2] Following seating, the surface is swept clean, a tack coat applied, and an asphalt leveling course placed within one week, avoiding exposure to freezing conditions. [2] When stabilization with cement grout is required, a deflection test is performed at each location, holes are drilled at the crack sites, any necessary patching is completed, and bags of cement grout—augmented with accelerator if needed—are placed into the prepared holes; the unit price includes the grout material, accelerator, drilling, and patching actions. [2] For severely distressed concrete pavements that are not economically repairable, the guides prescribe a cracking/breaking and seating method in which the existing jointed plain‑concrete or reinforced slabs are fractured into roughly twelve to twenty‑four inch pieces and rolled into the underlying base or subbase to provide a uniform support before any overlay is placed. [1] After this reduction, any residual cracks are patched with a flexible base‑course material rather than additional concrete, thereby ensuring a stable substrate for the new pavement. [1]

Implementation of crack mitigation begins with a thorough field investigation, and when the depth of a fissure cannot be measured with a mechanical probe, coring equipment is used to extract two cores at each location for laboratory analysis and local testing before any repair proceeds [2]. Minor cracking is typically addressed with seal coats, slurry seals or comparable surface treatments that require no special equipment, although the guides prohibit their use on Interstate highways and require compliance with specified criteria for other roadways [1]. When an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (UTWCEM) is selected, a self‑priming paver deposits the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane directly ahead of the screed and then lays the hot mix in a single pass using one paving machine; this operation is performed under normal paving weather and imposes no traffic restrictions while the surface cures [1]. For overlay methods that address structural cracking, the work sequence starts with preparation of the existing pavement, often involving removal of deficient material with heater planers or milling machines followed by placement of a scratch or leveling course, and must be timed within the weather limits specified in Publication 408 to ensure adequate drainage and to mitigate freeze‑thaw effects [1][2]. If traffic remains on site during overlay placement—particularly stopping, turning, or slow‑moving vehicles—a non‑tracking tack coat is applied to protect the fresh surface, and thin overlays on Interstates are limited to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches unless special approval is obtained [1]. For rigid pavements, after repairing any unstable slabs the surface is swept with a power broom or vacuum truck, then geotextile interlayers are unrolled in overlapping strips that must be kept clean and damp but not saturated, while construction traffic is avoided on these layers until sufficient strength develops; when an asphalt interlayer is used it is compacted with a rubber‑tire roller after the pavement surface temperature has been reduced to below 120 °F [2]. Concrete overlays require removal of existing asphalt using milling machines or pneumatic hammers, a final cleaning no more than thirty minutes before placement, and verification that the overlay surface temperature is under 120 °F—achieved with fog‑misting devices or protective blankets while ensuring no standing water remains—and saw cutting of transverse joints is performed with a green‑cut (or early‑entry) saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness as soon as the concrete can be cut without excessive raveling [2]. Environmental controls are critical throughout: when air temperature falls to 40 °F or lower, insulating blankets must be applied to prevent rapid heat loss and the rate of concrete surface temperature change is limited to no more than 40 °F per hour; curing blankets remain in place when ambient temperature is at or below 45 °F and are removed only after temperatures rise above that threshold or once compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi [2]. Variable‑force impact equipment may be used for fracture operations, but sonic vibratory tools are permitted only where subgrade liquefaction will not occur, and a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller inflated to at least 100 psi seats the slabs, with roller seating never performed under wet conditions [2]. Load testing with a falling weight deflectometer is allowed only when pavement surface temperatures are between 45 °F and 90 °F and the surface is not frozen [2]. Traffic restrictions accompany each stage: heavy equipment and vehicular traffic are prohibited on fresh latex surfaces until the curing period defined in Table A has elapsed, construction traffic must be avoided on newly placed geotextile or interlayers until adequate strength is achieved, and after fracture and seating all vehicular and construction traffic are barred from the roadway until an asphalt overlay is placed, with only limited crossing permitted for ramp access; a traffic‑control plan must be provided to preserve normal flow, combine repair patches less than 100 ft apart into continuous patches, and implement temporary repairs when adverse weather delays permanent work [1][2].

Quality and workmanship

The material package for any pavement layer that is susceptible to cracking must conform precisely to the contract specifications and be sourced from approved plants that operate documented quality‑control programs, whether the product is a latex overlay, an asphalt mixture, Class A cement concrete, shotcrete, or curb mix [2]. For latex systems the required bond strength between the overlay and its substrate is verified by a vertical pull test that must achieve at least 250 psi [2]. Asphalt mixtures are required to be produced in fully automated plants that regulate reclaimed‑asphalt‑pavement handling, aggregate drying, heating temperature and blending, and the placement temperature must be maintained between 290 °F and 315 °F with a tolerance of ±15 °F; if the mix falls below 275 °F hot‑asphalt cement is added to retain workability [2]. Shotcrete batches may not remain unagitated for more than forty‑five minutes, and water must be incorporated no later than ninety minutes before placement to preserve mix integrity [2]. When synthetic fibers are used in concrete they must be 100 % virgin polypropylene, fibrillated, rough‑textured, interconnected, free of reprocessed olefin, no longer than two inches, and possess a minimum tensile strength of 32 ksi, with compliance documented by an independent laboratory report meeting ICC‑ES AC32 and ASTM C1116 Type III criteria [2]. All joint‑sealing materials, expansion‑joint fillers, reinforcement and curing compounds must be the exact items listed in the contract documents [2]. In the case of a bonded Portland cement concrete overlay, the inspection must first evaluate the existing concrete for cracking and faulting because any significant distress will be transmitted through the new slab unless it is corrected, and this condition‑survey quantifies the extent of cracks and other defects before corrective work is undertaken [1]. More generally, surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch are required to be patched with an approved material and fully compacted, while unstable slabs or moving fragments must be stabilized prior to placement of any interlayer material [2]. The subgrade is excavated to the prescribed dimensions, unsuitable material removed, and the foundation compacted and protected from moisture until curing is complete [2]. Contraction joints are formed or sawed to a width of 3/16 inch and a depth of two inches; for slabs longer than twenty feet the saw cuts shall be placed within ±1 inch of two‑thirds slab depth, and panel aspect ratios should not exceed 1.5 with a preference toward unity [2]. Before placement the existing pavement or substrate is cleaned and a tack coat applied, and ambient and substrate temperatures must meet sealant manufacturer specifications before any high‑modulus water‑based penetrating crack sealer is used [2]. During installation the prescribed placement temperature and density targets must be achieved, with density verified either by pavement cores or, where cores are not required, by an optimum‑rolling pattern that satisfies the specified compactness criteria [2]. The overlay or shotcrete is placed at the depth stipulated in the design, and for bonded PCC overlays the thickness is checked to confirm it falls within the minimum and maximum limits prescribed for a monolithic slab behavior [1][2]. Equipment used for fracturing or impact must be capable of delivering a controllable variable force without causing surface spalling, punch holes, or shattering, and all asphalt courses, debonded joint seals and required saw cuts are inspected before fracture operations commence [2]. Continuous operation of pavers or shotcrete rigs along correctly set string or chalk lines is mandatory; overspray, rebound, sand lenses, voids, delaminations, sags or sloughing must be eliminated and line‑grade tolerances observed throughout placement [2]. A minimum protection period of twelve hours follows placement before the final evaluation of the work can begin [2]. After construction each crack is recorded with its width, depth, length and the locations of its ends relative to permanent reference points, and when depth cannot be measured a core is taken for laboratory analysis [2]. Non‑structural cracks such as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related cracking or finishing tears that exceed one‑quarter inch in depth and fall between 0.007 inch and 0.016 inch in width must be repaired with an approved penetrating filler documented in the quality‑control plan [2][1]. Core sampling is used to distinguish structural from non‑structural cracking, and any crack located within 1.5 ft of a joint or outside sawed joints renders the work defective [2]. A falling‑weight deflectometer test on each thousand‑foot lot must produce an average elastic modulus within the range specified in Table A, and overall density must exceed ninety‑two percent of the theoretical maximum [2]. Representative cores are also subjected to petrographic analysis per ASTM C856 to detect alkali‑silica gel formation and associated microcracking, with aggregate classification confirmed by ASTM C295 [2]. All repairs, including permitted crack sealing, must meet the surface tolerance required by Section 413.3(l), and the pavement is deemed acceptable only when it is free of fissures that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, confirming that material quality, joint construction, curing procedures and final finish were all executed correctly [2][1].

A satisfactory cracking treatment is judged by whether the existing concrete slab has been broken into fragments roughly twelve to twenty‑four inches long and those pieces have been seated into the underlying base or subbase so that a uniform support exists for any subsequent overlay. The fragment length serves as a primary visual indicator that free‑moving slabs have been sufficiently reduced to limit differential movement at existing cracks, joints, and punchouts. In addition to fragment size, the residual surface must exhibit only low‑severity (minimal or insignificant) cracking; any segment showing more extensive cracking or faulting is deemed outside the acceptance tolerance and must be repaired before an overlay can be placed. For non‑structural surface cracks in latex or polymer‑modified concrete overlays, any crack deeper than one‑quarter inch must be addressed; if its width lies between 0.007 in and 0.016 in it is repaired with a penetrating filler, whereas a crack that exceeds both the quarter‑inch depth and the 0.016 in width is classified as defective and requires removal of the wearing surface. A less severe limit applies to cracks as shallow as one‑eighth inch: those whose width falls within the same 0.007–0.016 in band are also sealed with filler, while any crack deeper than a quarter‑inch or wider than 0.016 in again triggers surface removal. When concrete overlays are saw‑cut, cracking is acceptable only when it occurs within the intended joint lines; fissures appearing outside those joints render the panel defective and sawing must be halted if a crack is observed at or within one and a half feet of a planned joint before or during cutting. For fractured rigid pavements, acceptance requires that every induced crack be visible on the surface, that the seated slab show no further vertical movement under roller inspection, that each broken piece remain within the size limits prescribed by the guide, and that a falling‑weight deflectometer test over a one‑thousand‑foot section produce an average elastic modulus within the specified range. In plain concrete slabs, crack width is the primary metric: cracks no wider than 0.007 in are simply sealed after cleaning and sanding; cracks wider than 0.007 in but with depth less than one‑third of slab thickness must be repaired in accordance with current ACI 224R recommendations and approved before work begins; cracks exceeding both the width threshold and a depth greater than one‑third of slab thickness require full slab replacement at no additional cost to the owner. Across all pavement types, the professional judgment of the responsible representative is decisive: any cracking or honeycombing that cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the representative classifies the segment as defective and mandates removal or replacement. For shotcrete surfaces, satisfaction is defined by the absence of excessive plastic or drying‑shrinkage cracking, a surface sag not exceeding one‑quarter inch over a ten‑foot straightedge, and abrupt irregularities limited to one‑eighth inch. Long‑term performance criteria also define acceptability: after five years, a segment is satisfactory when less than thirty percent of its area contains two or more low‑severity cracks and when the combined deviation area—including medium‑severity cracks counted as a six‑inch band on each side—does not exceed twenty percent of the segment surface; exceeding either threshold triggers remedial microsurfacing, and if the affected area surpasses twenty percent, complete segment replacement is required. Performance and service life

Cracking in both concrete and asphalt pavements manifests as fissures that create free‑moving slabs, producing differential movement that degrades surface condition and ride quality, thereby reducing structural durability and shortening the pavement’s expected service life. When cracks are extensive enough to propagate through bonded overlays, designers limit the use of such overlays to pavements with only minimal distress or require prior repair of the cracks before overlay placement. A common rehabilitation strategy therefore breaks the existing concrete into pieces roughly 12 to 24 inches long and seats them in the base or subbase prior to applying a new overlay, which stabilizes support, limits slab movement, and reduces reflective cracking in the overlying layer. Applying a rigid or flexible overlay of at least 1½ inches thickness after proper preparation further restores functionality, while the inclusion of an interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh, or composite grid retards crack propagation and provides moisture protection, especially in freeze‑thaw environments, contributing to a longer effective design life. Routine maintenance actions, notably cyclic crack sealing every three to five years on asphalt surfaces, preserve functional condition and delay the need for more extensive rehabilitation, helping maintain service life until reconstruction becomes necessary. For rigid pavements where cracking cannot be satisfactorily repaired, specifications require removal and replacement of the affected portion, a direct acknowledgment that untreated cracks shorten expected service life. Within warranty frameworks, low‑severity cracks that, together with other distresses, occupy at least thirty percent of a segment’s area trigger mandatory remedial actions such as microsurfacing or an approved alternative treatment. Medium‑severity cracks are treated as deviations extending six inches on each side for their entire length, and if the combined deviations from all distress types exceed twenty percent of the segment surface, the entire segment must be removed and replaced. These prescribed thresholds underscore that unchecked cracking degrades performance and accelerates loss of service life unless addressed promptly through either targeted rehabilitation or full‑depth replacement. [1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–126), Ch. 10 (p. 183), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–380)

Cracking can manifest as a spectrum of distresses ranging from non‑structural surface fissures such as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage and temperature‑induced tears to structural longitudinal cracks, spalls, random premature cracking in reinforced concrete panels, fatigue, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, edge‑slump induced cracks between lanes, joint faulting and alligator‑type oxidation cracking that may propagate through new surfaces [2][1]. When pre‑existing cracks are not adequately treated before an asphalt or concrete overlay is placed, reflective cracking commonly appears in the overlay, and if severe cracks remain the overlay can become a monolithic slab that transmits differential movement to the surface, accelerating crack propagation [1][2]. Improper slabjacking operations—such as lifting in large increments, grout leakage from joints or cracks before reaching target elevation, or pumping under compressive temperature conditions—can generate new cracking, tilting and faulting of concrete slabs, especially when the depressed area is extensive, subgrade drainage is poor, or existing joints are closely spaced [1]. The development of these distresses is strongly influenced by the depth and width of the underlying cracks; defects exceeding one‑quarter inch deep with widths between 0.007 in and 0.016 in in latex overlays, deeper than one‑eighth inch for polymer‑modified concrete overlays, or any crack larger than one‑eighth inch in slip‑form concrete must be repaired because they act as nuclei for reflective cracking [2]. High traffic loads, particularly when equivalent single axle loads surpass the design ESALs or exceed the forecasted 20‑year life, increase stress on cracked pavements and hasten the emergence of fatigue and transverse cracking [1][2]. Adverse climate conditions such as freeze‑thaw cycles, rapid temperature fluctuations greater than 40 °F per hour during curing, and moisture intrusion that raises subgrade water content weaken structural capacity and promote both reflective and alligator cracking [1][2]. Temperature control of the mix is critical; placement temperatures below 40 °F for asphalt mixtures, mixture temperatures outside the 290‑315 °F range or dropping below 275 °F, as well as high evaporation rates above 0.20 lb/ft²·h, create thermal stresses that can exceed the required bond strength of 250 psi and trigger crack formation [2]. Poor subbase material quality, inadequate compaction, contaminated reinforcement, misaligned tie bars and excessive edge slump also contribute to premature cracking, with edge slump greater than one‑eighth inch between lanes deemed unacceptable and tie bar elimination recommended when cracks open one‑eighth inch or more to reduce shear cracking [2][1]. Panel geometry that departs from an aspect ratio of 1.0, exceeding a ratio of 1.5 or a length greater than eighteen times the overlay thickness concentrates stresses and encourages premature cracking in slip‑form slabs [2]. Chemical reactions such as alkali‑silica reaction, which occur when reactive aggregates are combined with high cement alkali content without appropriate admixtures or supplemental cementitious material, generate gel formation that produces surface fissures and joint expansion that recur throughout service life [2]. Treatment failures arise when cracks are not sealed at recommended intervals—typically every three to five years for asphalt surfaces and approximately every five years for concrete joints—or when sealing materials do not meet the high‑modulus specifications required for latex or polymer‑modified overlays, allowing crack propagation to continue [1][2]. When structural cracks are identified, the prescribed remedial action is slab removal and replacement, while longitudinal cracks within a lane also trigger removal of the affected slabs; failure to execute these actions results in persistent reflective cracking and reduced pavement performance [1][2]. Joint faulting exceeding one‑half inch necessitates a scratch course or milling of the joint to reduce faulting, and avoidance of longitudinal joints in wheel paths is recommended to limit crack initiation under traffic loads [2][1]. Overall, successful mitigation of cracking requires consideration of anticipated traffic loading, moisture management, drainage adequacy, temperature control during construction, proper slabjacking techniques, appropriate sealant intervals, and adherence to geometric thresholds that together influence the likelihood of recurring distresses after treatment [1][2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After crack sealing or other cracking treatment, the segment is entered into the routine annual pavement condition assessment program [1]. The Automated Pavement Condition Survey (videologging) performed under STAMPP records cracking each year to provide a baseline for detecting recurrence [1]. When the survey does not capture a specific distress, field visual inspections are required so that any un‑identified problems are documented and addressed [1]. Upon completion of the cracking repair, an immediate investigation is conducted together with the Representative to verify the type, source and extent of any remaining fissures [2]. The inspector records the width, depth and length of each crack, establishes the end locations relative to permanent reference points, and obtains core samples if probe measurements are insufficient [2]. Until the approved sealant is placed, the repaired surface is kept clean, covered and dry in accordance with the quality‑control plan reviewed by the Representative [2]. Only cracks deeper than one‑quarter inch and whose widths fall between .007 inch and .016 inch are accepted for repair [2]. A visual inspection of the seated pavement follows, during which the surface is wetted to make every crack visible on the top and the roller is observed for vertical deflection; the absence of movement confirms satisfactory seating [2]. Ongoing performance monitoring includes falling‑weight deflectometer testing at intervals of fifty feet or less across the treated lane, with results submitted to the Representative within seven days to confirm that the elastic modulus remains within the prescribed range [2]. After these checks the pavement is swept clean, a tack coat applied, and an asphalt leveling course placed within one week so that the area is not exposed to freezing conditions [2]. The treatment is documented in a service record containing a visual examination report noting any residual cracks or joint expansion, and representative core samples are taken for petrographic analysis according to ASTM C856 to detect microcracking or alkali‑silica gel formation [2]. Under the pavement warranty the segment is monitored through automated performance surveys supplemented by manual surveys performed as required by Publication 336; these surveys are conducted annually and more frequently if distress limits are approached, with the Department notifying the contractor of the results within fourteen days [2]. If a year‑five survey shows that thirty percent of the segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action is mandated [2]. The contractor may also perform nondestructive testing of the repaired area after providing seven days’ notice to the District Executive [2].

overlay and must be stabilized or patched before further work.[2] A pavement segment is classified as being in poor condition when at least two of its three distress components (cracked surface percentage, roughness/IRI, and rutting/faulting) are rated poor, and if more than five percent of Interstate lane‑miles fall into this category network‑level corrective actions are mandated to avoid funding penalties.[1] Routine crack sealing intervals are prescribed—every three to five years for asphalt and every five years for concrete—and patching is considered cost‑effective only up to roughly ten percent of the pavement area; exceeding these frequency or extent thresholds indicates that preservation has been exhausted and more extensive rehabilitation, such as micro‑surfacing or overlay, is required.[1] Performance surveys that reveal fatigue cracking, transverse or miscellaneous cracking, raveling, weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint or edge deterioration, loss of bond, or delamination constitute additional triggers for remedial action, and when such distresses are observed beyond warranty limits the pavement must be treated accordingly.[2][1] If at least thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity cracks, or if the total area affected by cracks—counting each medium‑severity crack as a six‑inch‑wide band on either side along its full length—exceeds twenty percent of the surface, the department mandates corrective treatment ranging from microsurfacing to full‑segment replacement.[2] When repair patches are spaced less than one hundred feet apart they must be combined into a continuous repair, and any failure to meet prescribed bond pull‑off strengths (average greater than 145 psi with no individual result below 100 psi) also triggers additional repair or replacement.[2] Premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, often linked to inadequate subbase material, is another indicator that preservation measures have been exceeded and that rehabilitation actions are necessary.[1]
