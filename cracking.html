Cracking

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Cracking is a topic under Distress.
cracking
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Cracking is a pavement distress that manifests as fissures or breakage on the surface or within structural layers of both asphalt and concrete pavements, appearing as slab fracture in concrete, reflective cracking when an overlay is placed over existing cracks, and alligator‑type patterns caused by age oxidation. It occurs at joints, transverse joints, punchouts, longitudinal cracks within lanes, or anywhere the slab interior experiences tensile stresses, and is triggered by construction deficiencies such as improper slabjacking, inadequate subbase material, oversized overlay panels, rapid temperature changes, moisture intrusion, freeze‑thaw cycling, or material aging. The presence of these fissures degrades the structural capacity and durability of the pavement, leading to differential movement, reflective cracking in subsequent overlays, and ultimately a poorer condition rating (good‑fair‑poor) in FHWA performance reporting. [1][2]

PennDOT publications covering “Cracking” also include the following subtopics: Crack Width, Diagonal Cracking, Fatigue Cracking, Longitudinal Crack, Mid-Slab Cracking, Reflective Cracking, Shrinkage Cracking, Slippage Cracking, Thermal Cracking, and Transverse Crack.

Description and identification

Cracking is a pavement distress that manifests as fissures or breakage either on the surface or within the structural layers of the pavement. [1] In concrete pavements it is described as slab fracture that renders the pavement too deteriorated for economical repair; the distress appears as fissures and breakage at existing joints, cracks and punchouts that produce free‑moving slab pieces. [1] When an asphalt overlay is placed over a previously cracked roadway, those underlying fissures can propagate upward, producing reflective cracking, while age‑related oxidation may generate an alligator pattern of interconnected surface cracks. [1] For performance monitoring the presence of such fissures on the pavement surface is quantified by measuring the percentage of the surface that is cracked, a metric that contributes to the good‑fair‑poor condition rating used in FHWA reporting. [1] Annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys also record cracking as fissures or breaks observable on the surface and, for jointed concrete pavements, may include premature mid‑slab cracking that can be caused by inadequate subbase material. [1] Routine maintenance addresses these manifestations: cyclic crack sealing of asphalt surfaces should be performed every 3 to 5 years, and joint sealing of concrete pavements should be done every 5 years. [1] In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, cracking appears as visible fissures on the pavement surface or within the slabs; longitudinal cracks may run across a lane and are addressed by removing and replacing the affected slab, while tied longitudinal contraction joints are sawed to limit random cracking. [2] For unbonded concrete overlays, cracking refers to fissures that develop away from the intended saw‑cut joints and such defects are considered defective; if a crack is found at or within 1.5 feet of a joint location, sawing shall be omitted. [2] In roller‑compacted concrete pavements, random cracking can develop on the surface prior to control‑joint sawing, appearing as unintended fissures across the slab and must be repaired before the saw‑cut operation. [2] Slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements intentionally creates continuous full‑depth fractures that extend across the entire width of a slab; the cracks remain visible on the top surface without causing extensive spalling, punched holes, or shattering so that aggregate interlock is preserved, and a minimum 1 000‑foot test section is required to verify these appearance criteria before production fracturing proceeds. [2] Within full‑depth reclamation, cracking describes shrinkage fissures that can develop inside the chemically stabilized reclaimed layer when the material’s unconfined compressive strength exceeds 900 psi; this internal tensile failure appears within the base course rather than on the surface and triggers a rework requirement. [2] In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, cracking appears as fissures on the slab surface or through its thickness and is characterized by opening width and depth relative to slab depth D; cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of D require full‑depth slab replacement, cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are repaired according to ACI 224R recommendations, and very fine cracks of 0.007 inches or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after cleaning. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

In the overlay context, cracking is recognized by examining its pattern and surrounding context, with reflective cracking identified when a new asphalt overlay is placed over an existing cracked roadway and alligator‑type cracking that extends across the entire surface of the pavement—not limited to wheel paths—interpreted as age oxidation rather than a structural deficiency. [1] Cracking is quantified by measuring the proportion of the pavement surface that exhibits fissures and reporting it as a cracking percentage, a metric that is treated separately from other distress components such as International Roughness Index, rut depth, and faulting elevation differences, thereby allowing it to be distinguished from those conditions. [1] Under the unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements, cracking is identified by visual inspection of fissures that are not aligned with the prescribed saw‑cut joints, and any crack occurring outside of the sawed joint lines is classified as defective; when a crack is found at or within 1.5 feet of a planned joint before or during sawing, the specification requires omitting the sawing operation and handling the panel according to Section 590. [2] In the Superpave mixture design and related construction specifications, cracking is identified during the mandatory spring evaluation by a manual survey in which inspectors specifically look for the characteristics of fatigue cracking, transverse cracking and miscellaneous cracking, and these crack types are listed separately from other distresses such as raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, or loss of bond/delamination, thus distinguishing true cracking from similar surface conditions. [2] For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, visible fissures that exceed a width of three‑quarters of an inch are taken as evidence of cracking and must be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout before the overlay is placed; in planning transverse joints, any crack found at or within 1.5 feet of the intended joint location triggers omission of sawing at that spot, thereby distinguishing those cracks from the regular joint pattern. [2] In slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements, cracking is identified by continuous full‑depth cracks that are clearly visible on the top surface of the fractured slab, with wetting the pavement sometimes used to assist visual inspection, and the distinction from other distresses is reinforced by prohibiting equipment that would cause extensive surface spalling, punched holes, or shattering of the concrete, ensuring that only true crack patterns—not spalled or shattered areas—are present. [2] In slab stabilization work, cracking is identified specifically as radial cracking and is quantified by its linear extent in increments of five linear feet; when the observed radial cracks reach each five‑foot segment, the Department reduces the grout stabilization pay item by one bag of cement, a treatment that separates this distress from other types not subject to the same payment adjustment. [2] In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, cracking is recognized as a surface defect that renders a lot defective only when it cannot be satisfactorily repaired according to the Representative’s judgment, and it is listed under “Other Conditions” together with rain damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, poor final finish and honeycombing, thereby distinguishing cracking from those other distresses by its inclusion in a separate defect category that requires replacement rather than repair. [2] For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, cracking is identified by measuring crack width and depth relative to the slab thickness D; a crack wider than 0.007 inches and extending deeper than one‑third of D triggers full‑slab replacement, while a crack wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than one‑third of D requires repair according to ACI 224R, and any crack 0.007 inches wide or less is sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate, criteria that distinguish true cracking from other surface distresses such as spalls, which are addressed separately. [2]

Where it occurs

Cracking is observed on Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavement slabs when slabjacking is performed improperly, potentially introducing additional damage such as cracking, tilting and faulting especially if grout extrudes from joints, cracks or the pavement edge before the target elevation is reached. [1] In rehabilitation of existing concrete pavements—specifically Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) and Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP)—cracking and breaking of the slabs into 12‑ to 24‑inch pieces is employed prior to placing a flexible asphalt overlay in order to reduce differential movement at cracks, joints and punchouts and to lessen reflective cracking through the new overlay; these operations are not required for unbonded concrete overlays. [1] Minor cracking is identified as a deficiency on asphalt pavements that can be corrected with an asphalt surface treatment or a scratch‑course/seal‑coat combination. [1] Low‑severity cracking is noted on the road surface of asphalt wearing surfaces, and Type A micro‑surfacing mix will be used to seal cracks and address shallow surface defects. [1] The underlying hot‑mix asphalt concrete surface may be susceptible to low temperature cracking, and sealing helps keep the asphalt concrete resilient to fatigue and low temperature cracking. [1] Reflective (alligator) cracking can appear across the surface of existing pavements regardless of material when new asphalt overlays are placed over already cracked roadways, and interlayer systems are intended to mitigate such cracking. [1] Cracking is encountered on existing PCC pavements considered for a bonded PCC overlay; bonded overlays should only be used where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting because significant distress would be reflected through the overlay if not repaired. [1] Performance measures record cracking as the percentage of pavement surface that is cracked and include it as one of three distress components for both asphalt (IRI, Cracking, Rutting) and concrete (IRI, Cracking, Faulting) pavements. [1] Cracking is recorded as a pavement‑surface distress for both asphalt‑surfaced pavements and jointed concrete pavements; premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material, routine crack sealing on asphalt‑surfaced pavements should be done every 3 to 5 years, and joint sealing on concrete pavements should be done every 5 years. [1] For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements any longitudinal crack that occurs within a lane requires removal and replacement of the affected slab at no additional cost to the Department. [2] Any crack or spall that appears before final inspection must be repaired in accordance with Table A for new concrete pavement. [2] When constructing adjacent concrete pavement, shoulders, or structures after pavement construction, the ends of all transverse joints and cracks are required to be sealed to prevent intrusion of cement mortar. [2] In unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements panels that exhibit cracking outside of the sawed joints are considered defective; if a crack is found at or within 1.5 feet of a joint location before or during sawing, the sawing operation must be omitted and the condition handled as specified in Section 590, and panel dimensions may be adjusted only to avoid shapes that would otherwise be subject to premature cracking, with nominal limits of no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect‑ratio not exceeding 1.5. [2] Roller‑compacted concrete pavements may develop random cracking before the saw‑cutting operation; such cracks must be repaired as directed by the Representative at no additional cost, control joints are to be saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted RCC pavement, and sawing must begin no later than eighteen hours after placement. [2] Asphalt concrete courses designed under the Superpave mixture specifications are surveyed in the spring according to Publication 336, with the Department evaluating fatigue cracking, transverse cracking and miscellaneous cracking. [2] For bonded concrete overlays placed over asphalt‑surfaced pavements cracks greater than three‑quarters of an inch in the existing asphalt surface must be cleaned and filled before placing the overlay, and any crack that occurs at or within 1.5 feet of a planned joint in the fresh concrete overlay requires omission of sawing and separate treatment as noted. [2] Slab‑fracturing operations on existing concrete pavements require equipment capable of producing continuous full‑depth cracks across the entire width while maintaining aggregate interlock and without causing extensive surface spalling; all resulting cracks must be visible on the top surface, and any overlying asphalt courses must be removed prior to fracturing. [2] Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications list cracking among the surface defects that render a concrete lot defective, and under the plain or reinforced cement‑concrete RPS specifications the presence of cracking that cannot be satisfactorily repaired also makes the lot defective, necessitating removal and replacement at no additional cost. [2] In full‑depth reclamation, chemically stabilized base layers may develop shrinkage cracking when the unconfined compressive strength exceeds nine hundred pounds per square inch, requiring rework. [2] Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs are considered cracked if a crack wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth is present, in which case the slab must be replaced at no additional cost; cracks wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than one‑third of the slab depth are to be repaired according to the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R), and cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are sealed using high molecular weight methacrylate. [2]

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, when slab‑jacking is performed improperly, cracking can develop within the concrete slab interior rather than at joints, corners, edges, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade. The same manual states that in rehabilitation of Portland cement concrete pavements, cracking is described as a surface distress that manifests at existing cracks, transverse joints and punch‑outs—that is, within the slab interior; the prescribed cracking/breaking and seating method fractures the slab into pieces roughly 12 to 24 inches long and seats them in the base or subbase before an overlay. For overlays, the manual indicates that cracking appears on the existing pavement surface, with reflective cracking occurring when a new asphalt overlay is placed over an old cracked roadway and alligator‑type cracking capable of extending across the entire surface rather than being confined to wheel paths. The Pavement Project Criteria section reports premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements as occurring within the slab interior—not at joints or edges—and may be linked to inadequate subbase material. In the specifications for rigid concrete pavements (Section 501), cracking may appear anywhere on the slab—including longitudinal cracks that develop within a lane and any other crack or spall that occurs before final inspection. The unbonded and bonded concrete overlay sections (Sections 548 and 540) note that a crack appearing at or within 1.5 ft of a joint location triggers omission of sawing, whereas cracks occurring elsewhere in the overlay panel are treated as defects located in the slab interior. The Superpave mixture design specifications (Section 413) evaluate fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking that develop within the pavement slab interior, together with joint and edge deterioration that indicates cracking at joints and along edges. The slab‑fracturing procedure for existing concrete pavements (Section 522) is intended to produce continuous full‑depth cracks that extend across the full width of the slab, so the distress occurs through the whole thickness of the slab interior; all resulting cracks must be visible on the top surface. For long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530), cracking is treated as a surface defect on the exposed pavement surface rather than at deeper layer interfaces or in the subgrade. [1][2]

Causes and mechanisms

The Pavement Policy Manual notes that improper slabjacking, a construction‑related deficiency, may cause additional damage to the slab such as cracking, tilting, or faulting, and therefore recommends performing slab lifting in increments to minimize slab stresses and avoid cracking. Low‑temperature effects are identified as a climate‑related cause of fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, and the use of an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is said to reduce oxidation and weathering, thereby helping the pavement remain resilient to such cracking. Material aging, specifically age oxidation, is linked to alligator‑type cracking that extends across the entire surface of the roadway. Moisture‑related mechanisms—including water intrusion into the structural section and freeze‑thaw cycling—are identified as major contributors to crack propagation. Inadequate subbase material is cited as a materials or construction deficiency that may cause premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements. Within SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY, the specification attributes cracking primarily to design and construction deficiencies; it limits panel aspect ratio to near 1.0 and not above 1.5, caps maximum panel size at 18 times the overlay thickness, and requires that the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface does not exceed a 40 °F rise in any one‑hour period. The same section mandates omission of sawing if a crack occurs at or within 1.5 ft of a joint before or during sawing, indicating that improper panel geometry, joint layout, and rapid thermal gradients are the primary causes addressed. In SECTION 506—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, cracking appears among surface defects that render a lot defective when it originates from rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures or an improper final finish, thereby attributing cracking to moisture and to design‑ or construction‑related deficiencies rather than to traffic loading. SECTION 344—FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION identifies shrinkage cracking as a potential distress when chemically stabilized material attains an unconfined compressive strength greater than 900 psi, at which point rework is required. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93), Ch. 5 (pp. 107–120), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 389–474)

Cracking in concrete slabs during slabjacking develops when the lifting operation imposes tensile stresses that exceed the slab’s capacity; if the lift is not performed incrementally, these excessive stresses can produce fissures, and the distress is accelerated by improper procedures such as lifting too quickly or without careful control, as well as by high‑temperature conditions that cause slab expansion and place joints in compression [1]. Premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material that permits slab movement and joint opening [1]. In unbonded concrete overlays, cracks tend to initiate when stresses exceed the slab’s capacity, which occurs if panel dimensions are larger than allowed—no more than 18 times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5—and also when rapid cooling creates temperature gradients greater than 40 °F within any one‑hour period; cracks that appear outside the prescribed sawed joints are deemed defective [2]. The mechanism is accelerated by oversized panels, improper joint spacing or layout, and insufficient insulation during curing that allows a temperature change exceeding the 40 °F per hour limit, all of which promote random cracking if not controlled [2]. Reflective cracking progresses in new asphalt overlays placed over an existing cracked roadway, while alligator cracking may arise from age oxidation of the pavement; water intrusion into the structural section can promote crack propagation, and freeze‑thaw cycles cause expansion and contraction of moisture within the pavement [1]. The progression is accelerated by the presence of widespread existing cracks, inadequate drainage that allows moisture infiltration, and harsh environments with repeated freezing and thawing [1]. In chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed layers, shrinkage cracking may result when the material develops an unconfined compressive strength that exceeds 900 psi, because excessive strength induces tensile stresses during drying and curing; specimens testing above this threshold trigger rework [2]. Maintaining the prescribed strength range of 300–500 psi for most overlays and avoiding over‑stabilization are essential to prevent crack initiation and propagation [2].

Severity levels and rating

Within the micro‑surfacing project‑selection criteria of the Pavement Policy Manual, only a single severity designation for cracking is employed, namely low severity cracking, which is stipulated as a required surface condition before any treatment such as Type A crack sealing may be applied; the manual does not provide additional categories, numeric thresholds, counts or dimensional limits for that level [1]. Conversely, the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs define three distinct repair actions based on measured crack width and depth relative to slab thickness D. Cracks whose width exceeds 0.007 inches and whose depth is greater than one‑third of the slab thickness are required to be replaced in their entirety at no additional cost to the Department [2]. Cracks that exceed a width of 0.007 inches but have a depth less than one‑third of the slab thickness must be repaired in accordance with the latest edition of Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R) after an approved repair procedure is submitted [2]. Finally, cracks whose measured width is 0.007 inches or less are to be sealed using a high molecular weight methacrylate sealant [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 105); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

In rehabilitation projects the severity rating for cracking is drawn from Publication 408, Specifications, Section 522, which treats cracking that has progressed to a point where it is no longer economical to patch the pavement as a severe level of distress. The FHWA Interstate Management Program Guidelines (Chapter 13, Section 13.4) provide a performance‑measure rating procedure that quantifies the percentage of surface that is cracked and translates that value into a condition score of good, fair, or poor; the source’s criterion specifically cites the “poor” severity level, noting that a segment is deemed in poor condition when two or more of its three distress components—including cracking—are rated as poor. For slip‑form concrete pavements the longitudinal‑joint procedure set out in Section 501.3(j) of the Rigid Pavements chapter rates any crack that has opened at least 1/8‑inch as a severity condition that requires elimination of a tie bar adjacent to the joint and may lead to slab replacement. The performance survey prescribed by Publication 336 supplies the condition rating for fatigue, transverse or miscellaneous cracking; when such cracking is observed the Department evaluates whether the work meets acceptance criteria or constitutes an unacceptable condition that triggers remedial action. Under the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications, Section 530.3(t) Defective Work assigns a condition rating of “defective” to any pavement that contains cracking which cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative, obligating removal and replacement of the affected panel. Similarly, the Rigid Pavement Specification’s Section 506.3(t) Defective Work rates a pavement as ‘defective’ when surface cracks are judged unreparable, and once rated defective the specification mandates removal and replacement of at least 10 ft between transverse joints or an entire panel. Section 529.3 references ACI 224R Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures to classify cracking severity; cracks wider than 0.007 inches with a depth greater than one‑third the slab thickness (D/3) are deemed severe enough to require full‑depth slab replacement, whereas cracks of the same width but with a depth less than D/3 are considered less severe and are repaired according to the ACI recommendations. Measurement, survey and extent

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (section 13.4 Performance Measures > Pavement Condition), cracking is quantified by determining the percentage of the pavement surface that is cracked; this distress component is used in FHWA performance reporting and the measurement therefore consists of a surface‑area assessment expressed as a percent of the total pavement area [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation slab‑stabilization specification (section 679.4 Measurement and Payment), the amount of cracking is quantified by its linear extent, with a reduction of one bag of cement for every five linear feet of radial cracking identified; the measurement consists of a site count of crack length expressed in 5‑ft increments and no specific instrument or test is cited for taking this measurement [2].

Effects on performance

In rehabilitation where a cracked concrete pavement is broken, the slab is reduced to 12‑ to 24‑inch pieces that are rolled into the base or subbase before an overlay; this provides a level of support greater than a conventional subbase layer and therefore adds structural capacity. The reduction in size of free‑moving slabs minimizes differential movement at cracks, joints and punchouts, which reduces the occurrence and severity of reflective cracking through the new overlay, thereby improving durability and extending service life. Conversely, when an existing concrete pavement exhibits a great extent of cracking or faulting, a bonded PCC overlay should be avoided because the underlying distress will be reflected up through the new slab if it is not repaired, compromising the composite structural capacity and shortening the service life of the overlay; consequently, significant cracking reduces durability of the rehabilitated section by allowing existing flaws to continue influencing performance. No ride‑quality or safety effects are noted. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 183)

Under section 4.9 Rehabilitation, when cracks are left untreated concrete slabs can become free‑moving, which leads to slab rocking, pumping and faulting and causes differential movement at existing cracks, joints and punchouts [1]. That differential movement then manifests as reflective cracking in any later overlay placed over the cracked pavement [1]. The progression may require more intensive rehabilitation such as cracking/breaking and seating, a process that reduces the pavement to 12‑to‑24‑inch pieces and rolls them into the base or subbase before an overlay, or even rubblizing if conditions are severe [1]. Untreated surface cracks also permit water to infiltrate the pavement’s structural section, which is identified as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw‑induced deterioration [1]. When an overlay is later placed over such cracked pavement, the existing fissures commonly generate reflective cracking in the new wearing surface, further reducing the effectiveness of the rehabilitation [1]. Bonded PCC overlays are designed to function as a single monolithic slab and should only be used where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if significant distress remains unrepaired it will be reflected up through the bonded overlay, causing the same cracks and related damage to appear in the new layer [1]. According to Section 11.b Cracks of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Section 529.3 Construction), if a fissure widens beyond 0.007 inches and its depth exceeds one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) the slab must be replaced at no additional cost to the Department [2]. If the width also exceeds 0.007 inches but the depth is less than D/3, the crack should be repaired following the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R) recommendations [2]. For very fine cracks of 0.007 inches or less, sealing with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate and a sand finish is required before traffic can resume [2].

Repairs and treatments

Slabjacking, described in Section 4.6 of the Pavement Policy Manual, is used to fill voids beneath concrete slabs and raise them to the proper profile, thereby restoring structural integrity; it may be applied when correcting an extensive profile deficiency and only when the slab is in compression, being impractical during high temperatures that cause expansion, and must be carried out in incremental lifts to keep slab stresses low and avoid inducing new cracking, with dowel‑bar retrofits normally paired to reestablish load transfer on settled slabs that have lost support, which can reduce faulting and associated cracking [1]. Cracking/breaking and seating, presented in Section 4.9 of the Pavement Policy Manual, attempts to achieve a stable, uniform level of support by breaking the concrete pavement into 12‑to‑24 inch pieces and rolling them into the base; it is applied when the concrete pavement is distressed to such an extent that patching is no longer economical, addressing extensive cracking, joints and punchouts while reducing differential movement and reflective cracking in a subsequent overlay [1]. Rubblizing, also covered in Section 4.9 of the Pavement Policy Manual, reduces the concrete to 8‑ or 12‑inch pieces before rolling them into the base and is prescribed for badly distressed pavements—often with poor subgrade or moisture problems—and thus addresses severe cracking by providing a uniform support layer for an overlay [1]. Seal coats, slurry seals, and other surface treatments listed in Section 5.2 of the Pavement Policy Manual are intended to correct minor cracking (along with minor rutting and loss of fine aggregate); they may be used on non‑Interstate roads but shall not be placed on any pavement in need of structural upgrading, and seal coats, slurry seals, and surface treatments must not be used on the Interstate system [1]. Micro‑Surfacing Type A, described in Section 5.4 of the Pavement Policy Manual, directly targets cracking; it will be used to seal cracks, fill voids, and shallow (less than ½ inch) ruts, and a roadway may be chosen for a micro‑surfacing project only when the surface exhibits low severity cracking [1]. The Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course (UTWC), covered in Section 5.5 of the Pavement Policy Manual, is applied to address low temperature cracking; by sealing the pavement it reduces oxidation and weathering of the surface, allowing the pavement to remain resilient to fatigue and low temperature cracking, so the treatment applies when a surface requires protection from oxidation‑induced cracking [1]. An asphalt paving interlayer—offered as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids—in Section 5.12 of the Pavement Policy Manual is specified to mitigate reflective cracking that occurs when a new overlay is placed over an existing cracked roadway and to prevent alligator‑type age‑oxidation cracking from propagating through the new wearing surface; it may be applied only when the cracking present is not associated with a lack of structural capacity, with selection also considering moisture, climate, and traffic conditions as outlined in the interlayer guidance [1]. A bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlay, covered in Section 10.6 of the Pavement Policy Manual, addresses cracking in an existing concrete pavement by being applied only where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if there is a significant amount of distress it must be repaired before the overlay because otherwise the distress will propagate through the new slab [1]. Routine‑maintenance crack sealing for asphalt‑surfaced pavements and joint sealing for concrete pavements are listed in Section 13.7 of the Pavement Policy Manual; crack sealing should be done every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing every 5 years, and both are classified as actions intended to repair or retard existing distress [1]. In slip‑form concrete pavements, any crack or spall observed before final inspection must be repaired according to Table A, Repair Procedures for Deficiency in New Concrete Pavement, at no additional cost to the Department; if a longitudinal crack develops within a lane, the cracked slab(s) must be removed and replaced, also at no additional cost (Section 501.3(g)) [2]. In unbonded concrete overlays, controlled saw cutting is prescribed as the sole acceptable method for creating overlay joints (Section 548.3(i)), thereby mitigating random cracking by establishing predetermined joint locations; if a crack is found at or within 1.5 ft of a planned joint location before or during sawing, the saw cut must be omitted and the condition referred to Section 590 for repair [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements, control joints are to be saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth wherever indicated on the plans, with cutting performed behind the rollers as soon as possible but no later than eighteen hours after placement (Section 502.3(a)); should random cracks appear before the saw‑cutting operation, they must be repaired under the Representative’s direction at no additional cost [2]. In extended‑season asphalt courses, a spring manual survey evaluates fatigue, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, and when such cracking is deemed defective the contractor must remove and replace or otherwise repair the defective work at no additional cost; cracked areas spaced less than 100 ft apart are to be repaired as a single continuous repair (Section 413.3) [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements, cracks in the existing asphalt that exceed three‑quarters inch must be cleaned and filled with a non‑shrink grout or other Department‑approved material before surface milling and overlay placement; additionally, when sawing joints in the fresh overlay any crack at or within 1.5 ft of a planned joint location obliges the contractor to skip the saw cut and treat the condition as directed in Section 590 (Section 540.3) [2]. For existing concrete pavements with full‑depth slab cracking, two fracture‑and‑seat repairs—Break and Seat and Crack and Seat—are prescribed (Section 522.3); Break and Seat requires sawcutting slabs longer than 20 ft to a depth of approximately one inch (±1 in) of two‑thirds slab depth, while Crack and Seat does not require sawcutting of transverse or longitudinal joints unless directed, and both methods may be employed only after a test section confirms an acceptable fracture pattern, the equipment meets the variable‑force requirement, and they are prohibited within 10 ft horizontally and vertically of culverts, cross pipes, utility sleeves, within 40 ft of structures, or when sonic vibratory equipment would cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade (Section 522.3) [2]. Radial cracking is treated by Cement Grout Stabilization, for which the Department reduces the pay item by one bag of cement for each five linear feet of radial cracking as defined in Section 679.3(a)6 (Section 679.4) [2]. When surface defects including cracking cannot be satisfactorily repaired on reinforced cement‑concrete pavements, the specification requires removal and replacement of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints; on plain cement‑concrete panels the entire panel must be replaced (Section 506.3(t)) [2]. For any existing pavement where cracks or joints exceed 1/8 inch in width, preparation for a new wearing course calls for filling and sealing those openings with PennDOT‑approved fillers and sealants (Section 413.3(g)); this crack‑sealing treatment addresses wider surface cracking prior to overlay placement [2]. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, cracks wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth must be addressed by replacing the entire slab at no additional cost; cracks wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than one‑third of the slab depth are to be repaired in accordance with ACI 224R after acceptance of the repair procedure; cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are sealed with high molecular weight methacrylate applied per the manufacturer’s instructions, then sand‑blasted before traffic is allowed (Section 529.3) [2].

Prevention in design and construction

Incremental slabjacking lifts are prescribed so that slab stresses remain limited and cracking is avoided; this measure applies to any lift operation but shall not be attempted when pavement temperature is high because thermal expansion places joints in compression (section 4.6 Slabjack) [1]. Bonded jointed plain concrete‑pavement (JPCP) overlays add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress, including cracking, and are recommended for pavements that are in good to fair condition with a stable subgrade and functional drainage (section 4.9 C 1) [1]. When an existing concrete pavement is so distressed that patching is uneconomic, the cracking/breaking technique reduces slabs to 12‑24 inch pieces, limiting differential movement at cracks, joints and punchouts and thereby reducing the occurrence and severity of reflective cracking in the subsequent overlay (section 4.9 E) [1]. For asphalt overlays placed over concrete, saw‑cutting and sealing all underlying transverse and patch joints is required as a preparation that mitigates reflective cracking through the new layer (section 4.9 D) [1]. Seal coats, slurry seals and other surface treatments may be used to mitigate minor cracking, but they must not be applied on the Interstate system and shall only be placed where the pavement does not require structural upgrading; eligibility is decided according to Table 5.1 (section 5.2) [1]. Micro‑surfacing projects are limited to pavements exhibiting low‑severity cracking; a double application is recommended unless the road surface is in excellent condition, and on Interstate routes a double application of Type A SRL‑E is advised even when the surface is excellent, with the Type A mix sealing cracks, filling voids and shallow ruts less than ½ inch (section 5.4) [1]. An ultra‑thin bonded wearing course—comprised of a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane overlaid with a thin hot‑mix asphalt concrete in one pass—will seal the pavement, reduce oxidation and weathering, and thereby keep the surface resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking; this measure applies only when the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is placed as described (section 5.5) [1]. Asphalt paving interlayers such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid or composite reinforcing grid can mitigate reflective cracking by retarding crack reflection and providing tensile reinforcement; they are intended for overlays over pavements that exhibit alligator cracking caused by age oxidation rather than structural deficiency, and also act as a moisture barrier when surface or subsurface drainage is good or will be upgraded (section 5.12) [1]. A bonded PCC overlay forms a monolithic slab that prevents upward transmission of cracks; it should be used only where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking and/or faulting, because significant distress would otherwise be reflected through the overlay (section 10.6) [1]. Cyclic crack sealing of asphalt‑surfaced pavements every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every 5 years are routine maintenance design measures that repair or retard cracking; both activities receive emphasis in the Interstate system treatment matrices (section 13.7) [1]. In slip‑form cement concrete pavements, timely saw‑cutting of transverse joints after the concrete has hardened enough to avoid excessive raveling is required to prevent random cracking, and continuous transverse joints placed perpendicular to the centreline across the full width serve the same purpose (section 501) [2]. Removal of one longitudinal tie bar adjacent to any joint where a crack has opened 1/8 inch or greater reduces shear cracking, and from October 1 to April 1 an additional tie bar shall be omitted next to dominant joints for further limitation of cracking; new dominant joints must also be aligned with existing joints when adjoining pavements are placed so that joint development is coordinated and uncontrolled cracking is avoided (section 501) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, panel dimensions are limited so that the aspect ratio does not exceed 1.5 and the longest dimension is no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness; saw cuts must penetrate at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of edges and at least one‑quarter depth elsewhere, thereby confining cracks to joint lines (section 548) [2]. Joint spacing shall follow the project plans or an approved layout, longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths to prevent load‑induced cracking, and sawing may be performed only after the concrete is hardened enough for cutting without excessive raveling; any crack located within 1.5 ft of a planned joint precludes that cut (section 548) [2]. Curing material must be applied within thirty minutes of bleed water dissipation and insulating blankets are required when air temperature is 40 °F or less, with removal only after temperatures exceed 40 °F or concrete strength exceeds 3,600 psi and at a rate not exceeding a 40 °F change per hour to prevent thermal cracking (section 548) [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements, saw‑cut control joints are to be placed as shown on the plans, cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted pavement behind the rolling operation as soon as possible and no later than eighteen hours after placement; this measure is aimed at preventing random cracking (section 502) [2]. Bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements require cleaning and filling of pre‑existing fissures wider than three‑quarters inch with non‑shrink grout before overlay placement to reduce cracking; panel size limits identical to those in the unbonded overlay section are imposed, longitudinal joints in wheel paths are prohibited, early saw cutting is mandated as soon as the concrete can be cut without excessive raveling, and insulating blankets must be used when ambient temperature is 45 °F or less, with removal only when surface temperature changes remain below forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour to protect against rapid thermal gradients (section 540) [2]. The slab‑fracturing specifications for existing concrete pavements call for impact equipment capable of generating a variable force that creates continuous full‑depth cracks while preserving aggregate interlock and expressly forbid tools that cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes or shatter the concrete; sonic vibratory equipment may be used only when fracturing will not induce soil liquefaction (section 522) [2]. Prior to fracturing, all existing slabs must be saw‑cut to a maximum length of 20 feet across the lane width, and fracturing is prohibited within ten feet horizontally or vertically of culverts, cross pipes, utility sleeves, within forty feet of bridge approaches, and within three feet of any fixed appurtenance to prevent crack‑induced damage to adjacent structures (section 522) [2]. After cracking, a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller must seat the broken slabs with at least three passes for the Crack‑and‑Seat method or five passes for the Break‑and‑Seat method; vibratory rollers are not allowed, and verification testing is permitted only when pavement is unfrozen and air temperature lies between 45 °F and 90 °F to avoid thermal cracking (section 522) [2]. Chemically‑stabilized full‑depth reclamation must achieve a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi for thin overlays or 280–500 psi for thicker overlays, and the strength may not exceed 900 psi because values above that threshold may cause shrinkage cracking (section 344) [2]. For thin asphalt overlay courses, all existing pavement cracks or joints wider than 1/8 inch must be filled and sealed before placement to limit crack propagation; paving is prohibited when surface or air temperature is 50 °F or lower (or 40 °F if a weather‑limit exception is granted) to mitigate thermal cracking, pneumatic rollers are banned, steel‑wheeled rollers are required, and roller speed, pattern, size and optional vibration must be adjusted to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage while the mix remains hot (section 412) [2]. In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, any slab exhibiting a crack wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness shall be replaced; cracks wider than 0.007 inch but shallower than one‑third of the thickness are to be repaired in accordance with the latest ACI 224R recommendations after approval, and very fine cracks of 0.007 inch or less are sealed with high molecular weight methacrylate before opening to traffic (section 529) [2].

Tied longitudinal contraction joints shall be sawed after tie‑bars are placed, also to prevent random cracking (Section 501.3(j)) [2]. The first saw cuts must be aligned with the marked dominant joints of adjacent lanes, shoulders or structures so that joint development is encouraged and remaining joints are cut before uncontrolled cracking occurs (Section 501.3(i)) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, saw cuts shall be at least D/3 within 12 inches of pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere; the joints shall then be cleaned and sealed to prevent random cracking, and this applies whenever sawing is performed after the concrete has hardened enough to avoid excessive raveling (Section 548.3(i)6) [2]. Panel layouts shall keep aspect ratios no greater than 1.5 and overall dimensions not to exceed 18 times the overlay thickness; this requirement avoids premature cracking caused by overly large or oddly shaped panels (Section 548.3(i)5) [2]. Early‑entry saws and continuous day/night sawing regardless of weather are required to cut joints at the proper time, again to prevent random cracking in the overlay (Section 548.3(i)9) [2]. Curing materials shall be applied within 30 minutes of bleed water dissipation and insulating blankets provided when air temperature is 40 °F or less; removal rates must keep surface‑temperature change below 40 °F per hour, a regimen aimed at preventing thermal cracking of the concrete overlay (Section 548.3(l)1) [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete slabs, control joints shall be saw‑cut to one‑quarter the pavement thickness behind the rollers and no later than eighteen hours after placement, a practice intended to prevent random cracking (Section 502.3(j)4) [2]. Compaction shall continue until a smooth, flat surface free of tearing and cracking is obtained, thereby addressing any cracking that could arise from inadequate rolling (Section 502.3(h)1) [2]. In bonded concrete overlays, existing cracks wider than three‑quarters inch shall be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or an approved material before surface cleaning, reducing the likelihood of new cracking propagating from untreated fissures (Section 540.3(e)) [2]. Longitudinal joints shall be omitted from wheel paths, and panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness nor have an aspect ratio greater than 1.5; this layout is intended to avoid stress concentrations that cause premature cracking, with saw cutting of joints performed as soon as the concrete is hard enough to prevent random cracking (Section 540.3(i)) [2]. Insulating blankets shall be applied when air temperature is 45 °F or less and removed only after temperature rises above 45 °F and compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi; temperature changes must not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour, ensuring that thermal movements do not produce defective cracking (Section 540.3(l)) [2]. During slab fracturing, impact equipment whose force and point of impact are controlled shall be used to produce continuous full‑depth cracks while retaining aggregate interlock, and equipment that would cause extensive surface spalling, punched holes or shattering shall not be used, thereby preventing uncontrolled cracking during fracturing (Section 522.3(a)) [2]. All overlying asphalt and joint seals shall be removed and slabs saw‑cut to a maximum length of 20 ft (about one‑inch depth for Break‑and‑Seat) before fracture; this pre‑defining of fracture lines limits random crack propagation (Section 522.3(b)) [2]. Fracturing shall not occur within 10 ft of utilities or 40 ft of structures, and panels must be saw‑cut into small segments to avoid cracking that could damage underground facilities (Section 522.3(b)) [2]. After fracturing, the slab shall be seated with a minimum‑50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller using at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat; vibratory rollers or wet conditions are prohibited, preventing post‑fracture re‑cracking (Section 522.3(d)) [2]. Chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed material that attains a 7‑day unconfined compressive strength greater than nine hundred pounds per square inch may develop shrinkage cracking; therefore rework is required when average strengths exceed this limit to prevent shrinkage cracking (Section 344.3(e)(g)2) [2]. For thin asphalt overlays, all cracks wider than one‑eighth inch shall be filled and sealed before placement, preventing existing pavement cracks from propagating through the overlay (Section 412.3(g)) [2]. Placement shall not occur when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower; if a weather‑limit exception is granted, a minimum surface temperature of 50 °F and an air temperature of 40 °F must be strictly enforced to avoid low‑temperature cracking (Section 412.3(b)) [2]. Compaction shall begin immediately after placement with steel‑wheeled rollers; vibratory rolling is prohibited unless it can be shown that no aggregate breakage or binder flushing occurs, and roller speed, pattern and size shall be adjusted to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage (Sections 412.3(i) and 412.3(f)) [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

In the unbonded concrete overlay specification (Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, clause 548.3(i)6), any cracking that occurs outside of the sawed joints is deemed defective; this condition is evaluated by a visual inspection performed by the Representative. For slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements (Section 522—Slab Fracturing of Existing Concrete Pavements, clause 522.3), the specification requires that all cracks be visible on the top surface of the fractured pavement and compliance is judged by a visual inspection after the fracturing operation; no numeric distress threshold is provided. In the slab stabilization provisions (Section 679—Slab Stabilization, measurement and payment clause 679.4), cracking is quantified by measuring radial cracks in linear feet, and for each five linear feet of radial cracking identified the Department reduces the pay item by one bag of cement; this reduction constitutes the acceptance limit for cracking under that section. Under the long‑life concrete pavement specifications (Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, clause 530.3(t) “Other Conditions”), a lot is considered defective when any surface cracking is present that cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative; no numeric threshold is stipulated and unrepaired cracking triggers replacement of the affected panel. The plain or reinforced cement concrete pavement specifications (Section 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, clause 506.3(t) “Defective Work”) similarly treat surface cracks that cannot be satisfactorily repaired as a defect, requiring removal and replacement of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints or an entire panel; the determination is made by the Representative without a numeric crack severity limit. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, clause 529.3), any crack wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth (D/3) must be replaced at no additional cost to the Department; a crack wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 is to be repaired in accordance with the latest edition of ACI 224R after an approved procedure is submitted; cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate. In Section 501.3(g) of the Slip‑Form Paving Method, any crack or spall observed before final inspection must be repaired in accordance with Table A, Repair Procedures for Deficiency in New Concrete Pavement, and the repair is to be performed at no additional cost to the Department; if a longitudinal crack occurs within a lane, the contractor must remove and replace the slab(s) containing that crack, also at no additional cost, and the specification does not prescribe a specific time limit for completing these actions. In Section 413.3 of the Superpave mixture design specifications, when the spring‑season manual survey determines that cracking exceeds the acceptable limits, the Department notifies the contractor that remedial action is required; the contractor must then remove and replace or repair the defective work at no additional cost, may combine repairs into a single continuous patch when damaged areas are less than 100 feet apart, must satisfy the surface‑tolerance criteria of Section 413.3(l), and all such repairs must be finished before a Final Acceptance Certificate can be issued for the extended‑season paving. In Section 530.3(t) dealing with Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, if cracking is judged by the Representative to render the lot defective under the “Other Conditions” clause, the specification directs removal and replacement of at least an entire LLCP panel as written instruction from the District Executive pursuant to Section 110.10(d)1; no time frame for completion is stated. In Section 506.3(t) for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, when cracking is identified as a surface defect that cannot be satisfactorily repaired and the Representative declares the lot defective under Section 506.3(t), the contractor must, unless otherwise directed in writing by the District Executive per Section 110.10(d)1, remove and replace a minimum of ten feet of pavement between transverse joints on reinforced cement concrete or an entire panel of plain cement concrete, with no explicit time limit. In Section 529.3(f)11.b for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs, any crack wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth requires replacement of the slab at no additional cost to the Department; cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than one‑third of the slab depth must be repaired in accordance with the latest edition of ACI 224R after the contractor submits an approved repair procedure for acceptance; and cracks 0.007 inches wide or less are to be sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate (and sanded) before traffic is opened, with the specification providing no explicit time limit for these actions.
