Fatigue Cracking

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Fatigue Cracking is a topic under Cracking.
fatigue-crackingalligator-crackingalligator-cracksfatigue-cracksbottom-up-fatigue-cracking
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Fatigue cracking is a distress that appears as cracks in asphalt pavements and is identified during performance surveys of flexible pavement systems, including cold‑recycled asphalt base courses[1][2]. The warranty specification treats fatigue cracking as an indicator of a deficient base material rather than traffic loading, climate, moisture, or design deficiencies, implying that the underlying base condition is unacceptable[2]. When detected, it is rated at low severity and, if at least 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more such distresses, it triggers base‑condition procedures—including coring, laboratory testing, and ultimately remedial action such as microsurfacing—before any repair decision is made[2].

Where it occurs

In the discussion of cold‑recycled asphalt base courses, fatigue cracking is identified on asphalt pavements; the manual notes that asphalt pavements with fatigue cracks can be successfully recycled [1]. The 60‑month warranty specification for asphalt concrete pavement lists fatigue cracking among the distresses that performance surveys may detect on the warranted flexible pavement, and when such cracking is observed it triggers base‑condition procedures that require coring and laboratory testing of the affected area before any remediation decision is made [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Causes and mechanisms

In the warranty specification fatigue cracking is mentioned as a distress that may indicate an unacceptable base condition, so the probable cause assigned to it is a deficient base material rather than traffic loading, climate, moisture, or design deficiencies. The excerpt does not include any table that classifies fatigue cracking by load‑related versus climate‑ or materials‑related causes; only Table A is cited for distress limits without showing its content. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Severity levels and rating

Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty (Section 496.3(c)), fatigue cracking and other distresses are to be identified, measured and reported using the rating procedures set out in Publication 336, and a performance trigger is defined as when the year‑5 survey shows that at least 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more distresses with low severity; therefore the source assigns a low‑severity level for fatigue cracking. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Measurement, survey and extent

Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty, fatigue cracking is identified during the annual performance surveys that follow Publication 336 procedures; when a survey flags fatigue cracking, the specification requires drilling a series of 6‑inch diameter cores within the cracked area and having the inspector record the depth of each core, so the measured quantity is core depth obtained with standard coring equipment and a direct depth measurement by the inspector. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty the Department determines fatigue‑cracking performance primarily through automated surveys that identify and measure distresses in accordance with Publication 336; manual surveys are used only when required and follow the same publication, while nondestructive testing may be performed by the contractor after prior notice. Surveys are conducted annually throughout the warranty period, with additional surveys permitted if evidence shows criteria are not being met. The pavement is divided into Segments of about one‑half mile, and the extent of fatigue cracking is reported as a percentage of the segment area, for example a year‑5 survey that finds 30 % of a segment’s area containing two or more low‑severity distresses triggers remediation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Repairs and treatments

Cold recycling of asphalt base courses is presented as a treatment that can remediate fatigue cracking by destroying the existing crack pattern and producing a crack‑free layer for a new surface course, and it is described in section 5.13 COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSES > C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures [1]. The manual specifies that this approach is appropriate when the cracked pavement rests on a structurally sound, well‑drained base and subgrade, when at least two inches of existing asphalt are present, when daily traffic does not exceed 200 ESALs, and when traffic can be controlled or detoured during construction and curing [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty in Section 496.3(c), if a year‑5 performance survey determines that at least thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses—including fatigue cracking—remedial action must be taken by microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment [2]. Subsection 4.a (Base Condition) further states that when performance surveys detect fatigue cracking that may indicate an unacceptable base condition, the contractor is not held responsible for repairing that fatigue cracking provided a series of six‑inch diameter cores confirm the pavement meets minimum thickness, density greater than ninety‑two percent of theoretical maximum density and mix‑design tolerances; in such cases the Department may address the distress with routine repair techniques limited to square or rectangular patches (except allowable crack sealing) as prescribed in Table A [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

For cold recycling, as described in Chapter 5 Section 5.13 of the Asphalt Concrete Pavement Guidelines and Policies, fatigue‑cracked asphalt pavements may be treated provided the underlying base and subgrade are structurally sound and well drained; under those conditions the existing crack pattern is destroyed and a crack‑free layer can be placed. If the base or drainage is poor, cold recycling should not be attempted. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

Prevention in design and construction

Cold recycling is presented as a design measure that eliminates fatigue cracking by destroying the existing crack pattern and providing a new crack‑free surface layer; this applies to asphalt pavements that exhibit fatigue cracks (as well as transverse thermal, reflection cracks and raveling) provided the underlying base and subgrade are structurally sound and well drained and the project meets the selection criteria outlined in Section 5.13 D (for example, projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected, a minimum of 2 inches of existing asphalt is required, poor drainage disqualifies recycling, and traffic must be controllable or detoured during construction). [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

The source advises employing the cold‑recycling process as a construction practice to prevent further fatigue cracking; by destroying the existing crack pattern and laying a new crack‑free layer it directly addresses fatigue cracks, and this is recommended only for pavements that already exhibit fatigue cracking but rest on structurally sound, well‑drained bases and subgrades (Section D of Chapter 5, 5.13) and where project selection criteria such as limiting heavy truck traffic (i.e., not exceeding 200 daily ESALs) and ensuring traffic can be controlled or detoured during the base construction and curing period are met. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

Specification limits and acceptance

When the fatigue‑cracking limit in Table A is exceeded, the contractor must carry out the remediation prescribed in that table—typically microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment—and must complete the work within sixty calendar days after receiving the Department’s written notice; the Department notifies the contractor of the need for remediation and the contractor is responsible for performing it. The excerpt does not provide individual row remedies for fatigue cracking. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)
