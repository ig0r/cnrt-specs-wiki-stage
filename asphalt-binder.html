Asphalt Binder

Part of: Asphalt Concrete (HMA) is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Asphalt binder is a performance‑graded liquid or cementitious material that, when mixed with aggregate, forms the binding matrix of asphalt pavement structures, enabling the mix to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking under projected traffic loads[1]. In the Superpave system it is specified as a PG‑Binder—typically one of three standard grades (PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22)—and may be “bumped” to a higher high‑temperature grade to increase stiffness during summer conditions[1]. The binder must satisfy the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) performance criteria of AASHTO M 332, be maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F during placement to remain tacky, and forms a continuous film that coats aggregate particles, providing adhesion, moisture resistance, and durability across a wide range of pavement applications[2].

PennDOT publications covering “Asphalt Binder” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Rubber, Emulsified Asphalt, Performance Grade (PG) Binder, and Polymer-Modified Binder.

Asphalt Rubber and Polymer-Modified Binder serve as specialized high-viscosity variants that enhance stiffness and elastic recovery for heavy traffic applications, while Emulsified Asphalt provides a water-based alternative for surface treatments and cold recycling. [1][2] Materials function within the Performance Grade (PG) Binder framework to ensure temperature-dependent resistance against rutting and thermal cracking across diverse pavement structures. [1][2]

Material purpose and applicability

Asphalt binder is a performance‑graded liquid or cementitious material that, when combined with aggregate, provides the binding matrix for asphalt pavement structures. The Pavement Policy Manual describes the binder in the Superpave system as the PG‑Binder, a performance‑graded material that together with aggregate enables a paving course to be designed to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking under projected traffic loads; only three standard PG‑Binders—PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, and PG 64E‑22—are to be specified for both Conventional Pavement Courses and Superpave Pavement Courses, and the “bumping” practice is intended to provide a stiffer binder at high pavement temperatures typically occurring during summer months, thereby helping the pavement resist rutting [1]. In Stone Matrix Asphalt the asphalt binder forms a rich mortar that coats the aggregates, providing durability while stone‑to‑stone contacts supply strength, creating a tough, stable, rut‑resistant wearing course [1]. For an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded mixture the binder is a field or terminal blended blend of asphalt cement, selected additives, and reclaimed ground tire rubber containing at least 15 % rubber by weight; this binder has a higher viscosity than conventional neat binders, which allows a higher binder content and thicker film thickness that gives good elastic recovery, increased durability, improved long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking, better resistance to aging and oxidation, and reduced roadway noise [1]. Asphalt rubber binders should be considered for projects that would normally use gap‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, the mixture is required to be produced using the wet process in which the crumb‑rubber modifier is blended with the asphalt binder before mixing with aggregates, and for new construction the AR‑GG must be used as a wearing course [1]. Crumb Rubber Modified Asphalt Binder (CRMAB) is produced by blending ground tire rubber with hot asphalt cement at a refinery or terminal; its smaller particle size and lower viscosity permit incorporation into limited‑void dense‑graded aggregate where it coats the particles to supply cohesion and durability comparable to polymer‑modified binders, improving resistance to reflective, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, reducing rutting compared with conventional mixes, and lowering roadway noise; within the pavement system CRMAB constitutes the wearing and binder layers—generally placed in the upper portion of a new construction section—where its modified properties provide superior performance [1]. The Department of Transportation specifications define asphalt binder as a performance‑graded liquid or cementitious material used in a variety of pavement applications, and in Section 466 it is applied as a tack coat on a clean, dry surface to create a uniform, tacky coating that saturates and bonds interlayer components such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid to the existing pavement, thereby ensuring adhesion before an overlay is placed; the material must be kept at a temperature not less than 290 °F and must not exceed 320 °F during application, and it is applied at rates sufficient to fully saturate the specific interlayer product [2]. In Section 659 the binder, called mixed binder, serves as the adhesive that is spread over pavement surfaces and into pre‑treated cracks that are at least ¼ inch wide before high‑friction aggregate is placed; it is uniformly mixed and spread to a thickness of 50–65 mils, after which aggregate is laid at a rate of 12–15 lb per square yard so that the wet film bonds the stones to the underlying asphalt or concrete, forming a continuous film that seals the cracks and provides the retention and bonding needed for the aggregate layer, creating a durable high‑friction surface [2]. In Section 450 the binder is applied as an asphalt binder course when repairing deeper potholes; it is placed in lifts that do not exceed three inches uncompacted, each lift being leveled and compacted before a wearing‑course layer is placed, thereby providing the structural binding layer that supports the final surface of the pavement [2]. In Section 341 the binder is a performance‑graded asphalt material such as PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28 that conforms to AASHTO M 332; it is mixed with reclaimed material, aggregates and any emulsified or foamed asphalt in accordance with the approved mix design, and its coating function allows the components to achieve the specified compaction for the cold‑recycled base course [2]. In Section 489 the binder is a performance‑graded bituminous material required by AASHTO M 332 and obtained from an approved producer; it is combined with fine and coarse aggregate and filler to form a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete, where the binder acts as the binding component that holds the aggregates together to create the ultra‑thin bonded wearing surface [2]. In Section 314 the binder is the liquid material called for in the Superpave mixture design; it is mixed with uniformly graded aggregate fractions and optional warm‑mix additives or modifiers to produce a Joint Mix Formula that meets the required volumetric and performance criteria, and by being combined with the aggregates the binder forms part of the asphalt mixture that makes up the pavement structure [2]. In Section 471 the binder, supplied as asphalt cement, is applied to coarse aggregate to create a pre‑coated aggregate for seal‑coat work; it must coat the aggregate satisfactorily at a rate that leaves a thin, brownish, translucent film covering at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface, the resulting pre‑coating containing 0.6 % to 1.2 % residual binder by weight of the mix, and this film binds the particles together and permits the material to be stocked, handled, and uniformly spread [2]. In Section 413 the hot asphalt material designated for an asphalt course is used to coat vertical surfaces that will be in contact with new asphalt mixtures and may be applied as a tack coat before overlaying existing pavement; by applying this uniform coating the binder prepares the surface for placement of the new wearing course, and PG‑Binder or PG 64S‑22 painted as a uniform coating on longitudinal joints acts as a hot sealant that bonds adjacent pavement layers and protects the joint surface, with the sealant heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F [2]. In Section 412 the binder is the liquid component of a 6.3 mm nominal maximum aggregate size thin overlay mix; together with uniformly graded aggregates and any approved warm‑mix technology it is combined in defined proportions to develop a Joint Mix Formula that satisfies material, gradation and volumetric design criteria and meets all Department requirements [2]. In Section 360 the binder, referred to simply as asphalt material, is heated and mixed with dry aggregate so that it coats each particle, forming a continuous film of adequate thickness that provides the binding properties needed to hold the aggregate together and give the permeable base its structural integrity; the mixture must be produced at a temperature below 320 °F to retain suitable viscosity for coating without segregation [2].

For projects where the intended layer is a gap‑graded wearing or binder course that would otherwise employ a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, asphalt rubber binders may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements. They are especially suitable for Interstate and Interstate look‑alike projects that exceed 25,000 ADT, exceed 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and cover an area greater than 50,000 square yards. The mixture should not be selected for projects requiring significant handwork or raking, for shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, or for temporary lanes and crossovers where the premium cost is not warranted; in those situations a conventional Superpave or other non‑premium mix should be used instead. For projects where the intended layer is a dense‑graded wearing or binder course that would otherwise employ a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, crumb rubber modified asphalt binders may be considered for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements. Their use is recommended in the upper layers of the pavement section (wearing and binder courses) where superior performance can be utilized, particularly on Interstate and Interstate look‑alike projects that exceed 25,000 ADT, exceed 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and cover an area greater than 50,000 square yards. They should not be used in lower layers, in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, in temporary lanes or crossovers, or in any project that requires significant handwork or raking because of mixture stiffness; a premium, higher‑cost mixture is not warranted in those cases. Composition and characteristics

In friction‑course (FB) surface courses the binder is an asphalt cement that is typically specified as a PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 grade; it may be placed in cold mixes for FB‑1 and must be mixed hot and placed hot for FB‑2, where the high void content and relative softness compared with PG 64S‑22 give the binder a highly flexible structure suitable for low‑volume roads [1]. For conventional and Superpave pavement courses the specification limits binders to three standard performance‑grade (PG) types—PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, and PG 64E‑22—and when a higher high‑temperature grade is “bumped” (for example from PG 58S‑28 to PG 64S‑28) the resulting binder becomes stiffer at summer temperatures, improving resistance to rutting [1]. The PG 64E‑22 binder is a base asphalt binder that has been polymer‑modified by incorporating styrene block copolymers (SB or SBS formulations), and provisionally approved binders may also use other polymer additives such as natural latex (SBR) or additional modifiers, defining its composition for Superpave courses [1]. In Stone Matrix Asphalt the binder is described as a rich mortar that is fiber‑ or polymer‑modified, forming a high‑asphalt‑content matrix and containing a high amount of mineral filler added in a manner different from usual to enhance durability [1]. Rubberized asphalt binders used in an AR‑GG mixture consist of a field or terminal blended blend of asphalt cement, specified additives, and reclaimed ground‑tire rubber; the crumb‑rubber modifier must constitute at least 15 % of the total blend weight, the rubber particles are retained up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm) size, and the binder exhibits a higher viscosity than conventional neat binders while meeting the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II [1]. The AR‑GG binder is a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 bitumen combined with crumb rubber via the wet process, producing a stiff mixture that should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required and whose wearing layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches [1]. In dense‑graded courses the rubber‑modified binder consists of a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder blended with crumb rubber, imparting good elastic recovery and increased durability, improving resistance to reflective, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, reducing rutting relative to conventional mixes, lowering roadway noise, and effectively raising the performance grade from PG 64S‑22 to PG 64E‑22 [1]. The CRMAB binder is essentially a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder whose properties are altered by crumb rubber added through the wet process; it may also incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingle material as allowed, contains smaller particle size (#30 mesh or smaller) and lower viscosity to permit incorporation into dense‑graded aggregate with limited void space, and must satisfy the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332, with a finished layer thickness not exceeding 2½ inches [1]. In high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) construction the binder is a mixed material supplied by the manufacturer whose constituent components must be uniformly blended to within ± 2 % by volume; the freshly mixed binder is kept wet until placement, spread to a uniform film thickness of 50–65 mils (equivalent to 25–32 sq ft per gallon) before aggregate application, and may not be allowed to expose, separate, cure, dry or harden in any way that would impair its ability to retain and bond the aggregate [2]. Across the various pavement specifications the binder is defined by compliance with the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder as verified by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test of AASHTO M 332; it may be supplied as a virgin mix or blended with reclaimed materials—5 % to 15 % reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or recycled asphalt shingles—in Superpave mixture designs, while still satisfying the performance‑grade requirements established by the MSCR test [2]. For Stone Matrix Asphalt the binder may contain up to 10 % RAP and must meet the same MSCR‑based specifications [2]. Warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) technology additives or modifiers may be blended with the base binder either at the refinery/terminal or later at the production plant, provided that the additive name, dosage rate and blending method are documented and the resulting blend continues to meet the required performance grade; such blending is permitted for the thin‑asphalt overlay binder (PG 64E‑22) as well as for other specified grades [2]. In a thin‑asphalt overlay the binder is a virgin performance‑graded material identified as PG 64E‑22 and must conform to the MSCR test, while cold‑recycled base courses may use any of the listed grades—PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28—supplied in accordance with AASHTO M 332 and sourced from a Bulletin 15‑listed supplier [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems the binder is an asphalt cement of either PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22 for the 9.5 mm wearing course, or PG 64S‑22 for the 19.0 mm binder course; it may be modified with approved polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber to achieve the required grade, and when an anti‑strip additive is used its dosage shall not be less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt [2]. For pre‑coated aggregate seal coats the binder is supplied as an asphalt cement whose residual content must be between 0.6 % and 1.2 % by weight of the mix (or 0.4 % to 1.0 % when other asphalt materials are used); it is applied so that a thin brownish translucent film covers at least 90 % of the aggregate’s visible surface, and mixing temperature must not exceed 350 °F [2]. In an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course the binder functions as a liquid asphalt material that, when heated below 320 °F, coats dry aggregate to form a continuous film of adequate thickness providing the required binding properties without causing segregation during transport [2].

In FB‑1 mixes the use of asphalt materials softer than PG 64S‑22, together with a high void content, yields a highly flexible surface that is appropriate for low‑volume roads. For FB‑2 mixes the selection of stiffer PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 binders requires hot mixing and hot placement, which changes processing conditions and can affect stiffness; because both FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces have high void content a seal coat or surface treatment must be applied to protect the binder. Bumping from a PG 58S‑28 grade to a PG 64S‑28 grade makes the binder stiffer at high pavement temperatures, improving resistance to rutting; only three standard grades (PG 58S‑28, 64S‑22, and 64E‑22) are normally specified, and any use of other grades is considered nonstandard and may be permitted only after prior approval. Incorporating a fiber or polymer‑modified, high‑asphalt content matrix changes the binder composition, giving SMA mixtures greater durability but also raising material cost; the higher asphalt content is required to achieve the desired air‑void level after the aggregate gradation meets VMA requirements and influences moisture susceptibility and draindown sensitivity that must be evaluated. Raising the rubber content of the binder to at least 15 % by weight and using specialized mixing equipment to heat and agitate the blend causes crumb‑rubber particles to swell, producing a rubberized asphalt binder that contains rubber particles up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm) and exhibits higher viscosity than conventional neat binders; this elevated viscosity makes it feasible to place the binder in gap‑graded mixtures with high air voids, allowing a greater overall binder content and a thicker film thickness on the aggregate, and because of these compositional and processing changes the AR‑GG binder delivers performance comparable to polymer‑modified asphalt binders. Adding rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder raises stiffness, which improves rut resistance and overall durability; the resulting high‑viscosity binder also provides good elastic recovery, increased long‑term resistance to both rutting and cracking, greater resistance to aging and oxidation, and can reduce roadway noise, although such asphalt‑rubber binders cannot be tested or graded under AASHTO M 320 or AASHTO M 332. When the composition of an asphalt rubber binder includes crumb‑rubber modifier (CRM) and may also incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement or surface material, the resulting mixture tends to be stiffer, which can limit its use on projects that require extensive handwork or raking; because the CRM must be blended with the base binder before aggregate addition, the wet‑process production method is prescribed to ensure proper integration of the modifier, and if a warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) approach is chosen only a chemical warm‑mix additive may be used, as foaming is prohibited, further influencing the binder’s workability and performance; the design also limits the layer thickness to no more than 2 ½ inches. Using a lower crumb rubber content and a finer gradation (#30 mesh or smaller) produces a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder with smaller particle size and lower viscosity, which enables its incorporation into dense‑graded aggregates that have limited void space; whether the blend is produced at a refinery or terminal rather than on site does not change the essential properties, but the CRMAB must still satisfy the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332, and its performance is comparable to that of polymer‑modified asphalt binders. Because crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) makes the mixture noticeably stiffer, it should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required; due to its higher stiffness and cost CRMAB is recommended only for upper wearing and binder courses on high‑traffic projects—those with more than 25 000 ADT, more than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and more than 50 000 square yards—and the layer thickness should not exceed 2 ½ inches; the mixture is required to be produced using the wet process only, and if warm‑mix asphalt is used a chemical warm‑mix additive may be utilized but foaming is not allowed. For high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) construction the binder components must be mixed uniformly to within ± 2 % of the manufacturer’s volume proportions, and any deviation in proportioning can compromise the binder’s ability to retain and bond aggregate; exposure that allows the mixed binder to separate, cure or dry before aggregate placement may impair retention and bonding, and contaminated binder must be removed and replaced. In Superpave mixture designs that contain 5 % to 15 % reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or 5 % recycled asphalt shingles, the binder still has to satisfy the performance‑graded requirements verified by the MSCR test and be sourced from a supplier listed in Bulletin 15; when the mixture contains more than 15 % RAP—or both 5 % RAS and at least 5 % RAP—the LTS evaluates the reclaimed binder and determines the grade that must be used, ensuring that any compositional change is reflected in a verified performance class. When warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) technology additives or modifiers are provided either blended at the refinery/terminal or at the plant, the binder must conform to the same MSCR‑based specifications and be accompanied by a producer quality‑control plan documenting source, dosage, blending method, and testing; plant modifications are required to achieve a uniform blend of the additive, modifier, or foaming process so that the resulting mixture meets the specified limits. For stone matrix asphalt variations in binder composition such as type and amount of WMA additives, modifiers, or fibers should be introduced through plant modifications that achieve a uniform blend; otherwise the mixture may fail to meet specification limits for asphalt content, gradation, draindown, density, and air‑voids. The source of the binder is limited to a fully automated plant listed in Bulletin 41, and processing steps such as aggregate drying and increased mixing times are required so that both aggregates and the completed mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture. Precise proportioning of stabilizer or fiber is controlled by devices interlocked with the plant and limited to ±10 % of the required weight, which directly influences uniform binder distribution and mixture performance. Hot‑mixture storage must not exceed 2 hours after mixing; material that does not consistently match the quality of freshly discharged mix will be rejected. In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses a change in the grade or source of the performance‑graded binder—such as using PG 64S‑22 instead of PG 58V‑28—requires a new mix design, and when RAP gradations and composition vary sufficiently field adjustments prescribed in the QC plan must be made to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction. For pervious asphalt pavement systems incorporating polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber into the binder allows the mixture to achieve the specified PG grade, so changes in composition directly alter performance characteristics; RAP is limited to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture, anti‑strip additive must be blended at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt and selected for compatibility, and when a stabilizer is required its dosage must fall within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix, influencing moisture susceptibility and stability. In ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the binder must meet the Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder specification (AASHTO M 332) and be supplied by an approved producer listed in Bulletin 15; any change in material source triggers a requirement to submit a revised Job Mix Formula, and the binder’s properties must remain within the JMF tolerances such as asphalt content by mass and percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve, otherwise the material is considered defective. For asphalt seal coats using precoated aggregate selecting between asphalt cement and other asphalt materials changes the required residual binder content—0.6 % to 1.2 % for asphalt cement and 0.4 % to 1.0 % for other materials—and the processing steps of drying the aggregate, pre‑coating in a pug mill, and mixing at a temperature not exceeding 350°F control how uniformly that film forms; covering at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface is required, and if less than 90 % is covered the percent passing a 75 µm sieve must not exceed 0.5 %. For thin asphalt overlay courses when the aggregate fractions, asphalt binder, or a WMA additive/modifier/process are altered the mixture must be re‑designed through a new Job Mix Formula and verified to confirm that performance criteria such as moisture resistance are still met; any change in material source likewise triggers the requirement for a new JMF with updated calibration factors, the WMA technology is incorporated only during production, and a moisture susceptibility analysis must be performed on the resulting mix to ensure the additive does not degrade moisture resistance. For an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course the binder must be heated so that after the aggregate has been dried it can form a continuous film of adequate thickness; producing the mix at a temperature below 320°F provides the viscosity required for proper coating and prevents segregation of asphalt from the aggregate during transport. The specification also requires that the material not be stockpiled and that placement occur within eight hours of production, because delayed handling would degrade the binder’s binding ability; placement is prohibited when air or surface temperatures are below 35°F or when rain is present, since low temperatures prevent the binder from achieving the required coating performance. Key material properties

In FB surface courses (section 5.3) the binder’s flexibility is a key physical property because the mix has a high void content and most of the asphalt materials used are softer than PG 64S‑22; therefore selecting performance‑grade binders such as PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22, which must be mixed hot and placed hot, provides the lower stiffness (softness) and appropriate temperature susceptibility required for the intended flexible behavior of FB‑1 and FB‑2 overlays. [1] For stone matrix asphalt wearing courses (section 5.14) the binder’s resistance to moisture and its tendency to drain from the mix are the primary factors governing durability, so evaluating moisture susceptibility and asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity is essential, and a fiber or polymer‑modified binder with a high asphalt content is recommended because it contributes to durability and rut resistance. [1] In asphalt rubber gap‑graded wearing courses (section 5.16) performance is tied to the binder’s viscosity and to the proportion of reclaimed tire rubber; the blend must contain at least 15 % rubber by weight of the total blend, which swells during heating to give a higher viscosity than conventional neat binders, permitting greater binder content and thicker film thickness, and the binder must meet the physical requirements specified in ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II. In the FB surface courses specified by the Pavement Policy Manual, asphalt binders are classified by Superpave performance grades so that FB‑1 mixes require binders softer than PG 64S‑22 and FB‑2 mixes may use either a PG 58S‑28 binder or the more common PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement. Behavior and mechanisms

In the FB surface courses described in section 5.3, the binder used in FB‑1 (and similarly in FB‑2) behaves as a highly flexible material because the mix contains many voids and the asphalt cement is softer than PG 64S‑22, which gives it a low structural coefficient of 0.20 when designed as an overlay; the high void content also makes the surface prone to moisture ingress, so a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although this can be delayed up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound, and FB Modified surfaces may go without such treatment for at least four years [1]. When PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28 binder is used (as in FB‑2), the mix must be hot‑mixed and hot‑placed, reflecting sensitivity to temperature during construction [1]. In the Superpave paving courses of section 5.9, the binder is selected so that the mixture can resist permanent deformation (rutting) under traffic loading and thermal cracking at low temperatures; when projected traffic exceeds greater than 10 million ESALs a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is specified for wearing courses to avoid rutting [1]. Moisture susceptibility is addressed by using an Asphalt Rich Base Course whose mix designs are < 0.3 million ESAL with design air voids of 2.5%, providing high tensile strength and moisture‑damage resistance for the bottom pavement layer during its service life, and the design life assumed for these selections is a 20‑year period which establishes the expected loading rate used in binder specification [1]. Section 5.10 notes that increasing the high‑temperature grade of a PG‑binder – often called “bumping” – makes the binder stiffer when pavement temperatures are high, which helps it resist deformation from traffic loads and reduces rutting; the specification limits the use to three standard grades unless a nonstandard grade is approved [1]. In the rubber‑gap‑graded wearing course of section 5.16, the high‑viscosity binder shows good elastic recovery and increased durability, which translates into improved long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking under traffic loading and also exhibits better resistance to aging and oxidation, indicating enhanced performance against chemical degradation; adding rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder can raise stiffness further improving rut resistance, although such asphalt‑rubber binders cannot be tested or graded according to the performance‑graded requirements of AASHTO M 320 or AASHTO M 332 [1]. Finally, in the dense‑graded courses of section 5.17, rubber‑modified asphalt binder provides good elastic recovery and increased durability, helping the pavement retain its shape under traffic loads, improves resistance to cracking—including reflective, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking—and reduces rutting compared with conventional mixes, indicating a stronger response to repeated loading [1]. During construction the asphalt material that includes the binder must be kept between a minimum of 290 °F and not exceed 320 °F to avoid damage; it also must remain dry and be wrapped or covered during shipping and storage to protect it from precipitation, chemicals, ultraviolet light, and sparks, and it must be stored elevated off the ground (section 466) [2]. Prior to placement the pavement surface must be clean, dry, and free of moisture, and the interlayer system may not be installed on wet pavement; if rain falls after placement, the surface must be allowed to dry before the overlay is placed (section 466) [2]. In stone matrix asphalt mixtures the binder must stay locked in the stone matrix, and vibratory rolling is permitted only after a demonstration shows that no flushing of the binder occurs; placement is prohibited on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower because moisture and low temperature jeopardize binder adhesion, and both aggregates and the finished mixture must be free of excess moisture and unburned fuel oil (section 419) [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement compaction must be limited to no more than four passes, since over‑compacting may crush aggregate and reduce drainage; placement is not allowed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F, and compaction is permitted only when the surface is cool enough to resist a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller or a vibratory roller operated in static mode (section 420) [2]. In an asphalt treated permeable base course the binder must be mixed at a temperature below 320 °F so that it coats aggregate with an adequate film; placement may occur only when ambient conditions are above 35 °F and free of rain, and placement on frozen or wet surfaces is prohibited. After spreading, the mix should be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent pavement layer is laid, and compaction is limited to four roller passes while the material remains warm enough to avoid shoving but not so hard that aggregate crushing eliminates its free‑draining character (section 360) [2].

Durability and deterioration

In FB surface courses the high void content of FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces creates a durability concern that requires a seal coat or surface treatment, and a minimum of 3 months of warm‑weather traffic densification is recommended before applying either a seal coat or surface treatment; when the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface remains satisfactory these applications can be postponed for up to 3 years, while an FB Modified surface may not require a seal coat or surface treatment for at least 4 years. Superpave paving courses aim to protect the asphalt binder from permanent deformation (rutting) and thermal cracking; when projected traffic exceeds 10 million ESALs a polymer‑modified (PG 64E‑22) binder should be specified for wearing courses to avoid pavement rutting, and measures such as aramid fiber modification or an Asphalt Rich Base Course can delay crack initiation, limit bottom‑up cracking, and enhance moisture‑damage resistance during the service life. In Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing courses the asphalt binder’s durability is chiefly challenged by moisture susceptibility and the potential for asphalt‑cement draindown, which must be evaluated during mix design; consequently a modified binder and stabilizer are typically specified to enhance resistance to such damage. The asphalt binder is also susceptible to aging and oxidation, primary deterioration mechanisms that can diminish its elasticity and overall durability; high‑viscosity binders provide good elastic recovery and increased durability, improving long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking, and the addition of rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder can increase binder stiffness, further enhancing rut resistance. Performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders must be maintained at a temperature of at least 290 °F and must not exceed 320 °F because temperatures above this limit can damage the asphalt paving interlayer system. During shipping and storage the binder shall be kept dry and wrapped, and any damaged outer wrap shall be discarded before installation to protect it from outdoor weather elements, ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals, and sparks. Surface preparation requires a clean, dry pavement free of oil, dirt, gravel or vegetation so that the binder can achieve proper adhesion; contaminants or moisture may compromise the bond and lead to premature deterioration. In high‑friction surface treatment the binder must not be allowed to become exposed, separate, cure, dry, or otherwise harden in a manner that impairs retention and bonding of aggregate, and any contaminated binder shall be removed and replaced at no additional expense to the Department. Placement of HFST shall not be performed when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or when wind gusts of 20 mph or higher are anticipated because such conditions can compromise the binder film. Stone matrix asphalt mixtures shall not be placed on wet surfaces or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower, and SMA placement is further restricted to the period from October 1 to March 31 in many districts because low temperatures and moisture can impair binder performance. Both aggregates and the finished mix must be free of excess moisture and unburned fuel oil as specified; moisture intrusion could damage the binder‑aggregate bond. If vibratory rolling is proposed, a demonstration to the satisfaction of the Representative shall show that no breaking of aggregate or flushing of asphalt binder results from the vibration, highlighting flushing as a potential damage process. For pervious asphalt mixes moisture‑induced stripping is identified as the primary durability concern; when no performance history exists the finished mix must be tested in accordance with ASTM D3625 and achieve at least 90 % coating, and if the test indicates moisture susceptibility an anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime shall be incorporated using the manufacturer’s recommended dosage but not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt. The binder course in pervious pavement must not become contaminated by traffic; any binder surface that cannot be cleaned shall be removed and replaced to the Representative’s satisfaction. Compaction of pervious mixes shall be limited to no more than four passes so as to preclude a reduction in surface porosity, placement shall be avoided when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F, and no tack coat shall be applied between lifts of any pervious asphalt. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, moisture susceptibility of the binder is explicitly noted as a durability concern; the contractor must provide moisture‑sensitivity data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg wheel‑tracking results to demonstrate resistance to water‑induced damage. In asphalt rich base course mixtures recycled asphalt pavement content shall not exceed twenty percent by weight and manufacturer waste recycled asphalt shingles shall not be used; when a warm‑mix additive, modifier, or foaming process is incorporated the mix design shall include moisture‑susceptibility testing to prevent binder performance degradation. For thin asphalt overlays a moisture susceptibility analysis shall be performed according to Bulletin 27, and any warm‑mix additive, modifier, or process must not be detrimental to the mixture’s moisture resistance. When the total asphalt content of a mixture exceeds seven percent, binder draindown becomes a durability concern; the mix shall be tested for draindown sensitivity with AASHTO T 305 at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F, and if draindown exceeds the limits of Table A fibers or the stabilizers specified in Section 419.2(d) shall be used to reduce binder movement. Placement of an asphalt‑treated permeable base course on a frozen or otherwise unstable surface, or when ambient air temperature is below 35 °F, compromises binder durability because proper binding cannot be achieved; consequently the mixture must be produced at a temperature below 320 °F to avoid segregation and must be placed within eight hours of production to retain its coating film. After placement the mix should be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layers are added, and compaction shall be limited to four roller passes with an 8‑ton to 10‑ton steel‑wheel roller so that the material is not over‑compacted to the point where it ceases to be free draining or the aggregate becomes crushed. Selecting a higher high‑temperature performance‑grade (“bumping”) makes the binder stiffer at summer pavement temperatures and therefore reduces its susceptibility to rutting, whereas using an un‑bumped lower‑grade binder would leave it less stiff and more vulnerable under those same high‑temperature conditions [1]. A high‑viscosity binder provides good elastic recovery and increased durability, and the addition of rubber raises binder stiffness, improving resistance to rutting while also giving improved long‑term resistance to cracking, aging and oxidation, thereby lowering the likelihood of binder degradation [1]. Keeping the binder within the temperature window specified for performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders—at least 290 °F and not exceeding 320 °F—helps prevent heat‑induced damage, while temperatures above 320 °F are noted to cause damage to the interlayer system [2]. Storing the material elevated off the ground and covered protects it from ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals and sparks, thereby reducing exposure that could accelerate deterioration [2]. Installation must be avoided when weather conditions are unsuitable or when the pavement surface is wet, because improper weather or a wet substrate can increase susceptibility to failure; a clean, dry, debris‑free surface also supports proper bonding and limits degradation [2]. The binder’s susceptibility rises when the mixed binder is allowed to be exposed, separate, cure, dry, or otherwise harden in a way that impairs retention and bonding of aggregate, and contamination likewise increases risk; therefore the binder should be kept protected until it is applied [2]. High‑friction surface treatment (HFST) applications must not be placed on new asphalt pavements until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, and placement must not occur if wind speed is above 15 mph or if wind gusts of 20 mph or higher are anticipated during the treatment [2]. Any binder that becomes contaminated shall be removed and replaced at no additional expense to the Department [2]. Using asphalt mixtures at temperatures below the minimum specified or allowing cold lumps to remain in the mat increases the binder’s vulnerability to edge breakdown, pattern segregation and flushing; a mixture that is deficient or has an excess of asphalt content is also deemed unsuitable and can promote deterioration [2]. The exposed outer edges should be worked immediately after placement to eliminate sharp, ragged, and open edges, and maintaining a macro‑texture depth greater than 0.006 in prevents flushing [2]. The susceptibility of the binder in an SMA mixture rises when moisture or low‑temperature exposure occurs; placement on wet surfaces is forbidden, the air or surface temperature must be above 50 °F, and SMA paving is limited to periods outside October 1–March 31 in many districts [2]. Both the aggregates and the completed mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified, and binder exposure to moisture should be kept low by limiting apparent moisture content to no more than 0.5 % and by sampling whenever coarse‑aggregate water absorption exceeds 2.0 % [2]. Only one joint mix facility (JMF) may be stored in a surge or storage silo at any given time [2]. A compatible, heat‑stable, amine‑based liquid anti‑strip additive—or an alternate compatible anti‑strip agent—should be used, and moisture‑susceptibility analysis should be performed when such additives are employed; when moisture susceptibility has been demonstrated, the anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime shall be incorporated, with the additive blended into the cement at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt [2]. If testing indicates a stabilizer is required, cellulose fibers shall be added at a dosage rate between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix [2]. Recycled asphalt pavement shall be limited to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture [2]. Placement of asphalt‑paved permeable base course (ATPBC) must not occur on surfaces that are unstable, frozen, or when air temperature is below 35 °F, and it must not be placed during rain; the mixture should be produced at a temperature below 320 °F to provide adequate film thickness and to prevent segregation of asphalt from aggregate, and the mat should be allowed to cool to 100 °F before placing subsequent layers, with rolling promptly after sufficient cooling to limit binder softening and shoving [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

In Superpave mixture design the interaction between binder and additives is governed by the specified performance‑grade binder and the anticipated traffic level; when the estimated ESAL range exceeds ten million ESALs, the District should specify a polymer‑modified (PG 64E-22) binder for wearing or binder courses to avoid pavement rutting, indicating that the polymer must be compatible with high‑load mixes. Fiber‑modified courses are permitted only with PG 58S-28, PG 64S-22, or PG 64E-22 binders, and the aramid fibers can help prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation but only if they are uniformly distributed throughout the mix. Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) is a tough, stable, rut‑resistant mixture that relies on stone‑to‑stone contact to provide strength and a rich mortar binder to provide durability; consequently these objectives are usually achieved with a gap‑graded aggregate coupled with a fiber or polymer‑modified, high asphalt‑content matrix, and the mix design evaluates moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity while also ensuring that the interaction between binder and aggregate meets the required air‑void level and VMA criteria. In SMA binder may be virgin or blended with up to 10 % RAP provided the resulting material still conforms to the performance‑graded specifications defined by the MSCR test, and when a WMA technology additive or modifier is incorporated it can be introduced either at the refinery/terminal or at the plant and must be blended with a base binder of the specified performance grade so that the final blend meets the required performance‑grade criteria. In an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded (AR‑GG) mixture the rubberized binder is produced by blending asphalt cement, additives and reclaimed ground tire rubber until the rubber particles swell, creating a higher‑viscosity binder that can be placed in the high‑void gap‑graded aggregate skeleton; the elevated viscosity permits a greater binder demand and thicker film thickness on the coarse stones, and the rubber‑modified binder delivers performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders. To qualify, the AR‑GG binder must meet the physical requirements of ASTM D6114, Table 1, Type II. In AR‑GG mixtures the rubber modifier (CRM) must first be blended with the asphalt binder using a wet process, after which the combined binder is mixed with the aggregates; this sequencing ensures that the rubber is fully incorporated before aggregate contact, and the mix may contain RAP or RAS material as defined in the project specifications, allowing the recycled binder to interact with the virgin binder within the same blend. When rubber is blended into a conventional PG 64S-22 asphalt binder, the resulting mixture exhibits higher stiffness, which translates into better rut resistance; this interaction also contributes to the high‑viscosity binder’s good elastic recovery and increased durability, as well as improved long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking, however the modified asphalt rubber binders are not eligible for testing or grading under the performance‑graded asphalt binder requirements of AASHTO M 320 or AASHTO M 332. Crumb Rubber Modified Asphalt Binder (CRMAB) is blended with hot asphalt binder to create a material whose smaller particle size and lower viscosity enable it to be incorporated into dense‑graded aggregate that has limited void space, thereby achieving the necessary binder‑aggregate interaction for such mixtures; the resulting CRMAB provides similar performance to polymer‑modified binders while still having to satisfy the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332. CRMAB binders interact with other mix constituents through the wet‑process, where the rubber chemically modifies the binder properties, and they may incorporate RAP or RAS material as specified in the SSP; they are intended for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, showing compatibility with that polymer system. If a warm‑mix asphalt is used, a chemical warm‑mix additive should be utilized because foaming is not allowed. In HIGH FRICTION SURFACE TREATMENT the binder must be uniformly mixed to within ± 2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications and kept wet until the aggregate is placed; any hardening, separation or contamination that could impair retention and bonding requires removal and replacement at no additional expense to the Department, a serrated squeegee spreads a uniform film 50‑65 mil thick (25‑32 sq ft per gallon) and the aggregate is mechanically placed on the wet, uncured binder at a rate of 12‑15 lb per square yard so that full coverage is achieved without displacing the film. In SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN only materials explicitly specified may be combined with the asphalt binder; unspecified additives, rejuvenators or other substances must not be incorporated. When reclaimed asphalt pavement ranging from 5 % to 15 % or recycled asphalt shingle is present, Laboratory Testing Services evaluates the reclaimed binder and determines the performance‑grade class that the contractor must use, thereby ensuring compatibility with the virgin binder grade. Warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) technology additives or modifiers may be blended with the base binder only when supplied by an approved manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and documented in a Producer QC Plan that records the additive name, dosage rates, blending method and required testing; plant modifications are made per technical representative directions to achieve a uniform blend of the WMA technology additive, modifier or foaming process and produce an asphalt mixture meeting these specifications. In STONE MATRIX ASPHALT any WMA technology additive, polymer modifier or foaming process must be introduced at the plant and blended uniformly with the asphalt binder to meet SMA requirements; when a stabilizer fiber is used it is added to the aggregate in the weigh hopper, dry and wet mixing times are increased, and the stabilizer must be uniformly distributed before the binder is injected, ensuring proper binder‑fiber interaction. Both aggregates and the finished mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified, and fibers or pelletized additives may not be fed through cold feed bins or RAP bins. If the water absorption of a coarse aggregate exceeds 2.0 % (AASHTO T 85), the mixture is sampled according to PTM No. 1 at the frequency specified in the producer QC Plan and the apparent moisture content must not exceed 0.5 %. Compatibility of anti‑strip additives is achieved by using either a compatible, heat‑stable, amine‑based liquid anti‑strip or an alternate additive; when the WMA technology includes an anti‑strip additive, moisture susceptibility analysis is performed as specified and additional anti‑strip may be added or other adjustments made to meet the required moisture susceptibility criteria. In COLD‑RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSE emulsified or foamed asphalt binder is combined with reclaimed material and aggregates strictly according to the approved mix design, using the mix‑design‑specified optimum moisture level and prescribed emulsion or foam content; field adjustments detailed in the QC plan are made as needed to obtain satisfactory coating of the aggregate particles and achieve the specified compaction. In the PERMEABLE ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM modification of the binder with approved polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber may be used to achieve the required PG grade, RAP is limited to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture, and anti‑strip additives or hydrated lime must be blended with the asphalt cement before it enters the mix using the manufacturer’s recommended dosage but not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt; these additives must be compatible with both aggregate and binder. In ASPHALT RICH BASE COURSE RAP may not exceed 20 % by weight and RAS material is prohibited; for virgin‑material mixes the aggregate fractions, binder and any WMA additive or modifier are combined to meet JMF requirements, while special additives or modifiers need not be used if mixture temperature, workability and compaction can be achieved solely through plant mechanical modification such as foamed asphalt. The WMA technology additive, modifier or process is employed only when evaluating results from moisture susceptibility testing during the mix‑design process. In ASPHALT SEAL COAT USING PRECOATED AGGREGATE the binder is applied as a thin pre‑coating on coarse aggregate at a mixing temperature not exceeding 350 °F (or not exceed 150 °F when cut‑back asphalts are used); the residual binder content must be 0.6 %–1.2 % by weight of the mix when asphalt cement is used or 0.4 %–1.0 % when other asphalt materials are used, producing a brownish translucent film that must cover at least 90 % of the aggregate’s visible surface area, and any fraction passing a No. 200 sieve may not exceed 0.5 %. For mixes covered by the 60‑month warranty moisture sensitivity testing—either tensile strength and tensile strength ratio data or Hamburg wheel tracking results—must be submitted to verify binder‑aggregate adhesion and resistance to stripping, and the aggregate portion of the design must include its percent absorption because absorption directly influences the amount of binder required to achieve the target voids in the total mixture. In THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY the asphalt binder is blended with selected aggregate fractions and any approved WMA technology additive or modifier; a moisture susceptibility analysis performed according to Bulletin 27 on the blend that includes the WMA technology must confirm that the additive, modifier or process is not detrimental to the mixture’s moisture resistance, thereby protecting binder‑aggregate adhesion. Where the total asphalt content exceeds 7 %, a draindown sensitivity test using AASHTO T 305 at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F must be performed, and if draindown exceeds the limits of Table A, fibers may be added to reduce binder drainage and stabilizers identified in Section 419.2(d) can also be employed for this purpose. In ASPHALT‑TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE the binder is heated and mixed with dried aggregate so that a complete mixture forms; the heat enables the asphalt material to coat the aggregate and create a continuous film of adequate thickness, and production must occur at a temperature below 320 °F to ensure the binder viscosity is suitable for coating without causing segregation during transport, which is essential for effective adhesion between binder and aggregate. Influence of production and placement

FB‑1 wearing and binder materials are classified as “cold” mixes that are typically mixed and placed by a mobile plant, resulting in a high void content and the use of softer binders than PG 64S‑22, which makes the surface highly flexible and appropriate for low‑volume roads. Conversely, FB‑2 requires mixing at a stationary plant and, when a PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 binder is specified, the material must be mixed hot and placed hot, producing a stiffer mix. For performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders, plant mixing temperatures must be maintained at no less than 290 °F and shall not exceed 320 °F to allow a uniform spray pattern; these limits govern the viscosity of the binder during production. In stone matrix asphalt production, both aggregates and the finished mix must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture, and any warm‑mix additive or modifier must be uniformly blended so that the intended binder viscosity and adhesion are preserved. When pre‑coated aggregate is produced, the mixing temperature may not exceed 350 °F, and if cut‑back asphalts are used the aggregate temperature may not exceed 150 °F; these limits protect the thin binder film that must cover at least 90 % of the aggregate surface. For asphalt treated permeable base courses, the binder is mixed at a temperature below 320 °F to provide suitable viscosity for coating without causing segregation during transport. Mixtures must be delivered and placed within the laying‑temperature limits specified in Table A for the material type and class; placement outside those limits is prohibited because cold lumps or under‑temperature material can cause segregation and an imbalance of asphalt content that degrades binder performance. The placement of interlayer reinforcement systems requires a dry pavement surface whose temperature lies between 40 °F and 140 °F, and the binder must remain tacky at the time of placement. Stone matrix asphalt may only be placed when the air or surface temperature exceeds 50 °F and the surface is dry; pervious asphalt mixtures share the same lower limit of 50 °F, while ATPBC placement is prohibited on surfaces below 35 °F. Because both FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces have high void content, a seal coat or surface treatment is recommended only after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, and a seal coat may not be required on an FB Modified surface for at least four years; these post‑placement densification periods influence the final durability and timing of protective treatments. Full cure of interlayer systems must occur prior to construction traffic over fiberglass mesh grids, ensuring that the binder has achieved its designed strength before loading. Pre‑coated aggregate containing damp cut‑back asphalt must be sufficiently cured before use to prevent binder pickup, and mixed material should be stocked on a base no higher than four feet to control cooling before spreading. After spreading, pervious mixtures and ATPBC must be allowed to cool to approximately 100 °F before any subsequent layer is placed, providing adequate setting of the binder without premature reheating. The first lift of an asphalt overlay that includes an interlayer system must be compacted to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches, ensuring adequate structural support. Compaction must meet density acceptance requirements while roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size are adjusted to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage, and all roller marks must be removed. For stone matrix asphalt, steel‑wheeled rollers equipped with watering or soapy systems may be used provided that vibratory action is avoided unless it can be demonstrated that no aggregate breakage or binder flushing occurs. For pervious pavements, compaction is limited to no more than four passes and over‑compacting must be avoided so that the surface remains free‑draining and the aggregate is not crushed; similar limits of four roller passes apply to ATPBC, which must be compacted within eight hours of production. When Superpave material is used, an asphalt tack coat or prime coat that conforms to Publication 408 and Specification Sections 460 and 461 should be applied, with the project engineer adjusting the application rate based on surface porosity—using a lower rate for very smooth non‑porous surfaces and a higher rate for more porous surfaces. For each FB surface course the need for a tack coat must be evaluated; FB‑1 typically does not require one while FB‑2 mixes that employ PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement may benefit from a tack coat. Performance of FB surface courses can be enhanced by using higher‑grade binders such as PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28, provided the material is mixed hot and placed hot, and, where allowed, an FB Modified binder may be used either as a binder or wearing course in accordance with an appropriate Standard Special Provision. Because of the high void content in the FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification (or postponed up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound), while FB Modified surfaces may forego such treatment for at least four years. In Superpave projects the specification encourages selecting a PG‑binder matched to projected traffic; when the 20‑year design life ESALs exceed 10 million, the District should specify a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 to avoid pavement rutting, while for moderate traffic (0.3 to < 3.0 million ESALs) designers may specify lower‑ESAL binders and consider this change when asphalt cracking is a problem in the region, the average truck speed is above 30 mph, or rutting is not a concern. The “bumping” practice of moving from a lower high‑temperature grade (for example PG 58S‑28) to a higher one (PG 64S‑28) provides greater stiffness and better rut resistance when higher summer temperatures are expected. Nonstandard PG grades may be used only after prior approval from the BOCM Materials Division Laboratory Testing Section and completion of a Superpave Special Study Project, which also requires extensive quality controls including pre‑construction pavement distress surveys, extra asphalt binder sampling, loose box sampling, core sampling, and post‑construction manual pavement distress surveys and core sampling for three to five years under LTS and NECEPT guidance. Polymer modification is the primary means to improve PG 64E‑22 binder performance; approved binders are those modified with styrene block copolymers (SB or SBS), while provisionally approved binders may incorporate other polymers such as natural latex (SBR) or additional modifiers, subject to BOCM MD approval. The addition of aramid fibers in an Asphalt Fiber Modified Course can prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation, provided the fibers are uniformly distributed; such courses may be used with PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 binders on eligible wearing, binder, and base courses. Using a high‑viscosity binder or incorporating rubber into a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder can boost performance by providing good elastic recovery, increased durability, improved long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking, improved resistance to aging and oxidation, and reduced roadway noise. In gap‑graded wearing courses the specification requires that an asphalt rubber binder be blended with the binder through a wet process before aggregate mixing; when warm‑mix asphalt is employed, a chemical warm‑mix additive shall be used and foaming is prohibited, and the mixture may also contain reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingle material as permitted by the SSP, providing recycled content without compromising the blend. These premium AR‑GG mixtures replace polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binders and are limited to layer thicknesses not exceeding 2 ½ inches. In dense‑graded courses, incorporating reclaimed ground‑tire rubber as a crumb rubber modifier creates a Crumb Rubber Modified Asphalt Binder (CRMAB) that is produced with a lower crumb rubber content and a finer gradation of #30 mesh or smaller, resulting in smaller particle size and lower viscosity; this allows the binder to fill the limited void space of dense‑graded aggregate while delivering performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders. CRMAB must satisfy the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332, and rubber‑modified asphalt binder provides good elastic recovery and increased durability, improved resistance to reflective cracking, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, reduction in rutting relative to conventional mixes, and the addition of rubber to a PG 64S‑22 binder can raise the performance grade to PG 64E‑22. CRMAB mixtures should also not exceed a layer thickness of 2 ½ inches and may be applied on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes that exceed 25 000 ADT, exceed 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and exceed 50 000 square yards. The asphalt binder used in an interlayer system must be kept dry, wrapped, elevated off the ground and covered from ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals and sparks, must be labeled according to ASTM D4873, and during placement a calibrated distributor‑truck spray bar is required with the binder temperature no lower than 290 °F and not exceeding 320 °F to preserve a uniform spray pattern; surface preparation calls for a clean, dry pavement free of debris, oil or vegetation and any cracks must be sealed and cured before the binder is laid, with a manufacturer’s technical representative overseeing installation and verifying temperature and application requirements. A certified asphalt field technician must supervise all Superpave work so that mixtures are delivered at the temperatures specified in Table A and free of cold lumps; acceptance samples taken from each sublot are tested for asphalt content, gradation and theoretical maximum specific gravity with corrective action required if tolerances are exceeded, pattern segregation is monitored by macrotexture and a pavement is deemed defective when the average depth difference exceeds 0.024 inch, triggering placement of a test section not to exceed 200 tons after adjustments, flushing is identified when macrotexture is ≤ 0.006 inch and also requires suspension of work and replacement of the affected wearing course. The specification permits the use of warm‑mix asphalt technology additives or modifiers that are blended either at the refinery/terminal or at the plant, provided they come from an approved manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and are documented with dosage rates, blending method, quality‑control testing, corrective‑action points, disposition of failed material, storage, handling, shipping and bill of lading; the binder must satisfy the performance‑grade requirements of the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test (AASHTO M 332) and be supplied by a listed source with QC testing and certification as required in Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1. Moisture susceptibility is mitigated through incorporation of a heat‑stable anti‑stripping additive blended with the cement at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, or by adding hydrated lime where appropriate, while a pervious asphalt stabilizer in the form of cellulose fibers may be required after AASHTO T 305 testing and must be dosed within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix. To improve Stone Matrix Asphalt binder performance plants must be fully automated and equipped to blend WMA additives, modifiers or foaming processes as directed by the technical representative, and stabilizer fibers are introduced with specialized metering equipment calibrated to ±10 % of the required weight and monitored by low‑level and no‑flow indicators, feed‑rate printouts and a transparent pipe section; a paving‑operation QC plan submitted on Form CS‑413 and a preplacement meeting must detail construction methods, mixture acceptance criteria and handling of binder samples, while a trial demonstration of at least 100 tons verifies compliance with asphalt content, gradation, draindown, density, air‑voids and specific‑gravity limits. Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of asphalt binder combines laboratory testing, field observations, and long‑term performance assessments, with the specific evidence varying according to the pavement type and specification. For Superpave pavement courses, the evaluation of an asphalt binder relies on a combination of laboratory sampling and field performance monitoring; prior to construction the district must conduct detailed pre‑construction manual pavement distress surveys together with additional asphalt‑binder, loose‑box and core sampling that are analyzed by LTS and the Northeast Center of Excellence for Pavement Technology (NECEPT) [1]. After the pavement is placed, post‑construction manual distress surveys and core sampling are performed for a period of 3 to 5 years, while short‑term (construction) performance and long‑term performance over 5 to 10 years have been well documented as good under Pennsylvania conditions [1]. For asphalt rubber binders used in gap‑graded wearing courses, laboratory evaluation is defined by the requirement that the binder must satisfy the physical requirements set out in ASTM D6114, Table 1, Type II; no field or long‑term performance evidence is mentioned for this binder [1]. Under the Superpave pavement‑course specifications, the binder must be verified in the laboratory with the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test defined in AASHTO M 332, as revised in Bulletin 25, and the results are documented through the quality‑control testing and certification required by Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1 [2]. Acceptance of loose‑plant samples is performed at the Laboratory Testing Services with PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 to determine asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve, while Gmm verification samples are tested for theoretical maximum specific gravity [2]. For Stone Matrix Asphalt mixes a designated HOLA laboratory that has demonstrated proficiency through an AASHTO re:source On‑Site Laboratory Assessment performed within the last two years conducts acceptance testing of loose plant samples for asphalt content, gradation and draindown, and pavement cores are tested for density, air voids and maximum specific gravity [2]. In High Friction Surface Treatment applications a calibrated skid test is performed at least 31 days after the treatment is placed using the procedures of ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T 242) with a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus, and this measurement serves as the primary evidence of binder effectiveness [2]. For Superpave projects the pavement surface macrotexture is measured in the field according to PTM No. 751, and pattern segregation is considered unacceptable when the difference in average texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, while flushing is deemed defective when the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch [2]. Annual Joint Mix Formula verification and daily production quality‑control sampling check asphalt content, gradation, theoretical maximum specific gravity and volumetric properties of compacted specimens, with compliance determined after a minimum of three test results or by using the running average of the last five consecutive results [2]. For Stone Matrix Asphalt a pre‑construction demonstration strip of at least 100 tons is placed according to the approved paving operation QC Plan, and core densities from that strip together with visual monitoring for aggregate breakage or binder flushing provide on‑site verification, while stabilizer metering equipment must be calibrated to ±10 % of the required fiber weight [2]. The cold‑recycled asphalt base course specification requires a contractor‑submitted QC plan that governs field sampling of reclaimed materials and any mix‑design adjustments needed to achieve the specified coating and compaction, and these on‑site quality checks constitute the field evidence for the binder [2]. When mixtures contain more than 15 % reclaimed asphalt pavement or a combination of recycled asphalt shingles and RAP, the Long‑Term Performance evaluation examines the binder present in the RAP (and any RAS) to assign the appropriate performance grade for the project, and this LTS assessment together with the MSCR laboratory results and routine QC certification provides the evidence used to evaluate the binder’s suitability over time [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the evaluation of the binder includes laboratory moisture‑susceptibility testing in accordance with ASTM D3625 on the finished mix with a minimum of 90 % coating, bulk specific gravity determination according to AASHTO T209 (with gyratory compaction per AASHTO T312) to confirm air‑void targets, and AASHTO T305 to decide whether a stabilizer is required, while the plant production QC plan calls for testing of asphalt content, gradation and air voids as outlined in Table C and material certifications from the binder producer documenting compliance serve as additional evidence [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the binder must meet the Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder specification AASHTO M332 and be supported by documented quality‑control testing and certification as required by Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1, a QC Plan and Job Mix Formula are prepared and submitted for review at the start of the project and at least annually, any change in material source triggers a revised JMF, and acceptance is confirmed when laboratory mixture tests—specifically asphalt content by mass and percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200), 2.36 mm (No. 8) and 6.3 mm (¼‑inch) sieves—fall within the tolerances set out in Table E, with certification provided to the inspector within one working day of testing [2]. In Superpave asphalt rich base course designs moisture‑susceptibility testing is performed in the laboratory and the resulting tensile strength ratio data are incorporated into the Job Mix Formula; the WMA additive or modifier is used only to evaluate those test results, and no field or long‑term performance evidence is described for this specification [2]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, binder evaluation is incorporated into the mix design through volumetric procedures using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and ignition‑furnace correction factors, laboratory evidence includes moisture‑sensitivity data such as tensile strengths and tensile strength ratio, long‑term performance may be demonstrated by Hamburg Wheel Tracking results, and certified QC test results for the binder and other materials must be submitted to the Representative within 48 hours of material sampling [2]. For thin asphalt overlay courses moisture‑susceptibility analysis is performed according to Bulletin 27, field verification is provided by an annual Joint Mix Formula verification together with a producer QC plan that includes a signed Form TR‑448A submitted before production, laboratory tests determine theoretical maximum specific gravity, bulk specific gravity, air voids and VMA from compacted specimens prepared in accordance with AASHTO T312, for mixes containing more than 7 % total asphalt content draindown sensitivity is measured by AASHTO T305 at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F, field evidence is obtained by sampling the completed mixture according to PTM No. 1 at the frequency defined in the producer QC plan and testing each sample for sieve‑passing percentages and asphalt content against the tolerances in Table B, confirming that at least 90 % of daily printed‑ticket asphalt‑content results fall within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF, and comparing independent QA samples to Tables B and C with corrective actions triggered by any non‑conforming result [2].

In Superpave design the selected performance‑grade (PG) binder together with the projected equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) determines whether the mixture can resist permanent deformation and low‑temperature thermal cracking. For projects in which the estimated ESAL range exceeds 10 million, the specification should call for a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder on wearing courses to avoid rutting; when the ESAL range is between 0.3 and < 3.0 million, less severe binders may be permitted and even an ESAL limit of < 0.3 million can be used for wearing and binder layers. Only three standard PG‑binders—PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, and PG 64E‑22—are to be specified, and “bumping” the high‑temperature grade (for example from PG 58S‑28 to PG 64S‑28) is intended to provide a stiffer binder at summer pavement temperatures to improve rut resistance. Nonstandard PG‑binder grades may be used only after prior approval from BOCM, Materials Division, Laboratory Testing Section and must be submitted as a Superpave Special Study Project. Polymer‑modified binders such as the approved PG 64E‑22 containing styrene block copolymers (SB or SBS) have demonstrated good performance both in the short‑term construction phase and over a long‑term period of five to ten years, delivering reliable pavement behavior under Pennsylvania’s climate. The application rate for an asphalt tack or prime coat must be chosen according to the porosity of the existing surface, with a lower rate intended for very smooth nonporous surfaces and a higher rate desirable for more porous surfaces. The need for a tack coat is evaluated on a project‑by‑project basis; FB‑1 mixes typically do not require a tack coat while FB‑2 mixes that use PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement sometimes require one. Because the high void content of FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces makes the layer prone to opening, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although this application may be postponed for up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound; FB Modified surfaces may go without a seal coat for at least four years. When a deflection‑based design program assumes Superpave material, designers must apply a structural coefficient of 0.20 for FB overlays to reflect the binder’s lower stiffness, and FB‑2 must be mixed at a stationary plant with hot mix production when PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is used. In Stone Matrix Asphalt the rich mortar binder, often polymer‑modified, supplies durability and rut resistance while also influencing air‑void levels and moisture susceptibility; because SMA relies on stone‑to‑stone contact for strength, the binder must fill voids without exceeding the required air‑void target, which drives aggregate gradation and minimum VMA selection. Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded mixtures incorporate reclaimed ground tire rubber of at least 15 % by weight of the total blend, raising the binder’s viscosity relative to conventional neat binders; this higher viscosity is compatible with the high air‑void content of gap‑graded mixes and permits a greater binder dosage and thicker film coating around aggregate, improving stone‑to‑stone contact and providing resistance to rutting and fatigue comparable to polymer‑modified binders. The high‑viscosity asphalt rubber binder also delivers elastic recovery that translates into greater pavement durability, enhanced resistance to aging and oxidation, and reduced roadway noise; however, such rubberized binders are not eligible for grading under AASHTO M 320 or M 332. Crumb‑rubber modified dense‑graded binders use a lower crumb rubber content and finer gradation (≤#30 mesh), resulting in smaller particle size and lower viscosity that allow incorporation into dense‑graded aggregate with limited void space while still providing coating of the full particle size range; these binders must satisfy the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332 and deliver performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders, improving resistance to reflective, fatigue, low‑temperature cracking and reducing rutting. The binder temperature for interlayer systems must be maintained at no less than 290 °F and not exceed 320 °F; staying within this range preserves the tackiness needed for a uniform spray pattern and proper bonding to geotextile fabrics, fiberglass mesh or composite reinforcing grids, supporting the structural integrity of the interlayer system. For high‑friction surface treatments the binder must be mixed to within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications so that a consistent film can bond uniformly with the aggregate; after mixing it is spread to a uniform thickness of 50–65 mils and an application rate of 12–15 lb per square yard provides the saturation needed for durable skid resistance. Cracks one‑quarter inch or wider are pretreated with the mixed binder, but overfilling must be avoided and any excess scarified after the binder has gelled to maintain a flush surface; the treatment cannot be placed on new asphalt until a mandatory 30‑day cure period has elapsed, and application is prohibited when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or gusts reach 20 mph. The mixture must be delivered and placed at the laying temperatures specified for its material class because the binder’s temperature‑sensitive viscosity governs whether the mix can be compacted to the required density; if the mix is below the minimum temperature, contains a deficiency or excess of asphalt content, or is otherwise unsuitable it must not be added to the pavement mat, as such conditions lead to segregation or flushing that reduce surface macrotexture. Pattern segregation is unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between non‑segregated and segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and flushing is indicated when the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch; both signal performance problems that will require repair or replacement. The binder must be kept free of contaminants and uniformly blended with the stabilizer before it is added to the aggregate; placement is restricted to conditions where the air or surface temperature exceeds 50 °F and the surface is dry, ensuring the binder retains sufficient viscosity for proper adhesion. Vibratory rollers may be used only after a trial demonstrates that vibration does not cause flushing of asphalt binder; otherwise rolling must be stopped to protect pavement integrity, and mixed SMA material must be shipped within two hours of production so that the binder’s temperature and properties remain consistent with the plant‑discharged mix. Storage of modified asphalt or liquid additive shall be in appropriate tanks and any warm‑mix additive or modifier blended with the binder according to documented dosage rates; production must follow the prescribed asphalt production temperature range for normal paving (or special ranges when required) and maintain 90 % of daily printed ticket results within 0.2 percentage points of the Joint Mix Formula, thereby ensuring that volumetric properties such as air voids and VMA meet design limits and govern resistance to stripping and long‑term cracking. Apparent moisture in the mix may not exceed 0.5 %, and any coarse aggregate with water absorption over 2.0 % triggers extra sampling, reflecting how binder–aggregate interaction and moisture control are critical for maintaining designed performance throughout construction and later maintenance. For manual patching the asphalt binder must be placed in lifts that do not exceed three inches uncompacted depth; the tack coat must cure before the binder course is laid, and using the same PG‑binder as the existing pavement (or PG 64S‑22) when sealing patch edges aligns the binder’s properties with the surrounding structure, helping maintain long‑term durability and consistent ride quality. In pervious asphalt systems the binder class—PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22 for the 9.5 mm wearing course and PG 64S‑22 for the 19.0 mm binder course—may be achieved with polymer additives or ground tire rubber, influencing durability; an anti‑strip additive must be blended before mixing at a dosage not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, while a pervious stabilizer (cellulose fibers) is added only after AASHTO T 305 testing indicates need and must be dosed between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix, affecting mix stability. Any irregularities in the binder course must be corrected before the wearing course is placed; practical traffic should not be allowed on the binder course to prevent contamination, and a binder course that cannot be cleaned must be removed and replaced to preserve intended bonding and durability. For asphalt concrete pavements covered by a 60‑month warranty the selected type and class of binder together with its bulk specific gravity and any ignition‑furnace correction factor must be reflected in the volumetric mix design so that voids filled with asphalt (VFA) and related VMA/VTM values support meeting the warranty performance criteria; moisture sensitivity data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg wheel‑tracking results provide a direct measure of the binder’s resistance to stripping and rutting, and any change in binder source must be reported before construction proceeds to ensure consistent behavior throughout placement and subsequent maintenance. In thin asphalt overlay courses the binder content must stay within the tolerances defined in Table B; if a mixture contains greater than 7 % total asphalt content, its draindown sensitivity is evaluated at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F using AASHTO T 305, and fibers are required when draindown exceeds the limits of Table A; producing a mixture with volumetric properties conforming to the tolerances of Table C ensures that the mixture meets design performance criteria and avoids premature failure. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses require the binder to be heated so that its viscosity is sufficient to coat aggregate with an adequate film thickness, providing the required binding properties and preventing segregation; ATPBC is produced at a temperature below 320 °F that also provides suitable viscosity for adequate coating of aggregate particles and does not cause segregation during transportation. Placement of ATPBC cannot occur when the surface or air temperature is below 35 °F or during rain, the mix must be placed within eight hours of production, and after laying the material must be allowed to cool to 100 °F before subsequent layers are installed, allowing proper bonding while avoiding damage from excessive heat.
