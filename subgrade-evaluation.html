Subgrade Evaluation

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
Subgrade Evaluation is a topic under Subgrade.
subgrade-soil-evaluationpavement-subgrade-evaluation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Subgrade Evaluation is the systematic investigation of the soil layer that forms the foundation for all pavement structures, aimed at quantifying its elastic response and bearing capacity so designers can select appropriate pavement types or remedial measures[1]. The evaluation determines either a laboratory‑measured resilient modulus (Mr) or, when Mr is not obtained directly, a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) that must be corroborated by a full suite of companion tests—soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content—to capture variability across cut, fill and at‑grade locations and to reflect seasonal moisture effects, recognizing that spring modulus values are 10 %–20 % lower than normal/summer values and frozen conditions roughly double them[1]. Results that exceed the program thresholds—CBR greater than 10 requiring District Geotechnical Engineer recommendation and Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval, CBR less than 5 triggering required subgrade improvement, and any back‑calculated Mr above 15 000 psi demanding verification—directly shape design choices such as “going‑up” overlays, subdivision of the project into sections with differing designs, or stabilization treatments[1].

Purpose and investigation objectives

Subgrade Evaluation assesses the condition of the soil beneath a pavement structure, which serves as the foundation for all pavement types. The investigation must establish the resilient modulus of the subgrade (recognizing that spring values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen values are typically two times greater) or, when Mr is not measured directly, determine a CBR value and verify that any CBR greater than 10 is recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer while any CBR less than 5 triggers required improvement actions. It also must confirm that the CBR result is supported by laboratory tests of soil classification, gradation, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content and AASHTO Soil Classification Group Index, and ensure samples represent cut, fill and at‑grade locations to capture variability across the project alignment. The subgrade evaluation identifies prevalent clay and silt soils that often fail to support construction equipment after removal of the existing pavement, leading planners to anticipate frequent work‑order adjustments for undercutting and stabilization. This condition prompts consideration of the “going‑up” overlay strategy, which protects unsuitable subgrade from traffic loads and adds vertical distance between the grade line and moisture, thereby improving performance. The same evaluation also signals that shoulder width, slope, guide‑rail and vertical adjustments may increase overall project cost, allowing planners to weigh these items when choosing between overlay and full reconstruction. For all reconstruction projects (including rubblized projects), an additional pavement subgrade evaluation shall be performed as required by Section 6.2.F. The laboratory‑derived resilient modulus and CBR results together with soil classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content give designers a quantitative basis to select the appropriate Mr for flexible pavement design or convert it to an effective subgrade reaction k for rigid pavements. Seasonal stiffness changes can be anticipated because spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer modulus values and frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater than the normal/summer modulus values. Designers use CBR thresholds: values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values less than 5 ensure that recommendations for significant undercutting and/or other methods of subgrade improvement are necessary and justified. Subgrade evaluation results, especially measured CBR values, directly shape planning and design decisions by identifying zones where low CBR requires improvement to achieve values representative of overall project conditions; when isolated low‑CBR areas are found, the subgrade should be considered for improvement. All CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and in no case will a CBR value greater than 20 be allowed to be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. The evaluation also informs whether the project can be divided into subsections with different pavement designs when large variations in soil type and CBR exist, guiding rehabilitation strategies by adjusting conversion factors for in‑situ densities and applying more conservative equations when subgrade density is significantly less than maximum dry density. When considerable uncertainty exists regarding the validity of laboratory‑tested CBR values, additional laboratory and/or field testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or Dynamic Cone Penetration should be performed, and any back‑calculated resilient modulus above 15 000 psi must be verified by another form of testing before use. For reconstruction projects, gradation, in‑place moisture content, density and CBR tests are required; if CBR values are less than 5, undercutting or stabilization may be required, influencing cost estimates and construction sequencing. The findings also enable planners to map soil type distribution across the alignment, set compaction specifications, and evaluate long‑term performance and maintenance needs. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 57–58), Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Scope and applicability

Subgrade Evaluation is required whenever a pavement is being reconstructed or replaced, including rubblized work, because the condition of the subgrade soil is very important in such projects and low‑support clay or silt soils are common statewide. The evaluation also applies when an overlay—either asphalt or concrete—is considered for existing concrete pavements that have been patched, fractured, or rubblized, since the overlay protects unsuitable subgrade from construction traffic. It addresses the subgrade soil layer beneath all structural layers, regardless of whether the material is cut, fill, or at‑grade, and must consider seasonal moisture effects that can cause spring modulus values to be 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen conditions to be typically two times greater than normal/summer values. Subgrade Evaluation is required for any pavement reconstruction project—including rubblized projects—and is routinely applied to interstate and expressway designs where laboratory resilient‑modulus or CBR data are used. When CBR values are greater than 10, a higher level of analysis and geotechnical approval is mandated; when CBR values are less than 5, recommendations for significant undercutting or other subgrade improvement measures are triggered. The evaluation is also called for when rehabilitation overlays leave the original pavement in place so that in‑situ density and potential low CBR values (less than 5) are considered before using laboratory values. It becomes appropriate for full‑depth asphalt sections as well as for asphalt overlays on thin concrete (< 8 in), on concrete suffering severe ASR degradation, on pre‑1945 concrete, or when pavement is placed directly on subgrade/subbase (rare). If back‑calculated resilient modulus exceeds 15,000 psi, it must be verified by another form of testing prior to its use in the pavement design process. Subgrade strengthening would be a cost‑effective solution whether the existing CBR is below or above a CBR of 5, and the assessment should also be performed for projects of at least 25,000 SY on Traffic Pattern Groups 1‑4 when frost‑susceptible soils, differential heaving conditions, rock cuts, isolated low‑CBR zones, or large variations in soil type are present. DCP testing may be used only on roadways with MFC B, C, D or E. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 57–58), Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

In section 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS the subgrade evaluation based solely on laboratory CBR or resilient‑modulus tests is limited when the measured CBR exceeds ten or falls below five because such extreme values may produce unrealistic Mr estimates and therefore require additional review by the District Geotechnical Engineer; likewise, any CBR greater than ten must receive approval from the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and values above twenty are prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. The evaluation is also limited when CBR results are unusually high or low, vary widely across the project, or when a CBR greater than twenty would be required for the Heukelom‑Klomp equation, in which case the value must be rejected or approved only after additional scrutiny. If CBR values cannot be obtained at all, designers should consult with the Highway Design and Technology Section of the Bureau of Design and Delivery and also with their District Geotechnical Engineer to consider other possible options. Seasonal moisture effects further limit a single‑time modulus measurement because spring modulus values are typically ten percent to twenty percent lower than normal or summer values and frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater than the normal/summer values, so testing should represent the range of conditions or be supplemented with field methods such as FWD to locate representative sampling spots. Moreover, when back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD yields a value above 15 000 psi, it must be verified by another form of testing prior to its use in the pavement design process. When any of these thresholds are met or when considerable uncertainty exists with regard to the validity of the laboratory tested CBR value, a complementary investigation—such as additional laboratory tests (soil classification, gradation, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density) or field testing such as FWD or DCP—should be employed in consultation with the District Geotechnical Engineer. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Existing information and records

Subgrade Evaluation must close record gaps concerning whether existing laboratory CBR and resilient‑modulus values truly represent the site’s variability, because designers are required to verify CBR results with companion tests and to confirm that values greater than 10 or less than 5 have been reviewed by the District Geotechnical Engineer. The records often show laboratory CBR results that are unusually high or low, display a wide spread across the site, or lack accompanying in‑situ density data, creating considerable uncertainty about which value truly represents the subgrade; consequently field work such as FWD or DCP testing is required to back‑calculate Mr and confirm the appropriate design CBR. Field sampling therefore has to capture cut, fill and at‑grade locations and seasonal moisture conditions so that spring modulus (10% to 20% lower) and frozen modulus (approximately twice normal) can be measured. In addition, any CBR greater than 10 must be approved and values above 20 cannot be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp equation, so verification of extreme laboratory numbers is needed. When a back‑calculated resilient modulus exceeds 15,000 psi, another test must be performed to validate it because the back‑calculation can be unreliable if pavement layer information or condition is unknown. Uncertainties in soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content must be resolved by collecting representative samples throughout the alignment to ensure the laboratory data support the assumed subgrade performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Field measurements and testing

Subgrade evaluation may begin with a Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) test, which applies a 9,000‑pound impact per ASTM D 4694/D 4695 to obtain midslab deflection readings at locations that are not cracked; the resulting deflection data provide an indication of the in‑situ elastic response and can be back‑calculated to a subgrade resilient modulus (Mr). The back‑calculated Mr must be adjusted with a correction factor C = 0.25 and divided by two to convert dynamic k to static k, and if the resulting value exceeds 15,000 psi it must be verified by another test before use in design. The FWD can also be used to locate representative sampling points for laboratory testing. Laboratory tests then include a Moisture Density Test (PTM No. 106) to determine optimum moisture content and maximum dry density, an Unconfined Compressive Strength test (AASHTO T 208) to gauge soil strength, a Soil Classification test (AASHTO M 145) to identify the AASHTO group, and a Resilient Modulus test (AASHTO T 307) which directly measures the elastic modulus Mr of the subgrade. When laboratory resilient modulus testing is impractical, a soaked California Bearing Ratio test (AASHTO T 193) may be used; its result is multiplied by 1500 to estimate Mr, and CBR values greater than 10 trigger a higher level of analysis while values less than 5 require justification for subgrade improvement. Laboratory CBR values may also be used as an alternative to laboratory Mr for interstates and expressways. A Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) test drives a metal cone into the ground using a 17.6‑pound hammer dropped from 2.26 ft, recording penetration per blow; this provides an in‑situ resistance that can be converted to CBR and then to Mr, indicating shear strength of the subgrade. Field measurements of gradation, in‑place moisture content, and in‑place density are taken to characterize soil type and compaction before using CBR values for design. All laboratory results are provided to the project manager and must be verified against other soil properties such as gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, and Group Index before being used in design. [1]

Equipment for subgrade evaluation includes a Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) to locate sampling points and laboratory instruments that perform the Moisture Density Test (PTM No. 106), Unconfined Compressive Strength Test (AASHTO T 208), Soil Classification (AASHTO M 145), Resilient Modulus Test (AASHTO T 307) and, when used as an alternative, a CBR test on soaked specimens per AASHTO T 193. The test configuration calls for submitting three 50‑pound bags of representative soil to the PennDOT laboratory where the full suite of tests is run, with CBR testing performed on soaked samples if it replaces resilient modulus determination. Testing must be completed prior to pavement design and should capture seasonal effects by using stress and moisture conditions that simulate seasons with significantly different moisture, recognizing that spring modulus values are typically 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen subgrade values are typically about twice the normal/summer values. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Sampling and laboratory investigation

Subgrade Evaluation shall submit three 50‑pound bags of representative soil to the PennDOT Laboratory where a Moisture Density Test identified as PTM No. 106, an Unconfined Compressive Strength Test designated AASHTO T 208, a Soil Classification test specified by AASHTO M 145, and a Resilient Modulus test performed in accordance with AASHTO T 307 are performed; alternatively, soaked CBR testing per AASHTO T 193 may be used to estimate the resilient modulus. These laboratory procedures provide the resilient modulus required for flexible‑pavement design and, when converted, the effective subgrade reaction k for rigid pavements, while also yielding soil classification, gradation, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content and the AASHTO Group Index, all of which indicate moisture sensitivity, compressibility, frost‑heave potential and overall suitability of the subgrade. The program additionally includes gradation testing to determine particle‑size distribution for soil classification and to support the CBR test, moisture‑content determination to measure the in‑place water ratio, density testing to assess compaction, and the CBR test itself to quantify the potential strength of cohesive subgrade material used for resilient‑modulus development. When subgrade strengthening is contemplated, further laboratory tests are required: soil gradation with hydrometer and classification, liquid limit, plastic limit and plasticity index to characterize plastic behavior, shrinkage limit (AASHTO T92), unconfined compressive strength and dry density to evaluate strength and compaction potential, sulfate and chloride concentrations and organic content to assess chemical suitability for cement stabilization, and pH when lime stabilization is considered. Seasonal testing is recommended so that spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater than the normal/summer modulus values. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Structural and functional performance

The subgrade evaluation should quantify the soil’s elastic response through the resilient modulus (Mr), obtained from laboratory testing or field Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) surveys, and may alternatively use California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values that are converted to Mr; both require supporting moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength, and classification tests (AASHTO T307, T208, M 145). Deflection measurements are obtained with an FWD at midslab locations that are not cracked using a 9,000‑pound load in accordance with ASTM D 4694 and D 4695, and the resulting back‑calculated Mr is used to assess bearing strength. The back‑calculated Mr value must be adjusted to make it consistent with the laboratory‑measured value used in the development of the flexible pavement design equation. Field Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) tests furnish in‑situ resistance values that can be converted to Mr. Additional measurements such as gradation parameters, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index), Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content are recorded to verify that CBR‑derived Mr is realistic. Seasonal effects are captured by noting that spring modulus values are 10 % to 20 % lower than normal/summer values while frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater. CBR values above 10 or below 5 trigger higher‑level review, and the CBR test must not exceed 20 for use in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Measured CBR values from laboratory or field testing are checked against the program’s thresholds, with any value greater than 10 requiring approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and any value greater than 20 prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation (which caps the product at 30,000 psi). When isolated low‑CBR spots are found, especially if the CBR is below 5, the subgrade should be improved or strengthened—particularly on projects with Traffic Pattern Groups 1 to 4 where a cost/benefit analysis may favor treatment. Resilient modulus (Mr) values derived from tests must also meet limits; Mr values above 15,000 psi need verification by additional testing, and values between 20,000 and 30,000 psi should be used with extreme caution to ensure the design assumptions remain valid for the intended service life. By adhering to these comparison criteria, the evaluated subgrade conditions are aligned with expected traffic loads, design assumptions, applicable thresholds, and the durability requirements of the pavement’s remaining service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Data interpretation and diagnosis

During subgrade evaluation the engineer first notes any visually identified zones where CBR values appear unusually low and flags those isolated areas for possible improvement so that the final design reflects conditions representative of the whole project. Laboratory CBR results are then screened against the thresholds that require Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval for values greater than 10 and prohibit using any value above 20 in the Heukelom‑and‑Klomp correlation; outlier test results that do not match the soil type or other samples are discarded, and when a wide spread of CBR values exists the controlling (lowest) soil type is selected. To confirm or refine the laboratory numbers, field measurements such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or Dynamic Cone Penetration tests are performed to back‑calculate the resilient modulus (Mr), and any Mr exceeding 15 000 psi must be verified by an additional test before it can be used. For rehabilitation projects where the existing pavement remains in place, the in‑situ density of the subgrade is compared with laboratory CBR values and a more conservative conversion factor (multiply by 1,000 rather than 1,500) is applied to avoid overestimating strength. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

In section 2.2 Subgrade Soil Evaluation, the low‑subgrade support is primarily attributed to the widespread presence of weak clay and silt soils that do not adequately bear construction equipment loads, and to elevated moisture conditions that further diminish soil strength when the subgrade is exposed during reconstruction; these factors together create inadequate drainage and environmental exposure to high moisture levels that compromise foundation stability. Section 6.2 Subgrade Soils notes that observed subgrade problems are most often traced to insufficient drainage, low bearing capacity as indicated by low CBR values, frost‑related material degradation, and abrupt subgrade transitions that reflect construction shortcomings; differential heaving caused by abrupt changes in soil type or groundwater conditions points to poor grading or backfill practices, while freeze‑thaw cycles introduce excess moisture and damage the materials themselves. [1]

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Subgrade Evaluation confirms that low‑support soils—typically clay or silt that have failed to support construction equipment after removal of the existing pavement—are a statewide issue requiring stabilization before reconstruction and provides the basis for selecting an overlay (“going‑up”) strategy that shields these weak materials from traffic and can improve long‑term performance. The evaluation also reveals that an overlay is not automatically the most cost‑effective alternative because required adjustments to shoulder width, slopes, guide rails or vertical clearances may increase overall project costs, leaving uncertainty about the optimal treatment for a given site. The subgrade evaluation can confirm the elastic stiffness of the foundation by providing a resilient modulus (Mr) derived either from direct laboratory testing or estimated from CBR using the Heukelom‑Klomp equation, and it supplies ancillary soil properties—classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content—that together justify whether the measured CBR is realistic for design. Seasonal effects are captured because spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater than the normal/summer values, allowing designers to account for moisture‑driven variability. The reliability of the Mr estimate remains uncertain when CBR values are greater than 10 or less than 5; such extreme results require additional review by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, as well as verification against the full suite of laboratory tests, and site heterogeneity may limit confidence unless samples represent all cut, fill, and at‑grade conditions. The evaluation can confirm that isolated low‑CBR zones should be improved to bring the CBR up to values representative of the overall project, and when laboratory results show a wide range the design should be based on the soil type with the lowest CBR so the pavement will be adequate for all areas; it also permits dividing a project into a limited number of subsections when distinct soil types and CBR values exist, provided transitions can be accommodated. The investigation is limited by potential non‑representative laboratory samples, uncertainty when CBR values exceed 10 without Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval, and an absolute prohibition on using any CBR greater than 20 in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation (i.e., 1,500 × 20 = 30,000 psi maximum). Back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD may be unreliable—values above 15 000 psi must be verified by another test—and considerable uncertainty in laboratory CBR requires additional lab or field testing such as FWD or DCP. [1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 57–58), Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

After the initial subgrade soil assessment, PennDOT requires that every reconstruction effort—including rubblized projects—perform an additional pavement subgrade evaluation and consider a going‑up overlay approach to shield weak clay or silt soils from construction traffic; the overlay utilizes the existing pavement structure and raises the grade line away from high‑moisture zones, which should ultimately improve performance. Any necessary shoulder width, slope, guide rail, or vertical adjustments at structures must be examined because they may adversely affect overall project cost. Following that evaluation, the designer shall obtain resilient‑modulus values by submitting representative samples for laboratory Mr testing in accordance with AASHTO T 307 together with moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength and classification tests, or alternatively conduct soaked CBR testing in accordance with AASHTO T 193 when appropriate; seasonal testing is required so that spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen values are typically two times greater. The resulting CBR results must be verified against the companion laboratory data—including soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content—to confirm that the subgrade can achieve the estimated Mr. If any CBR value exceeds 10, the District Geotechnical Engineer shall recommend its use and obtain Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval; if any CBR value is less than 5 a recommendation for significant undercutting or other improvement methods shall be justified. When low CBR values are found in isolated spots, the subgrade should be improved to bring those values up to the project‑wide representative level, coordinating improvement limits, quantities and special provisions with the District Geotechnical Engineer and documenting them in the PS&E. If laboratory CBR results appear uncertain or show a wide range, additional laboratory work and field testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or Dynamic Cone Penetration should be performed to back‑calculate the resilient modulus for validation, using a 1,000 multiplier rather than 1,500 in the Heukelom‑Klomp equation when in‑situ density is well below maximum dry density; any CBR greater than 10 must receive Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval and values above 20 are prohibited for that correlation. Finally, when CBR falls below five, subgrade strengthening options—including cement or lime stabilization, rock capping, geogrid or geotextile reinforcement, frost‑susceptible soil removal, drainage installation, selective grading or gradual transitions—must be evaluated, designed and costed, with the minimum acceptable resilient modulus specified for both existing and borrow materials.
