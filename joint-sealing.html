Joint Sealing

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Joint Sealing is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
joint-sealingjoint-sealjoint-sealscleaning-and-sealingjoint-cleaning-and-sealing
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Joint sealing is a preventive‑maintenance treatment applied to transverse, longitudinal and construction joints in concrete pavements when early‑stage distress such as spalling, faulting or surface cracking becomes evident [1][2]. The method involves thoroughly cleaning the joint faces, removing all debris, moisture and foreign material, and then filling the resulting reservoir with a rubberized or hot‑pour asphalt sealant that fully occupies the cavity to create a watertight barrier against water, chlorides and other incompressible agents [1][2]. By eliminating infiltration and the associated pumping action, the sealed joint restores load‑transfer capacity, arrests further settlement and spalling, and thereby extends the service life of the pavement [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Joint Sealing” also include the following subtopics: Joint Sealant.

Joint Sealing utilizes Joint Sealant as the primary material input, which is placed into prepared concrete or asphalt pavement joints and cracks after thorough cleaning—typically following diamond grinding—to form a reservoir with the correct shape factor that protects the joint interior. [1] Specified sealants may be rubberized, hot‑pour silicone or neoprene products, each required to meet temperature, dimensional and mechanical limits before being applied as a recessed fill level below the surface. [2]

Joint Sealant

Purpose and treatment objectives

Joint sealing targets concrete‑pavement joints—both transverse and longitudinal—when they begin to exhibit early‑stage distress such as spalling, faulting, or other surface breakdown that creates openings for external agents [1][2]. The primary performance problem addressed is the infiltration of water, chlorides, cement mortar, scale, dirt, curing compounds, and other incompressible materials into joint cavities, which can initiate pumping action beneath the slab [1][2]. This ingress leads to subgrade consolidation, settlement, and the formation of small voids under the slab, reducing support and causing excessive slab deflection and stress [1][2]. The loss of support manifests as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, delamination through joints, and ultimately complete slab breakup if left untreated [1][2]. By thoroughly cleaning each joint, removing all foreign material, and applying a sealant that fully fills the joint reservoir to create a watertight barrier, the treatment restores load‑transfer capability and arrests further deterioration, thereby preserving pavement service life [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 273–314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–418)

Joint sealing is classified as a preventive‑maintenance activity that aims primarily to keep water, chlorides and other incompressible materials from entering transverse, construction or expansion joints [1][2]. By blocking these agents the treatment prevents pumping, faulting and subsequent cracking, which in turn slows further loss of load‑transfer capacity and overall deterioration of the concrete slab [1][2]. The practice is routinely applied on a regular schedule—often every five years for existing pavements—and must be completed before construction equipment or traffic are allowed onto new sections, ensuring that joints are watertight from the outset [1][2]. Through this continual protection the treatment retards the progression of distress and contributes to extending the service life of the pavement structure [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 273–274), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Distress characterization and causes

Joint sealing is directed at the intentional gaps in concrete pavements—both transverse and longitudinal joints—as well as cracks that develop beneath these joints, particularly at the outer corners of slabs where small voids tend to form. At first the distress manifests as low‑severity signs of joint deterioration, such as early void formation and minor surface cracking. If these initial symptoms are not corrected, the voids expand, causing settlement and pumping of water and incompressible materials into the pavement structure. This leads to excessive slab deflection and stress, which in turn produces transverse‑joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and eventually full‑depth slab breakup. The progression moves from subtle voids to visible surface faults and structural failure, increasing the severity of the distress over time. Operationally, untreated joints diminish pavement serviceability by impairing load transfer and reducing support beneath the slabs, which can create hazardous riding conditions and pose safety risks for traffic. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92)

Joint sealing targets a set of interrelated mechanisms that generate or accelerate distress in concrete pavement joints, chiefly the ingress of water, chlorides and other incompressible materials through deteriorated sealants [1][2]. When moisture penetrates an unfilled or contaminated joint cavity it may freeze, expand, and disrupt load transfer, thereby accelerating cracking and reducing structural capacity [2]. The presence of cement mortar, scale, dirt or other rigid inclusions in the sealant reservoir further interferes with the pavement’s ability to distribute traffic stresses, acting as non‑compressible bodies that promote faulting and spalling [2][1]. Ineffective drainage systems exacerbate these processes by allowing a pumping action combined with subgrade consolidation and settlement to create voids beneath transverse joints and cracks [1]. The resulting loss of support produces excessive slab deflection, faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately complete slab breakup [1]. Heavy traffic loads open the joints further, increasing the magnitude of load‑related stresses that contribute to joint widening and spalling [1]. Climate‑material interactions such as frost damage, aggregate deterioration, alkali‑silica reaction or issues associated with Vanport Limestone weaken the concrete matrix over time, compounding the effects of water ingress [1]. An inadequate subbase or subgrade can precipitate premature mid‑slab cracking and diminish load‑transfer capacity, thereby accelerating faulting and spalling [1]. To interrupt these mechanisms the guides require thorough joint preparation, including removal of debris, moisture and contaminants so that the surface is dry before sealant placement [2][1]. After cleaning, a minimum of six inches of hot or emulsified asphalt must be applied on each side of the joint, with controlled placement rates to ensure the residual material completely fills all voids and creates a watertight bond [2]. When sealants are placed after diamond grinding, the joint should be shaped to achieve the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir, which helps maintain load transfer and structural capacity [1]. Resealing is recommended at the first sign of distress—often on a five‑year maintenance cycle—and should employ a rubberized material that restores the barrier against water and preserves load transfer until more extensive rehabilitation is required [1]. By addressing water infiltration, eliminating non‑compressible inclusions, ensuring adequate sealant width and bonding, and maintaining functional drainage, joint sealing mitigates both moisture‑related and load‑related distress mechanisms that would otherwise degrade pavement performance [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 273–274), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Applicability and treatment selection

Joint sealing is suitable for both rigid Portland cement concrete pavements that are being patched rather than overlaid and for flexible pavement sections that contain intentional gaps such as notched‑wedge joints, transverse saw cuts, paving notches, lane additions, utility openings, or longitudinal butt joints in surface courses. [1][2] For concrete pavements the treatment is applied when transverse or longitudinal joints are just beginning to exhibit minor distress—such as early faulting or spalling—while the overall serviceability of the slab remains acceptable, and the underlying subgrade is stable with a functional drainage system that can prevent water, chlorides, or incompressible material from infiltrating the structure. [1] In addition, any joint that has been widened by a second‑stage saw cut must be sealed before the pavement section is opened to construction equipment or traffic. [2] The concrete joints or cracks should be routed to approximately three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep, flushed, thoroughly cleaned and dried, and the sealant may only be placed after the concrete has attained its initial strength and all curing requirements have been satisfied. [2] For flexible pavements, joints whose opening is between one quarter inch and one inch are sealed with a hot asphalt binder—typically PG 64S‑22 cement or an NMAS mixture not exceeding 19 mm—heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F; joints wider than one inch are filled with a wearing course rather than a sealant. [2] In all cases the joint faces must be clean, dry, and free of loose material or debris before sealant application, and a backer rod (and any required tape bond breaker) should be installed where appropriate. [2] Ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature must each be at least 40 °F; temperatures above 90 °F are prohibited unless a special waiver is granted, and placement below 40 °F is only allowed with an explicit exception from the responsible authority. [2] Traffic may not be permitted onto a sealed rigid joint until the epoxy or anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, unless an approved protective method is used; for flexible joints adjacent to opposing traffic the new asphalt mixture must be placed within one working day of sealant placement, and when traffic moves in the same direction the window extends to two working days. [2] After sealant placement the area should be protected from vehicular loads until the pavement cools to at least 140 °F or for a minimum of 24 hours, whichever is longer, to ensure proper curing and bond development. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 269–339), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–464)

Joint sealing should not be performed when the concrete pavement is scheduled to receive an overlay, whether with unbonded Portland cement concrete or asphalt, because sealants would become trapped beneath the new surface and thus defeat their purpose [1][2]. When an overlay is planned, the joints must instead be cleaned and filled with a thin layer of asphalt wearing‑course material, or, if spalling is severe, full‑depth concrete patching may be required [1]. Extensive spalls that extend beyond the top half of a slab also preclude simple sealing; in such cases partial‑depth or full‑depth joint repairs are called for [1]. The guides further restrict sealing to expansion joints only when an overlay is part of the work, prohibiting widening of other joints with second‑stage saw cuts and requiring alternative treatments for those widened joints [2]. Successful sealant placement demands that joint faces be thoroughly cleaned and dried to the satisfaction of the overseeing representative; failure to achieve this level of cleanliness makes sealing inappropriate [2]. The ambient air or pavement surface temperature must remain between 40 °F and 90 °F unless a written waiver is obtained, because temperatures outside this range prevent proper bonding of the sealant [2]. Additionally, the joint geometry must meet specific dimensions: the opening should be routed to at least three‑quarters of an inch in width and one inch in depth, and its final width may not exceed one inch when asphalt sealant is used; joints wider than one inch require placement of an Asphalt Wearing Course instead of sealant [2]. The sealant material itself must be applied within the manufacturer’s prescribed temperature range and placed promptly after heating, with the material filled to just below the pavement surface; any deviation from these preparation or application requirements necessitates a different maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach [2]. Thus, whenever any of these overlay, spalling, cleanliness, temperature, dimensional, or material‑application conditions are not satisfied, joint sealing is deemed unsuitable and another appropriate treatment must be selected [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Condition assessment and timing

The determination of whether joint sealing is required begins with a systematic visual survey of every transverse, longitudinal and shoulder joint, looking for early signs such as hairline cracks, spalling, transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines on the lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow‑holes), corner breaks, or any indication that slab support has been lost [1]. The inspector then verifies that any transverse or longitudinal joint has been widened by a second‑stage saw cut and confirms that the joint faces are clean, dry and free of scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material or other foreign matter before proceeding [2]. In the presence of the Representative, concrete is sounded with a steel bar, drag chains or a hammer to locate delamination and determine whether it propagates through an existing joint, providing a direct indication of joint compromise [2]. When distress is observed, cores are extracted from the vicinity of the distressed joint as well as from random locations so that subbase or subgrade conditions and the depth of damage beneath the surface can be assessed [1]. Deflection testing and faulting measurements follow, and additional nondestructive techniques—including ground‑penetrating radar, friction and roughness surveys, MIRA assessments, MIT‑Scan 2 for dowel bar alignment, MIT‑Scan T2 for PCCP thickness, and tensile bond tests—are employed to confirm which joints have lost integrity and where slab stabilization may be needed [1]. Falling Weight Deflectometer data are examined because they reveal concrete‑pavement joint efficiency and can pinpoint locations that require patching or sealing [1]. Ambient air temperature is measured and must be above 40 °F before sealant application, while the asphalt cement sealant itself is heated and its temperature monitored to remain between 265 °F and 320 °F during placement, ensuring suitable conditions for successful sealing [2]. The shape factor of the sealant reservoir is measured to verify conformity with the dimensions prescribed in Publication 408, Section 521 before the joint can be sealed [1]. If delamination reaches a transverse joint, ½‑in expansion material is installed; if it reaches a longitudinal joint, ¼‑in preformed cellular polystyrene is placed, after which sealing proceeds according to the applicable specifications [2]. A QC wipe test prescribed by PTM No. 643 is performed at two locations on each of the first three saw‑cut joints for that day, with additional wipes taken as required, to confirm that the joint surface is clean and ready for sealant placement [2]. No depth or density testing is required to determine the need for sealing; the decision rests on the visual, acoustic, core, deflection, nondestructive, and temperature assessments described above [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 338–339), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–418)

Joint sealing is considered a preventive‑maintenance activity that should be performed either on a regular preservation schedule—typically every five years for concrete networks—or at the first indication of joint distress [1]. The guides agree that sealing should be accelerated when early‑stage symptoms appear, such as minor cracking, loss of sealant, transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines adjacent to joints, small blow‑hole depressions in shoulders, corner breaks, or general slab settlement suggesting water infiltration and pumping [1]. In addition, the guides require sealing whenever a joint has been intentionally opened, widened, or exposed during construction or repair activities, including after a second‑stage saw cut creates an opening, after placement and compaction of a longitudinal surface‑course joint, after a partial‑depth repair that cuts through or terminates at an existing transverse or longitudinal joint, and after spall repairs that leave residual spalls two inches or less [2]. Before sealant placement the joint faces must be thoroughly cleaned and completely dry, and both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature must each be at least 40 °F; when hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is used its temperature must be maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F [2]. For cracks prepared by sawing or grinding, the opening should be at least three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep (or up to one inch wide and 1¼ inches deep for larger cracks) before sealant is applied, with a backer rod installed where required and the sealant placed slightly below pavement level [2]. If a joint—particularly a transverse joint—has already failed and has not been previously rehabilitated, it must first be repaired before sealant can be effective; otherwise existing joints are simply cleaned and resealed to protect against further intrusion [1]. This combined timing approach ensures that sealing is performed while the pavement remains in good condition with significant remaining service life, thereby maintaining slab support and extending overall pavement longevity [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 11 (pp. 193–194), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 269–339), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–464)

Materials and repair details

Joint sealing begins with thorough cleaning of the joint or crack, removing any existing sealant, asphalt, neoprene compression seals or other foreign material, and then eliminating all loose debris, oil and moisture by media‑blasting the vertical faces followed by an oil‑free compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi (or a water blaster delivering ten gallons per minute at seven thousand psi for wide joints) [1][2]. The cleaned joint faces must be dry and both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are required to be no lower than 40 °F and not exceed 90 °F unless written approval is obtained [2]. For full‑depth joints a four‑inch wide cut extending through the entire pavement thickness is made, while longitudinal cracks or narrower joints are routed to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch (or widened to one inch by 1¼ inches where the original crack is larger) [2]. When diamond grinding has been performed, sealant must be placed promptly so that the reservoir attains the proper shape before the material can be damaged, and the resulting reservoir must satisfy the shape‑factor requirements specified in Publication 408 [1]. For pavement‑to‑shoulder joints a reservoir of 0.75 in by 0.75 in is constructed, and transverse joints are treated similarly with reservoirs sized to meet the same shape‑factor criteria [1]. In cases where a joint is severely spalled before an overlay, the void is first filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material or an equivalent product as required by Section 469 of Publication 408 [1]. Prior to sealant placement a tape bond‑breaker is laid on the bottom of the reservoir; it may be self‑adhesive or duct tape but must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the joint faces [2][1]. An oversized backer rod whose diameter exceeds the joint opening by 25 % to 35 % (or roughly fifty percent in some applications) is positioned so that its top lies recessed below the pavement surface, providing a sealant depth‑to‑width ratio of approximately one‑to‑one to two‑to‑one [2]. For partial‑depth repairs the transverse side receives a half‑inch expansion‑joint insert and the longitudinal side a quarter‑inch pre‑formed cellular polystyrene filler, each extending at least one‑half inch below the repair bottom; these materials are installed when the repair abuts existing joints [2]. The repair zone itself must be rectangular with minimum dimensions of 15 in by 10 in by 2 in [2]. The sealing material may be an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant, or a rubberized joint sealant listed in Bulletin 15 and applied in accordance with ASTM D6690 (Type II or Type IV for asphalt pavement joints and only Type IV—or Type I where permitted—for other joints) [2][1]. Silicone sealants are required to be low‑modulus, non‑sag, meet a tensile elongation limit of 150 % per ASTM D412, possess specified extrusion rates through a 1/8‑in orifice, specific gravity, Shore A hardness after cure, shelf life, ozone and UV resistance, flow rating, bond strength to primed cement mortar, and a maximum tack‑free time, while rubberized sealants must conform to the same bulletins [2]. Pre‑formed neoprene compression seals may be used when free of pinholes, capable of operating from –20 °F to +140 °F, not stretched more than three percent or compressed more than two percent, and meeting width‑and‑height tolerances (no more than 1/16 in over nominal for widths up to 1½ in) with a minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of four psi at 85 % nominal width [2]. For surface courses the preferred material is hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder heated between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied with a pressure applicator wand or nozzle that produces a straight band approximately four inches wide (±1 inch) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1/32 inch), centered within an inch of the joint [2]. The hot asphalt is placed to a level leaving the finished surface one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement grade, excess material is removed, any missed spots are resealed, and a thin film of sealant remains about 1/32‑1/16 inch thick over a wipe width of one to three inches [2]. An alternative surface‑course sealant may be an emulsified asphalt such as PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT, applied evenly at least six inches on each side of the joint with sufficient volume to fill surface voids and create a watertight connection; multiple applications are permitted for emulsified products [2]. Heavy‑duty membranes may be employed where additional protection is required, and saw cutting is often performed prior to sealing an existing transverse or patch joint that will receive an overlay [1]. All sealant lots must be accompanied by an eCAMMS laboratory report, proper lot marking, and must adhere to linear‑foot production limits; sealant batches must be kept within the manufacturer’s safe pouring temperature and not held longer than six hours [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 269–339), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–464), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 673–676)

The durability of a joint‑sealing treatment begins with the condition of the concrete that forms the joint, where surfaces that have been diamond‑ground to achieve the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir are less prone to mechanical damage than joints exhibiting high percentages of patching, low compressive strength, excessive air content, or weak aggregate durability. The sealant itself must conform to the specifications outlined in Publication 408 (Sections 512 and 521), requiring a rubberized material that can bond effectively to sound concrete while also meeting performance criteria such as tensile stress at 150 % elongation, minimum elongation at maximum strength, appropriate Shore A hardness, and resistance to ozone and ultraviolet exposure. In addition to the rubberized option, hot‑pour asphalt binders such as PG 64S‑22 heated between 265 °F and 320 °F, other approved hot‑asphalt classes, or emulsified asphalt meeting PennDOT Class TACK or NTT/CNTT requirements are acceptable provided they are kept within their manufacturer‑specified temperature window and not allowed to remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours without reheating. Prior to placement, both vertical faces of the joint must be thoroughly cleaned of scale, dirt, oil, curing compounds, moisture, or any other contaminant, a process that may involve media blasting or a 100‑psi stream of compressed air free of oil and water, followed by drying to the satisfaction of the representative. When a bond‑breaker tape is employed it may not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the joint face, and any backer rod used must be limited to an extension of one‑eighth inch above the bottom of the sealant reservoir. Compatibility with the surrounding pavement requires that the joint not be scheduled for an overlay, because sealing is discouraged on sections slated for such treatment. Stable subgrade conditions and a functional drainage system are also essential compatibility factors, as they prevent water, chlorides, and incompressible materials from entering the joint and degrading the sealant bond. The geometry of the joint dictates the type and dimensions of filler material; transverse joints receive half‑inch expansion joint material while longitudinal joints use quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene, each extending at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the repair. If steel plates intrude into the reservoir their protrusion may not exceed one‑quarter inch, and when the existing joint is wider than the required sealant reservoir it must be cleaned to the proper depth or backed with material to achieve the correct width. Backer rods are sized 25–35 % larger in diameter than the opening and positioned so that their top lies one to two times the joint width plus a quarter‑inch below the pavement surface, creating a recessed sealant depth that maintains a depth‑to‑width ratio of approximately 1:1 to 2:1. The finished seal should be placed only when ambient air and pavement temperatures are at least 40 °F, with most specifications capping the upper limit at 90 °F unless a waiver is granted, and it should fill the reservoir to just below the surface, leaving an allowance of one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch while extending at least six inches on each side to eliminate voids. Early intervention improves performance; resealing is recommended when joints first show minor distress such as hairline cracking with retained aggregate interlock, limited spalling, or small faulting, allowing serviceability to be maintained without resorting to deeper rehabilitation. Existing pavement conditions that undermine sealing include premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, joint spalling, and joint faulting identified during routine surveys, all of which tend to recur if not addressed promptly. Because these distress types are recurrent, the guidelines treat joint sealing as a routine maintenance activity with a recommended resealing interval of five years for concrete pavements. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 263–339), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–464), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 673–676)

Treatment procedures

Joint sealing is undertaken only after all spall repairs have been completed and the repair limits are clearly defined. The contractor then creates a sealant reservoir by making second‑stage saw cuts that follow the project drawings; typical dimensions include a four‑inch wide, full‑depth cut for longitudinal joints or a three‑quarter‑inch wide by one‑inch deep cut for cracks, while pavement‑shoulder joints often require a 0.75 in × 0.75 in reservoir [1][2]. All existing joint sealing material, neoprene compression seals, asphalt, and other foreign substances are removed to expose a clean concrete surface, with the cleaning depth extending at least 1½ inches [2]. The newly cut faces are flushed with water, loose debris is swept away with a power broom, sidewalls are media‑blasted and roughened, and the vertical faces are blown dry using oil‑free, moisture‑free compressed air at approximately 100 psi; a QC wipe test performed in the presence of the Representative confirms that the surfaces are dry and free of contaminants [2]. A bond‑breaker tape is placed on the bottom of the reservoir, ensuring it does not extend more than one‑eighth inch up each face [2][1]. Ambient temperature must be between 40 °F and 90 °F unless a deviation is authorized, and both air and pavement surface temperatures must be at least 40 °F before sealant placement; the sealant—whether an approved hot‑pour, silicone, or hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder—is heated to the manufacturer’s safe pouring temperature, with asphalt binder maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F and not held longer than six hours without reheating [2][1]. Placement is performed with a pressure applicator wand or nozzle capable of delivering a straight, consistent band about four inches wide (±1 inch) and approximately one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1/32 inch); the sealant is poured to fill the joint leaving the surface ⅛‑¼ inch below the pavement and extending at least six inches on each side to ensure complete void fill [2][1]. If a backer rod is specified, it is inserted before placement, sized 25–35 % larger than the opening so its top lies one to two times the joint width plus about a quarter inch below the surface, creating a recessed profile; excess material is trimmed and the surface finished flush with the joint face, after which a light layer of acceptable dry sand may be placed over the sealant [2][1]. When the sealed area abuts an existing transverse joint, ½ in expansion joint material is installed, and when it abuts a longitudinal joint, ¼ in pre‑formed cellular polystyrene is used, each extending at least ½ in below the bottom of the repair whenever possible; any pavement markings damaged during the operation are restored after sealing [2][1]. The sealant is then allowed to cure fully in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, with no special curing procedure beyond maintaining the minimum temperature threshold, and traffic may not resume until the sealed surface has cooled to the temperature limits specified for newly placed courses and the sealant has attained full strength [2][1].

Joint sealing on concrete pavements begins with a documented quality‑control plan that locates every longitudinal and transverse joint, followed by thorough cleaning of the joint faces after any spalling is removed [1][2]. Spalled concrete is chipped away with a hammer not exceeding thirty‑five pounds held at a thirty to sixty degree angle, or where larger areas exist a milling machine equipped with appropriate heads may be employed [2]. The exposed surfaces are then media‑blasted, swept clean with a power broom, and finally flushed with water; a final cleaning with an oil‑free compressed‑air stream of at least one hundred pounds per square inch is applied within thirty minutes of the flush to remove residual debris [2]. If the project includes diamond grinding, the joint cleaning and subsequent sealant placement are scheduled after grinding so that the sealant reservoir attains the intended shape factor and is not damaged [1]. When a new asphalt wearing course has been placed, full‑depth longitudinal cuts are made only after the surface temperature has fallen below one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit and must be completed within seven days of placing the overlay; transverse cuts follow only after all longitudinal cuts are finished [2]. The longitudinal cuts are performed with a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw (or a diamond‑tipped saw equipped with a blade guard, alignment guides, water‑cooling system and depth controls) that creates two parallel cuts per dowel‑bar slot across three slots in each wheel path [1][2]. Immediately after each cut the joint is flushed with water, and for wider joints a water‑blaster capable of delivering at least ten gallons per minute at seven thousand pounds per square inch may be used to achieve the required depth; the final compressed‑air cleaning is repeated within thirty minutes before sealant placement [2]. Backer rod or tape bond breaker is installed while the joint remains dry, and where required an expansion‑joint filler of one‑half inch or cellular polystyrene of one‑quarter inch thickness is positioned [2]. The sealant material may be hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder or another approved hot‑pour product; it is heated and maintained between two hundred sixty‑five and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit using an indirect double‑boiler system that provides positive temperature control and mechanical agitation, and batches may not remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours [2]. Application is made with a pressure wand or nozzle that lays a band approximately four inches wide (±1 in.) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1⁄32 in.), centered within one inch of the joint face; excess material is removed, any gaps are resealed, and disturbed pavement markings are restored [2]. When repair rather than overlay is required on concrete slabs, a power‑driven self‑propelled saw creates two parallel cuts per dowel‑bar slot across three slots in each wheel path; the freshly cut slots are then cleaned, epoxy‑coated dowels are positioned, and the voids are filled with a concrete patching material that must be allowed to cure [1]. Slab stabilization may involve drilling rigs coupled with pumping equipment for cement grout, hot asphalt or high‑density polyurethane; the drilling of holes is timed immediately before the stabilizing material is pumped so that both crews can share a single set of Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) devices and only one lane is treated at a time [1]. Curing requirements impose traffic restrictions: cement grout must harden for approximately twelve hours, hot asphalt or polyurethane require a minimum of thirty minutes, and the sealant itself must cure for seven days at roughly seventy‑seven degrees Fahrenheit with relative humidity between forty‑five and fifty‑five percent [1][2]. Ambient air and pavement temperatures must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit; work is prohibited when the air temperature exceeds ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless a waiver is obtained, and sawing cannot commence until the asphalt surface has cooled below one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, with temperature conditions also dictating whether set times for stabilization materials can be met [1][2]. Evaporation rates must not exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·hr, and hot‑weather conditions that raise plastic concrete above ninety degrees Fahrenheit are disallowed; in cold weather accelerated‑strength concrete may not be placed when the air temperature falls below forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Traffic control follows a one‑lane construction philosophy whenever two‑lane work would interfere with traffic flow, and all construction equipment and vehicles must be kept off the slab until second‑stage saw cuts have been sealed; new pavement cannot be opened to any traffic until every joint is sealed or an approved protective method is in place, with MPT devices coordinated for drilling and stabilizing crews [1][2]. After sawing, traffic may use the repaired area for a maximum of seventy‑two hours, but any cut wider than one‑half inch must remain closed; vehicular loads are prohibited on freshly compacted courses for at least twenty‑four hours or until the surface uniformly cools to one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit or less [2]. Cooling water, slurry and dust from cutting operations must be kept out of open lanes, and the slab cannot be reopened until the anchoring material (e.g., epoxy) has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications, as well as until cement grout, hot asphalt or polyurethane have achieved their respective hardening times [1][2]. All work is performed under Plant Maintenance activity code 711‑7147‑01 on a five‑year cycle for the concrete network, and completion must occur before the segment is officially marked “open to traffic” because mileage and cost reporting are tied to that status [1].

Quality and workmanship

Joint‑sealing inspections begin with verification of the sealant material. The sealant must be sourced from a supplier listed in the applicable AASHTO Bulletin 15 or 41 and must match the class and type prescribed for the pavement surface, whether that is hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder, an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant, or an emulsified asphalt such as PennDOT Class TACK or NTT/CNTT. For preformed neoprene or silicone seals the material must be free of pinholes, uniform in all dimensions, capable of functioning from –20 °F to +140 °F and must satisfy wall‑misalignment limits of one‑eighth inch, side‑wall bearing pressure of at least four psi at 85 % nominal width, and specified movement‑range limits. All physical properties—including maximum tensile stress at 150 % elongation, minimum elongation at peak strength, extrusion rate, specific gravity, Shore A hardness after a seven‑day cure, shelf life, ozone/UV resistance, flow, bond strength to cement mortar, and tack‑free time—must be documented in an eCAMMS laboratory report.

Preparation is inspected for complete removal of any existing sealant, neoprene compression seals, asphalt, or other foreign material so that a clean, newly exposed concrete surface remains. The cleaning depth must reach at least one‑and‑a‑half inches for saw‑cut joints and three‑quarters of an inch for formed construction joints. After mechanical removal, the joint faces and reservoir are cleaned with oil‑free, moisture‑free compressed air at a minimum pressure of 100 psi; a QC wipe test per PTM 643 is performed in the presence of a Representative on two locations of each of the first three saw‑cut joints daily and thereafter on every twentieth joint, repeating cleaning until the test passes. All surfaces must be dry and free of oil, moisture, or other contaminants before sealing. Ambient temperature is required to be between 40 °F and 90 °F unless a waiver is granted, and neither ambient air nor pavement surface temperature may be below 40 °F when work begins. Where specified, bond‑breaker tape or backer rod is placed on the bottom of the joint reservoir immediately before sealing; the tape must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the face, and the backer rod diameter must be 25–35 % larger than the opening with its top positioned one to two times the joint width plus roughly a quarter inch below the pavement surface.

During installation the sealant is heated only within the manufacturer’s safe heating range and maintained at the recommended pouring temperature—265 °F to 320 °F for hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder. No single batch may remain at pour temperature longer than six hours, and any reheating must follow the producer’s instructions. Hot‑poured seals require indirect double‑boiler equipment with positive temperature control and mechanical agitation. The sealant is applied as a continuous band four inches wide (± 1 inch) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (± 1⁄32 inch), centered within one inch of the joint line, producing a uniform film thickness ranging from one‑thirty‑second to one‑sixteenth inch over joints whose width is between one‑quarter and one inch. For hot asphalt material the sealant is spread at least six inches on each side of the joint, filling all surface voids; multiple passes may be used and excess material removed. Preformed seals are installed with a lubricant adhesive whose viscosity matches the installation temperature and whose age does not exceed nine months; the seal must not be elongated more than three percent or compressed longitudinally more than two percent, and it is placed without cutting, nicking, twisting, or otherwise damaging the seal. The joint reservoir is filled to the level shown on the standard drawings—typically one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the finished pavement surface—and no material may spill onto the pavement.

Completed work undergoes a visual acceptance check and surface‑tolerance testing. A straightedge inspection confirms that surface irregularities do not exceed three‑sixteenths of an inch; any joint exceeding this limit is rejected. The inspector verifies that the seal’s surface elevation, continuity of the film, absence of voids or contamination, backer‑rod positioning, and joint width all meet the specified criteria. The sealant must be allowed to cure fully before traffic is permitted, confirming that any anchoring epoxy has cured and that the joint satisfies all performance requirements, including wall‑misalignment, side‑wall bearing pressure, and movement limits. Both guides require that the joint and any sealant reservoir be thoroughly cleaned of all moisture, scale, dirt, curing compound, residual sealant or other contaminants before any material is placed, a condition confirmed by a representative‑witnessed wipe test. The cleaning must be accomplished with a water‑blaster capable of at least ten gallons per minute at 7 000 psi followed by drying with a compressed‑air stream of roughly 100 psi measured at the source to ensure a dry surface. The joint geometry must conform to the prescribed dimensions; longitudinal joints are routed to three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep, with larger openings permitted up to one inch wide by 1¼ inches deep, saw‑cut joints require a minimum cleaning depth of 1½ inches, and formed construction joints must be at least three‑quarters of an inch wide. For pavement/shoulder joints the reservoir is required to have a cross‑section of 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch, while transverse joints must achieve the shape factor specified in Publication 408. When a backer rod is employed it shall be sized 25–35 percent larger than the joint opening and positioned so that its top lies one to two times the joint width plus a quarter inch below the pavement surface, establishing a sealant depth‑to‑width ratio between 1:1 and 2:1. The sealing material must be the rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material prescribed in Publication 408 (such as hot‑poured PG 64S‑22 binder), heated and maintained within the temperature window of 265 °F to 320 °F, and placed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F and not above 90 °F unless an authorized exception is granted. The sealant must be applied with a pressure applicator in a continuous band centered within one inch of the joint, with a nominal width of four inches plus or minus one inch and a thickness of 1/16 inch plus or minus 1/32 inch. For joints ranging from a quarter‑inch to one inch wide the material is laid level with the pavement surface and then wiped flush so that a uniform film remains between 1/32 and 1/16 inch thick and extends one to three inches beyond each side of the joint; joints wider than one inch are filled with an Asphalt Wearing Course of 4.75 mm thickness. The final fill level is required to leave the sealant surface from one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surrounding pavement, with no overflow onto the wearing course, and any excess material must be removed and the face lightly covered with an acceptable dry sand. Dimensional tolerances are strictly enforced: bond‑breaker tape may not project more than one‑eighth inch above the joint face, steel plate intrusion must not exceed one‑quarter inch into the seal space, wall misalignment cannot exceed one‑eighth inch, and the width of the sealant may not exceed the nominal by more than 1/16 inch for seals up to 1½ inches (with progressively larger allowances for wider joints as detailed in the guides). Irregularities greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch are considered defective, and the cured sealant must be free of pinholes or uncured areas and exhibit uniform dimensions throughout. The underlying subgrade must remain stable and the existing drainage system functional so that water, chlorides and incompressible materials are prevented from infiltrating the pavement structure after sealing. Acceptance is confirmed when the Representative verifies compliance with all cleanliness standards, temperature limits, backer‑rod sizing, sealant width, thickness, fill level, dimensional tolerances and drainage functionality, and traffic may not resume until the manufacturer’s cure time has elapsed. Performance and service life

Joint sealing is a preventive‑maintenance operation that removes debris from concrete joints and installs a rubberized sealant to block water, chlorides and other incompressible materials [1][2]. When the treatment is applied at the first signs of joint distress while the pavement still appears serviceable, it preserves the slab’s load‑transfer capability, curtails faulting and spalling, and avoids more extensive rehabilitation later [1]. The process must be completed before any traffic can use a new concrete slab, requiring thorough cleaning, a QC wipe test to confirm dryness, proper backer‑rod placement, and sealant fill to the specified depth under air temperatures above 40 °F [2]. By limiting moisture ingress, sealing works together with slab stabilization to eliminate pumping action, prevent subgrade void formation, and reduce excessive deflections that would otherwise lead to corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup [1]. This protection prevents intrusion‑related cracking, spalling, and loss of load transfer, thereby preserving surface smoothness and maintaining structural functionality [1][2]. Although the procedure does not increase the design structural capacity, it sustains structural integrity, improves functional performance, and slows deterioration, which enhances overall durability [1]. Because it is a cost‑effective strategy for pavements in good condition with remaining service life, joint sealing is scheduled as routine maintenance—typically every five years on the Interstate system—to keep condition and functionality acceptable and to extend the useful service life of the concrete pavement [1]. Acceptance criteria that are not met result in defective work that must be repaired before opening to traffic, reinforcing the importance of proper sealing for achieving the intended durability and service‑life benefits [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Joint sealing on concrete pavements commonly exhibits a suite of distresses that begin with early joint deterioration such as spalling at the joint face or adjacent slab, accumulation of fines along transverse and longitudinal joints, and loss of load‑transfer capacity manifested as faulting [1]. Transverse joint faulting is especially prevalent and is often accompanied by small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder, corner breaks, overall slab settlement and can evolve into diagonal cracking and premature mid‑slab cracks when underlying subbase material is deficient [1]. The primary driver of these symptoms is water, chlorides or other incompressible materials infiltrating through inadequately sealed joints, particularly where drainage is poor; the resulting pumping action and subgrade consolidation generate voids beneath the concrete that accelerate spalling, faulting and cracking [1]. Construction‑related deficiencies also predispose joint seals to failure, including a joint face that is not clean and dry, which prevents proper bonding of the sealant material [2]. Temperature control during placement is critical; pouring sealant when air temperature falls below 40 °F or rises above 90 °F impedes adequate curing, and maintaining a single batch at pouring temperature for more than six hours compromises performance [2]. Saw cuts that define the joint must be cut within a ±¼‑inch tolerance; any deviation renders the cut defective and predisposes the seal to premature failure [2]. The seal material itself must remain within its allowable deformation limits, because stretching beyond roughly three percent elongation or compressing more than two percent destroys its ability to accommodate joint movement and leads to loss of seal integrity [2]. Additional sources of distress include improper positioning of bond‑breaker tape that extends more than one‑eighth inch up the joint face and overfilling the reservoir so that sealant spreads onto the pavement surface, both of which can cause seal failure and require corrective action before traffic is reopened [2]. When any of these conditions occur, the recommended response is to clean and reseal all affected joints, using rubberized sealants where possible, and to address severe spalling by filling with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material; load‑transfer problems are treated with dowel‑bar retrofits or slab jacking, while underlying voids are stabilized with cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam before traffic returns [1].

Monitoring and follow-up

After the sealing material has been placed, the contractor must continue to watch the joint during its cure period, checking that the joint does not open or shift before the permanent seal is installed; any opening requires removal and replacement of the backer rod and re‑sealing. This ongoing monitoring ensures the joint remains properly sealed until traffic can be allowed on the pavement. [2]

Joint sealing should be redone at the first sign of distress such as loss of sealant or early cracking because delaying allows deterioration to accelerate and may lead to more extensive repairs.
