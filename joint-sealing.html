Joint Sealing

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Joint Sealing is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
joint-sealingjoint-sealjoint-sealscleaning-and-sealingjoint-cleaning-and-sealing
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Joint sealing is a preventive‑maintenance treatment applied to deteriorating transverse, longitudinal or construction joints in concrete (and, where specified, asphalt) pavements that removes all scale, dirt, curing compounds and other foreign material before filling the joint reservoir with an approved sealant to create a watertight connection [1][2]. By blocking water, chlorides, cement mortar and other incompressible agents from entering the joint, the treatment preserves load‑transfer continuity, eliminates pumping action and subgrade void formation, and curtails slab deflection, faulting, spalling and eventual slab breakup, thereby extending service life without increasing the original structural capacity [1][2]. The procedure requires thorough cleaning and media‑blasting of joint faces, a representative‑witnessed QC wipe test, installation of an oversized backer rod and bond‑breaker tape, and placement of a rubberized or hot‑poured asphalt sealant that extends at least six inches on each side of the joint and is applied only when ambient and surface temperatures are between 40 °F and 90 °F [2].

PennDOT publications covering “Joint Sealing” also include the following subtopics: Joint Sealant.

Joint Sealing utilizes Joint Sealant as its primary material input, which provides the watertight barrier and movement accommodation necessary for the treatment’s durability. [1][2] The sealant’s specific physical properties, such as tensile stress limits and ozone resistance, define the performance standards that the sealing procedure must meet to prevent water and chloride infiltration. [2]

Joint Sealant

Purpose and treatment objectives

Joint sealing targets deteriorating pavement joints—both transverse and longitudinal—in concrete pavements, as well as any cracks or openings that have become open or compromised, allowing water, chlorides, cement mortar, debris, or other incompressible material to infiltrate the gap [1][2]. This infiltration creates pathways for moisture‑related deterioration, loss of load transfer, subgrade consolidation, pumping action beneath slabs, void formation, excessive slab deflection and stresses, which manifest as joint spalling, faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and eventual slab breakup [1][2]. The treatment is therefore intended to address the distress of open or widened joints caused by second‑stage saw cuts, concrete delamination, repaired spalls, random surface cracks in flexible pavements, and cracked joints in rigid pavements [1][2]. By cleaning joint faces, removing debris, and filling the reservoir with an appropriate sealant, the process restores joint integrity, blocks further ingress, preserves load‑transfer capability, and arrests early‑stage deterioration, thereby extending service life [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–475)

Joint sealing is classified as a preventive‑maintenance activity whose primary purpose is to keep water, chlorides, cement mortar and other incompressible materials from entering transverse, construction or expansion joints, thereby preventing the onset of pumping, faulting and cracking [1][2]. By blocking these agents the treatment slows any further loss of load‑transfer capacity and retards the progression of distress in the concrete slab [1]. The practice is typically scheduled on a regular interval—commonly every five years—to preserve the existing structure and to extend the pavement’s useful service life [1]. Implementation begins with thorough cleaning of the joint faces and reservoir, removing scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealant or any foreign material using methods that will not damage the joint, followed by a quality‑control wipe test to confirm cleanliness [2]. Once the surfaces are dry and ambient temperature is at least 40 °F, an oversized backer rod is placed and hot‑poured silicone, hot asphalt or approved emulsified asphalt sealant is introduced so that it completely fills surface voids [2]. The sealant is spread a minimum of six inches on each side of the joint to create a watertight connection, ensuring that moisture loss and contaminant intrusion are prevented [2]. By maintaining sealed joints, pavement performance is improved and its service life is prolonged without significantly increasing structural capacity [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–282), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Distress characterization and causes

Joint sealing is directed at the intentional gaps in concrete pavements—both transverse and longitudinal joints—as well as cracks that develop beneath these joints, particularly at the outer corners of slabs where small voids tend to form. At first the distress manifests as low‑severity signs of joint deterioration, such as early void formation and minor surface cracking. If these initial symptoms are not corrected, the voids expand, causing settlement and pumping of water and incompressible materials into the pavement structure. This leads to excessive slab deflection and stress, which in turn produces transverse‑joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and eventually full‑depth slab breakup. The progression moves from subtle voids to visible surface faults and structural failure, increasing the severity of the distress over time. Operationally, untreated joints diminish pavement serviceability by impairing load transfer and reducing support beneath the slabs, which can create hazardous riding conditions and pose safety risks for traffic. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92)

Joint sealing addresses a set of interrelated mechanisms that generate or accelerate distress in concrete pavement joints, chiefly the ingress of water, chlorides and other incompressible materials through deteriorated or absent sealants. [1][2] When joint faces retain cement mortar, scale, dirt, curing compound, slurry, dust, oil, grease or similar foreign material, they create channels that allow standing water after milling, rain on an open patch, or cooling water from sawing to penetrate the concrete. [2] Ineffective drainage—such as failure to excavate an outlet at the lowest point of a patch during rainfall—allows accumulated water to pressurize joints and contributes to a pumping action that drives moisture deeper into the pavement structure. [1][2] The resulting pumping, together with subgrade consolidation and settlement, can produce small voids beneath transverse joints and cracks, removing support from the slab base. [1] Loss of sub‑base or subgrade support leads to excessive slab deflection, faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately complete slab breakup, while reduced load‑transfer capacity accelerates cracking and spalling under traffic loads. [1][2] Heavy traffic loads impose additional stresses that tend to open joints further, especially when the sealant barrier has been compromised. [1][2] Climate‑related material degradation—such as frost damage, aggregate deterioration, alkali‑silica reaction or reactions associated with Vanport Limestone—weakens the concrete matrix over time and makes joints more susceptible to moisture‑induced distress. [1] An inadequate subbase or subgrade also contributes to premature mid‑slab cracking and diminishes load‑transfer capacity, thereby promoting faulting and spalling. [1][2] Poor construction practices that leave joint faces uncleaned or wet at the time of sealant placement create voids in the sealant reservoir, preventing a watertight joint and allowing continued ingress of deleterious agents. [2] Insufficient sealant application—such as uneven fill or failure to extend the material at least six inches on each side of the joint—leaves surface voids that compromise water tightness. [2] Because these mechanisms recur if left unchecked, the recommended maintenance practice is to clean joints after diamond grinding to achieve the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir and to reseal with a rubberized material at the first sign of distress, typically on a five‑year cycle. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–282), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–461)

Applicability and treatment selection

Joint sealing is suitable for Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements that are being patched rather than overlaid, provided the transverse or longitudinal joints are only just beginning to show minor distress such as early faulting or spalling while overall serviceability remains satisfactory [1]. The treatment is also applicable to both new and existing rigid concrete pavements where the joints meet specific geometric requirements, namely transverse relief joints that are saw‑cut directly over the original joint within plus or minus one inch and longitudinal cracks or joints that have been routed to the required width and depth, with all cutting and sealing completed within seven days of placing the wearing course [2]. For flexible pavements, joint sealing may be used on longitudinal joints in the wearing or binder course that are between a quarter‑inch and one inch wide when an asphalt sealant is to be placed, and on joints wider than one inch when the joint will be filled with an asphalt wearing course; notched‑wedge joints are permissible provided the nominal maximum aggregate size does not exceed 19 mm [2]. The pavement must rest on a stable subgrade and the existing drainage system must be functional so that water, chlorides, or incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the joint after sealing [1]. Traffic may not be allowed onto a newly constructed section until all joints have been sealed unless an approved protective method is used, and during sealing of transverse joints lane‑by‑lane diversion is required with traffic resuming only after the anchoring material has cured in accordance with manufacturer recommendations; under normal traffic volumes the work can proceed once these pavement, traffic, environmental and site conditions are satisfied [2]. Ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature must each be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, and sealing should not be performed when temperatures exceed roughly ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless a special exception is granted [2]. For flexible‑pavement sealants the hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement must be heated and maintained between two hundred sixty‑five and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit before application, and it may only be placed on joint faces that have been cleaned to a minimum depth of one and a half inches, free of moisture, curing compound, debris or any previous sealant [2]. Rigid‑pavement joints must also be thoroughly clean and dry; a tape bond breaker is installed on the bottom of the reservoir with backer‑rod positioned so its top lies one to two times the joint width plus a quarter inch below the surface, creating a recessed profile that is then filled to between one eighth and one quarter inch below the pavement surface [2]. When these pavement types, traffic controls, temperature limits, subgrade stability, drainage functionality, and joint‑preparation requirements are all met, joint sealing is considered appropriate across the guides [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

Joint sealing is inappropriate whenever an overlay—whether unbonded concrete or asphalt—is planned for the pavement because sealant would become trapped beneath the new surface and the joints must instead be prepared by cleaning and filling with a thin layer of asphalt wearing‑course material, or by full‑depth patching if spalling is severe. [1][2] When an overlay is anticipated, specifications also prohibit widening the joints with second‑stage saw cuts and limit sealing to expansion joints only, requiring an alternative preservation or rehabilitation technique for all other joint types. [2] If a joint exhibits extensive spalls that extend beyond the top half of the slab, partial‑depth or full‑depth joint repair is required rather than simple sealing. [1] Sealant placement must be avoided when either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless a Representative grants an exception, because curing would be inadequate outside this range. [2] The joint faces must be thoroughly cleaned and dried to the satisfaction of the Representative; any moisture, debris, or loose material makes sealing unsuitable and another repair method should be selected. [2] Sealing is limited to longitudinal joints in surface courses that are treated with hot PG‑64S‑22 asphalt binder at no additional cost; transverse joints, cracks, or non‑surface‑course joints must be addressed by a different technique. [2] When any of these conditions—planned overlay, excessive spalling, temperature outside 40–90 °F, insufficient joint cleanliness, or inappropriate joint orientation—cannot be met, the appropriate course is to defer sealing and employ an alternative maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach such as joint filling with asphalt wearing‑course material, full‑depth concrete patching, or other prescribed treatments. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for joint sealing begins with a systematic visual inspection of every transverse, longitudinal and shoulder joint, during which inspectors look for early signs such as hairline cracks, spalling, transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines on the lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow‑holes), corner breaks, or any indication that slab support has been lost [1]. When any of these distress indicators are observed, the joint is flagged for additional evaluation and a cleaning operation is performed to remove all scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealant material and other foreign matter from the joint face and reservoir before further testing [2]. The cleaned sidewalls are then media‑blasted to create a roughened surface that will accept bonding agents, a step required prior to any sealing material being placed [2]. To verify that the cleaning and blasting have produced an adequately dry and contaminant‑free surface, a concrete‑pavement joint wipe test prescribed by PTM No. 643 is administered in the presence of an authorized representative; the protocol calls for two wipe locations on each of the first three saw‑cut joints daily, followed by two locations on every joint thereafter and an additional test after every twentieth joint inspected, with sealant application permitted only when the test passes [2]. In parallel with these surface preparations, cores are extracted from the vicinity of the distressed joint as well as from random locations to assess subbase or subgrade conditions and to determine the depth of any hidden deterioration beneath the pavement surface [1]. Deflection testing and faulting measurements are then carried out to quantify slab movement at the joint, while a suite of nondestructive techniques—including ground‑penetrating radar, friction and roughness surveys, MIRA assessments, MIT‑Scan 2 for dowel bar alignment, MIT‑Scan T2 for PCCP thickness, and tensile bond testing—are employed to confirm loss of joint integrity and to locate areas where slab stabilization may be required [1]. Falling Weight Deflectometer data are also examined because they reveal concrete‑pavement joint efficiency and can pinpoint locations that will need patching or sealing [1]. Once a joint is identified as needing treatment, the shape factor of the sealant reservoir is measured to ensure compliance with the dimensions prescribed in Publication 408, Section 521, and the underlying drainage system and subgrade are inspected for proper functionality so that water infiltration will be prevented after sealing [1]. If the joint exhibits only minor distress but retains adequate support, resealing is scheduled after diamond grinding to achieve the correct sealant reservoir shape factor; more severe damage triggers deeper rehabilitation measures such as patching or reconstruction [1]. The combined results of the visual survey, core analysis, deflection and nondestructive testing, FWD evaluation, and wipe‑test outcomes constitute a quality‑control plan that identifies which joints are inadequately sealed and therefore where joint sealing treatment should be applied [2][1]. Across the guidance documents, resealing is recommended when joints first begin to show distress and is typically incorporated into a five‑year routine maintenance cycle for concrete pavements to extend service life [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–375)

Joint sealing is classified as a preventive‑maintenance operation that should be performed either on a regular preservation schedule or at the first observable sign of joint distress [1]. For concrete networks, a five‑year cycle is commonly prescribed; when a pavement reaches roughly five years in service without having been sealed, all longitudinal, transverse and shoulder joints are cleaned and resealed as part of routine upkeep [1]. The treatment is also triggered the moment early distress becomes evident, such as minor cracking, loss of sealant, faulting of transverse joints, accumulation of fines adjacent to joints, small blow‑hole depressions in shoulders, corner breaks or general slab settlement that suggest water infiltration and pumping [1]. In addition to these deterioration cues, the guides require joint sealing immediately after construction activities that alter joint geometry, specifically when a joint is widened by a second‑stage saw cut; the joint must be cleaned, dried, pass a QC wipe test, and an oversized backer rod (width plus 50 %) installed before any equipment or traffic enters the section [2]. The same immediate sealing requirement applies whenever a joint becomes involved in distress, for example when delamination propagates through a transverse or longitudinal joint or when a partial‑depth repair terminates at an existing joint; appropriate expansion material—½‑inch for transverse joints and preformed cellular polystyrene for longitudinal joints—is placed before sealant application [2]. If a joint has already failed and has not been previously rehabilitated, it must first be repaired; otherwise the existing joints are simply cleaned and resealed to protect against further intrusion [1]. After spall repairs, any remaining spalls two inches or smaller are cleaned and sealed, with the joint prepared by flushing a sealant reservoir, installing a tape bond breaker, and media‑blasting the faces prior to sealant placement [2]. For surface‑course construction, every longitudinal joint of the newly placed pavement is sealed at the time of construction provided the joint face is clean, dry, and free of loose material; a hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder is applied in a four‑inch wide band with the binder temperature maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, and placement is avoided when ambient air temperature falls below 40 °F unless an exception is granted [2]. By addressing these early‑stage conditions—whether on a scheduled cycle or at the first indication of joint deterioration—the treatment helps maintain slab support and extends pavement service life [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 11 (pp. 193–194), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–461)

Materials and repair details

Joint sealing starts with removal of deteriorated material and thorough cleaning of the joint, whether longitudinal or transverse in concrete pavement or a crack/joint in asphalt pavement. [1][2] Cleaning may be accomplished by media‑blasting, power brooming, or water‑blasting at a minimum flow of ten gallons per minute and pressure of seven thousand psi, followed by oil‑free compressed air of at least one hundred psi on vertical faces, and the surfaces must be dry and protected from curing compounds before sealant placement. [2] A quality‑control wipe test is required to verify dryness, and ambient air and pavement temperatures must be no lower than forty degrees Fahrenheit and not exceed ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless an authorized representative permits otherwise; these limits also apply to the heating and placement of the sealant material. [2] After cleaning, a joint reservoir is cut to the required dimensions, typically one inch deep and between one‑half and three‑quarters of an inch wide, centered on the existing opening, with the shape‑factor requirements of Publication 408 governing the final geometry; if the existing joint already meets or exceeds this width, it is cleaned to the prescribed depth, and any intruding steel plate (Type 2) is trimmed so that no more than one‑quarter inch remains in the cavity. [1][2] For pavement‑to‑shoulder joints a reservoir of three‑quarters of an inch by three‑quarters of an inch is constructed, while transverse joints are treated similarly with reservoirs sized to satisfy the same shape‑factor criteria. [1] Prior to sealant placement a tape bond breaker—duct tape or an acceptable self‑adhesive tape cut to the exact width of the reservoir—is placed on the bottom of the joint and must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the faces. [2] An oversized backer rod, sized twenty‑five to thirty‑five percent larger in diameter than the joint opening, is positioned so that its top sits one to two times the joint width plus a quarter inch below the surface, establishing a sealant depth‑to‑width ratio of roughly one‑to‑one to two‑to‑one. [2] For longitudinal surface‑course joints, a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is provided in Type A and B patches and corner repairs, and a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene insert may be placed extending at least one‑half inch below the bottom of any repair whenever feasible. [2] Expansion‑joint material is installed with a thickness of three‑quarters of an inch when a new joint replaces an existing one, and a half‑inch thickness for corner repairs or transverse joints in partial‑depth repairs; premolded expansion‑joint filler not less than three‑quarters of an inch thick is trimmed to match gutter cross‑sections at structures. [2] The sealant material selected depends on joint width and pavement type; joints up to one inch wide are sealed with hot‑pour PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder sealant heated between two hundred sixty‑five and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, while openings from a quarter‑inch to one inch may be sealed with an approved asphalt sealant listed in Bulletins 15 or 41. [2] Joints larger than one inch require placement of Asphalt Wearing Course 4.75 mm filler, as specified for severely spalled joints before an overlay; this same 4.75 mm material may be used to fill badly spalled joints and interior spalls per Publication 408 Section 469. [1][2] Rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521, conforming to ASTM D6690 Type II for asphalt pavement joints or Type IV for all other applications, is an approved product and may be used in either rubberized or asphalt joint sealant form. [1][2] Silicone joint‑sealing material, a low‑modulus non‑sag one‑part formulation from a Bulletin 15 manufacturer, is also acceptable where specified. [2] The sealant is poured or pumped into the reservoir and filled to a level that leaves the finished surface between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement, as shown on standard drawings; hot‑asphalt sealant is applied with a pressure applicator wand or nozzle laying a straight band approximately four inches wide (± one inch) and one‑sixteenth inch thick (± one thirty‑second inch), centered within one inch of the joint. [2][1] Excess material is trimmed, any gaps are resealed, and damaged pavement markings are repaired at no additional cost; protective covers or curing compounds may be applied after sealing. [2][1] Preformed neoprene compression seals may be used with a lubricant adhesive, provided they are not elongated more than three percent or compressed longitudinally more than two percent and function between minus twenty degrees Fahrenheit and plus one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit. [2] Additional filler options such as closed‑cell polyethylene joints with a minimum compressive strength of five psi, or polyurethane foam joint fillers supplied in lengths of at least eight feet, widths from four seven‑sixteenths to four three‑quarters inches and depths from seven three‑quarters to thirteen inches, may be employed when laboratory approval reports are available. [2] Where tie bars are required, epoxy bonding compound is injected into drilled holes using a caulking gun to provide about an inch of coverage at the surface. [2] All original longitudinal and transverse joints intersecting a repair are re‑established and sealed according to Section 501.3(n); sealant is applied only to the joint itself, not to the perimeter of any new patch, and any saw cuts extending beyond the patch limits are also sealed. [1][2] Saw cutting may be performed to form a reservoir typically one inch deep by one‑half inch wide (or four inches wide full depth for certain applications), and the joint faces must be protected from exposure to curing compound or cleaned thoroughly before sealant installation. [2][1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–475), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 562–563), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 687–689)

The performance of a joint‑sealing treatment depends first on the condition of the concrete that forms the joint, including surface preparation such as diamond grinding to achieve the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir and to protect the material from mechanical damage [1]. Joints exhibiting high percentages of patching, low compressive strength, excessive air content, or aggregate durability problems are less likely to develop a durable bond [1]. In addition, the joint faces must be completely free of oil, moisture, scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material, grease or other foreign substances before sealant placement, which is achieved by media‑blasting all vertical faces and cleaning with an oil‑free, moisture‑free high‑pressure air stream of at least 100 psi (or water blasting at a minimum of ten gallons per minute at 7 000 psi) followed by drying and verification with a wipe test [2]. Any spalls, delaminations, exposed dowel bars or surface irregularities greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch must be repaired, and cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch filled with an approved non‑shrink grout to provide a sound bond [2]. The joint opening is prepared to specified dimensions—typically a longitudinal joint cut to three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep, or a full‑depth transverse opening four inches wide across the pavement width—and cleaned to a minimum depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches, removing existing concrete to at least that depth [2]. A backer rod sized twenty‑five to thirty‑five percent larger than the joint opening (or oversized up to fifty percent) is placed so its top lies one to two times the joint width plus a quarter inch below the surface; for transverse joints a half‑inch expansion‑joint material and for longitudinal joints a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene are installed, each extending at least half an inch below the repair [2]. A bond‑breaker tape is positioned on the bottom of the reservoir but must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the joint face [2]. The sealant material must meet the specifications of Publication 408 (Sections 512 and 521), requiring a rubberized joint‑sealing product, or an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant that satisfies manufacturer requirements for tensile stress at 150 % elongation (ASTM D412, Die C), durometer hardness, ozone and ultraviolet resistance, flow characteristics, shelf life, tack‑free time, and the ability to accommodate joint movement [1][2]. Minimum bond strength to cement mortar is required, with priming when specified, and a side‑wall bearing pressure of four pounds per square inch at eighty‑five percent nominal width must be achieved [2]; the sealant also must be compatible with the pavement surface course, using the same class and type of hot asphalt as the wearing course (commonly PG 64S‑22) or an approved emulsified asphalt such as PennDOT Class TACK or NTT/CNTT, sourced from bulletins governing compatibility (e.g., Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41) [2][1]. The sealant is heated and maintained within the safe heating range indicated on its shipping container—typically 265 °F to 320 °F for PG 64S‑22—and each batch may remain at pouring temperature no longer than six hours before reheating [2]. Placement is permitted only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and not above ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless a written exception is granted, and the sealant is applied with a pressure‑type applicator to form a uniform band approximately four inches wide (± 1 in) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (± 1/32 in), centered within one inch of the joint and filled to a level one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface [2]. Compatibility also requires that the pavement not be slated for an overlay, because sealing is discouraged on sections scheduled for such treatment, and subgrade stability together with functional drainage are essential to keep water, chlorides, and incompressible materials from entering the joint and compromising the sealant [1]. Early intervention is critical; resealing should be undertaken when joints are only just beginning to show distress—such as minor spalling, hair‑line cracking where aggregate interlock remains, or limited faulting—so that serviceability can be preserved without deeper rehabilitation, with a recommended resealing interval of five years for concrete pavements [1]. Existing pavement conditions that undermine sealing include premature mid‑slab cracking caused by an inadequate subbase, joint spalling, and joint faulting identified in routine surveys; these distress types tend to recur if not addressed promptly [1].

Treatment procedures

Joint sealing starts with thorough cleaning of the joint faces and any reservoir that will receive sealant, removing all loose material, existing sealants, asphalt, curing compounds, temporary agents and other foreign matter [1][2]. For saw‑cut joints the cleaning must extend at least 1½ in down, while for formed construction joints it must reach a minimum of ¾ in; original joints are typically cleaned to these depths before any further work [2]. After mechanical removal the joint is flushed with water, swept with a power broom and then dried with oil‑free compressed air at about 100 psi, and a QC wipe test is performed immediately before sealant placement to verify that no dust or debris remain [2]. Cracks that have been opened are routed to roughly three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep (up to 1¼ in deep for wider cracks), providing a uniform profile for sealing [2]. A sealant reservoir is then formed to the dimensions specified in the guides, typically a 0.75 in × 0.75 in pocket for pavement‑shoulder joints and shaped according to the required shape factor for transverse joints; some specifications also require sawing a joint opening four inches wide full depth as shown on standard drawings [1][2]. When the design calls for it, a bond‑breaker tape is placed on the bottom of the reservoir, ensuring that it does not rise more than 1/8 in above the face [2]. For wide joints an oversized backer rod is installed—generally a ½‑in expansion joint material for transverse joints or a ¼‑in pre‑formed cellular polystyrene for longitudinal joints—selected 25–35 % larger than the opening so that its top sits one to two times the joint width plus roughly ¼ in below the pavement surface, providing at least a ½‑in recess [2]. The sealant material may be the rubberized or rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material specified in Publication 408, or an approved hot‑pour, silicone, or hot asphalt binder such as PG 64S‑22, heated to the manufacturer’s safe heating temperature (typically between 265 °F and 320 °F) and poured within the recommended temperature range indicated on the shipping container; batches must not remain at pouring temperature longer than six hours without reheating [1][2]. Placement is permitted only when ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, with an upper limit generally of 90 °F unless a special exception is granted [2]. Using a pressure wand or nozzle the sealant is laid in a straight band approximately four inches wide (±1 in) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (±½ in, with allowable variation of ±1/32 in), centered within one inch of the joint and extending a minimum of six inches on each side [2]. The band is poured to a level 1/8 inch to ¼ inch below the pavement surface, completely filling all voids to create a watertight connection [2][1]. After placement any excess material is trimmed and the sealed area is immediately covered with a light layer of acceptable dry sand; the joint face is then wiped flush leaving a thin film roughly 1/32‑to‑1/16 inch thick and one to three inches wide [2]. Missed spots are resealed before the work is considered complete, and the seal must be protected until fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications; traffic may not reenter the area until the prescribed cure time has elapsed [2][1]. The entire sealing operation should be finished within seven days after the wearing course is placed, and it is performed only when the pavement is being patched rather than overlaid, being most effective when distress is just beginning [1][2].

Joint sealing operations commence only after spall repairs are completed and the existing transverse and longitudinal joints have been located, referenced, and prepared for work [2]. The first field activity is a comprehensive cleaning of those joints to remove loose material, dust, moisture, and other contaminants before any cutting or sealant placement occurs [1][2]. When the project includes diamond grinding, the cleaning and subsequent sealant application are scheduled after grinding so that the sealant reservoir attains the intended shape factor and is not damaged by the grinding process [1]. Sawing or grinding of joints is performed with a concrete saw or grinder equipped with a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, water‑cooling system, alignment guides, and depth controls; longitudinal cuts must be at least 1½ inches deep while formed construction joints require a minimum depth of three‑quarters of an inch, and larger cracks may be opened up to four inches wide and full depth [2]. Immediately after each cut the joint is flushed with water using a water‑blaster capable of delivering at least ten gallons per minute at 7 000 psi, then the removed material is cleared and the faces are cleaned with an oil‑free compressed‑air stream of no less than 100 psi (or a hot‑air lance) to eliminate residual dust and moisture, ensuring that the joint faces are completely dry before sealant placement [2]. For repairs that involve dowel‑bar slots, a power‑driven self‑propelled saw creates two parallel cuts per slot across three slots in each wheel path; the freshly cut slots are then cleaned, epoxy‑coated dowels are positioned, and the voids are filled with a concrete patching material that must be allowed to cure [1]. Heavy‑duty membranes may be incorporated into the sealant system where required, and slab stabilization often involves drilling rigs coupled with pumping equipment for cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane; the drilling is timed immediately before the stabilizing material is pumped so that both crews can share a single set of Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) devices while only one lane is treated at a time [1]. After cleaning, a backer rod 25–35 percent larger than the opening or a premolded expansion‑joint piece about three‑quarters of an inch thick is placed to a recessed depth typically one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, and a tape bond‑breaker is applied on the bottom of the reservoir without extending more than one‑eighth inch up the face [2]. The type of sealant determines the application equipment: hot‑poured asphalt binders such as PG 64S‑22 are heated in an indirect double‑boiler system with positive temperature control and mechanical agitation, maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F and applied with a pressure applicator that lays a straight band approximately four inches wide (±1 in) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1⁄32 in); silicone sealants are placed using an air‑powered caulking gun with a 1/8‑inch orifice operating near 90 psi, while additional tooling such as lightweight chipping hammers not exceeding 35 lb and milling machines fitted with V‑heads may be used for edge preparation [2][1]. Environmental constraints are strict: neither ambient air temperature nor pavement surface temperature may be below 40 °F, the upper ambient limit is generally 90 °F unless a written exception is granted, relative humidity should be maintained between 45 % and 55 %, and during placement the surrounding surface must remain dry and within the specified temperature range; after sealant application the pavement surface must be cooled to 140 °F or less before any vehicular loads are permitted, and the cured sealant must function from –20 °F up to +140 °F while the concrete must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3 000 psi before traffic can be restored [2][1]. Curing requirements impose traffic restrictions: slabs treated with cement grout cannot be reopened until the grout has hardened, which typically requires about twelve hours, whereas hot asphalt or polyurethane require a minimum of thirty minutes, and these set times are dependent on ambient temperature conditions; consequently sealing operations are scheduled when temperatures permit the required set times [1]. In addition, no construction equipment or public vehicles may enter the slab until all joints have been sealed unless an approved protective method is in place, saw cuts are made only within the lane that contains the joint, lanes may be diverted while referencing and sealing occur, and for joints abutting opposing traffic the adjoining asphalt must be placed within one working day (two days for same‑direction traffic) [2]. After sealant placement vehicular loads are prohibited for twenty‑four hours or until the surface uniformly cools to 140 °F and the required concrete compressive strength is verified, saw cuts may be opened to traffic after a maximum of seventy‑two hours provided the cut width does not exceed one‑half inch, and any excavated repair areas must be patched within forty‑eight hours (or sooner if work extends over weekends or holidays) [2][1]. All joint sealing activities are performed under Plant Maintenance activity code 711‑7147‑01 on a five‑year cycle for the concrete network, and completion must occur before the segment is officially marked “open to traffic” because mileage and cost reporting are tied to that status [1].

Quality and workmanship

A joint‑sealing inspection must verify that the sealant material is an approved product—typically hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder or an authorized emulsified asphalt—sourced from a Bulletin 15 or 41 list and accompanied by permanent batch identification, cure date, and a laboratory test report. The sealant must be heated within the manufacturer’s specified range of 265 °F to 320 °F, not held at pouring temperature for more than six hours, and placed only when both ambient air and pavement temperatures are at least 40 °F (and below any upper limit such as 90 °F unless a waiver is granted). Physical‑property requirements documented in the supplier’s report—including tensile stress at 150 % elongation (ASTM D412), minimum elongation, extrusion rate, specific gravity, durometer hardness, shelf life, ozone and UV resistance, flow, bond strength to cement mortar, tack‑free time, movement capability, adhesion, and a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F with 45–55 % relative humidity—must be confirmed. Any lubricant adhesive used must retain the proper viscosity at installation temperature and must not be older than nine months from its manufacture date.

Preparation begins with a thorough cleaning of each joint face and the adjoining reservoir to remove all scale, dirt, curing compounds, temporary sealing material, oil, moisture, or any other foreign matter. Cleaning methods may include power brooming, media blasting, a high‑pressure water flush immediately after sawing, followed by an oil‑free, moisture‑free compressed‑air stream measured at 100 psi at the source. The vertical faces must be cleaned to a minimum depth of one and a half inches, and a representative‑witnessed quality‑control wipe test is performed on two locations of each joint—on the first three sawcut joints of the day and thereafter on every twentieth joint—to confirm that the surfaces are free of contaminants. A bond‑breaker tape is placed on the bottom of the reservoir without extending more than one‑eighth inch up the face, and a backer rod or cellular filler is installed as required: a ½‑inch expansion‑joint filler for transverse joints or a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene for longitudinal joints, each extending at least half an inch below the bottom of the repair. The backer rod diameter must be 25 % to 35 % larger than the joint opening and positioned so its top sits one to two times the joint width plus a quarter inch below the surface.

During installation the inspector confirms that the sealant is applied with a pressure applicator capable of delivering a continuous, straight band approximately four inches wide (± 1 inch) and 1/16‑inch thick (± 1/32 inch), centered within one inch of the joint. The band must extend at least six inches on each side of the joint, and the reservoir is filled to a level between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, leaving a thin film flush with the surface that is between 1/32 and 1/16 inch thick and spans one to three inches in width. Overflow onto the riding surface is prohibited; any excess material is removed promptly and any thin spots are resealed. Preformed neoprene or silicone seals, when used, must show no damage, with elongation not exceeding three percent and compression not exceeding two percent. Wall alignment must be within one‑eighth inch and side‑wall bearing pressures must meet the specified movement‑range criteria.

The completed joint is inspected for full coverage, uniform film thickness, absence of pinholes or uncured areas, and overall dimensional uniformity. A twelve‑foot straightedge is run across the pavement; irregularities greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch are deemed defective and must be corrected. The inspector also verifies that pavement markings damaged during the work have been restored, that the seal remains clean through periodic wipe tests at the prescribed intervals, and that traffic is not reopened until the seal has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Any deviation from these material, preparation, installation, or post‑installation criteria—such as improper routing dimensions, insufficient cleaning or drying, temperature violations, incorrect fill depth, over‑spreading, misalignment, or premature opening to traffic—is recorded as defective work. A satisfactory joint seal is achieved only after the joint faces have been thoroughly cleaned and dried to a condition free of oil, moisture, dust, or any loose material, with the cleanliness verified by a QC wipe test performed in accordance with PTM No. 643; if contaminants are detected the cleaning must be repeated using either a water‑blaster delivering at least ten gallons per minute at 7 000 psi or an oil‑free compressed‑air stream of no less than 100 psi until the test passes, and the Representative must deem the surfaces satisfactory [1][2]. The existing drainage system must remain functional so that water, chlorides, and incompressible materials are prevented from infiltrating the pavement structure, a condition required for the seal to be considered effective [1][2]. Sealing work may proceed only when ambient air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F (or an approved waiver is obtained) and the pavement surface temperature is at least 40 °F, ensuring that the material can achieve proper adhesion and cure [2]. The sealant must be an approved hot‑pour asphalt cement such as PG 64S‑22, silicone, or preformed neoprene, heated within the manufacturer’s specified range of 265 °F to 320 °F; it may not remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours per batch without reheating, and each lot must be accompanied by an eCAMMS laboratory approval report confirming tensile strength, elongation, durometer, ozone/UV resistance, and a side‑wall bearing pressure of no less than four psi at eighty‑five percent of the nominal width [1][2]. Joint preparation follows strict dimensional limits: longitudinal cracks are routed to a maximum width of three‑quarters inch with a depth of one inch (wider openings may be cut to one inch wide by 1¼ inch deep), full‑depth construction joints are sawed to an exact four‑inch width, and cleaning depths must reach at least one‑and‑a‑half inches for longitudinal joints and three‑quarters of an inch for constructed joints; any second‑stage saw cuts creating or widening the reservoir must be located within plus or minus one inch of the existing joint and sealed before traffic is allowed [2]. A sealant reservoir is then built to the required dimensions and shape factor, such as a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir for pavement/shoulder joints or the appropriate shape factor for transverse joints, with an oversized backer rod sized twenty‑five to thirty‑five percent larger than the opening so that its top lies one to two times the joint width plus a quarter inch below the surface [1][2]. The sealant is placed as a continuous band centered within one inch of the joint, having a nominal width of four inches plus or minus one inch and a thickness of one‑sixteenth inch plus or minus one‑thirty‑second inch, extending several inches on each side; after placement excess material is removed so that the sealant surface sits between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement crown with no spill onto the wearing course, and a light layer of acceptable dry sand is applied over the sealed face [2]. Bond‑breaker tape at the bottom of the reservoir may not project more than one‑eighth inch above the face, steel plate intrusion into the cavity is limited to a quarter inch, and preformed neoprene compression seals must not be elongated more than three percent or compressed longitudinally more than two percent, with wall misalignment in the compressed condition limited to one‑eighth inch and overall dimensions not exceeding one‑sixteenth inch over nominal for seals up to one‑and‑a‑half inches wide; silicone seals must satisfy the full suite of ASTM‑based performance criteria specified in Section 705.4 [2]. Joint fillers are installed according to orientation, using half‑inch expansion joint material for transverse joints and quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene for longitudinal joints, each extending at least a half inch below the bottom of the repair when feasible [2]. Final acceptance is confirmed by a visual inspection that verifies all dimensional tolerances, fill level, tape projection, side‑wall bearing pressure, and that the QC wipe test remains satisfactory; the seal must be free of pinholes, uncured areas, and uniformly dimensioned, indicating that the treatment meets the required performance criteria [1][2].

Performance and service life

Joint sealing is a preventive‑maintenance operation that removes debris from concrete joints and installs a sealant to block water, chlorides and other incompressible materials [1][2]. The procedure begins with thorough cleaning of the joint faces to eliminate scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealants and any foreign material before the sealant is placed [2]. After cleaning, an approved sealant—commonly a rubberized material or hot/emulsified asphalt of the same class as the wearing course—is applied at a controlled rate to a minimum width of six inches on each side so that all surface voids are completely filled and a watertight joint is achieved [1][2]. By creating a watertight seal, water, cement mortar and other incompressible substances are prevented from entering the joint, which preserves load‑transfer continuity and reduces the development of cracking, faulting and spalling [1][2]. The elimination of moisture pathways also curtails pumping action, subgrade void formation and excessive slab deflections that would otherwise lead to corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup [1]. Although joint sealing does not increase the original structural capacity, it sustains the existing integrity, improves functional performance, and slows deterioration, thereby enhancing overall durability [1]. Because the treatment maintains pavement condition and functionality, it is scheduled as a routine preventive maintenance activity—typically every five years on interstate systems—for pavements that are still serviceable but showing early signs of joint distress [1][2]. Performing sealing at the first indication of joint distress allows the pavement to remain in acceptable condition, supports safe traffic operation before construction equipment or traffic re‑enter, and extends the expected service life of the concrete pavement [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–282), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Joint sealing on concrete pavements is prone to several recurring distresses including early joint deterioration such as spalling at the joint face or adjacent slab, accumulation of fines along transverse and longitudinal joints, loss of load‑transfer capacity manifested as faulting, and development of blow‑hole depressions in shoulders. Transverse joint faulting is especially common and may be accompanied by corner breaks, overall slab settlement, diagonal cracking, and premature mid‑slab cracks when subbase material is deficient. Inadequate sealing that permits water, chlorides, or other incompressible materials to infiltrate joints—particularly where drainage is poor—creates pumping action and subgrade consolidation that generate voids beneath the concrete and accelerate these symptoms. The most effective response is early intervention; guidelines advise cleaning and resealing all joints at the first sign of distress, with emphasis on rehabilitating any failed transverse joints before they are left untreated. Preferred sealants are rubberized materials, while severely spalled joints may be filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course to restore surface continuity. When faulting or poor load transfer is observed, dowel‑bar retrofits or slab jacking are employed, and joint sealing should be combined with slab stabilization using cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam, allowing appropriate cure times (approximately 12 hours for grout and at least 30 minutes for asphalt or polyurethane) before reopening to traffic. Joint sealing treatments also fail when the joint is not properly prepared; failure to remove scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealant material, moisture, or other contaminants prevents adequate bonding of the sealant. Placement of poured sealant at ambient or surface temperatures below 40 °F inhibits proper cure and leads to loss of adhesion, so work should not be performed when either temperature is under this limit unless an exception is granted. Using an undersized backer rod—or omitting the required oversized rod whose width must be at least 150 % of joint depth—allows sealant migration onto the pavement face, while over‑filling so that the surface rises within one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch of the pavement creates extrusion and premature leakage. Opening the joint to traffic before the anchoring material has fully cured according to manufacturer recommendations results in early distress. Preformed neoprene or silicone seals that are stretched more than 3 % of their original length or compressed more than 2 % lose integrity and must be replaced, and improper drilling that penetrates through the slab bottom can require slab removal and patching, representing additional failure mechanisms. Routine maintenance schedules such as resealing every five years on interstate systems help mitigate these recurring distresses and extend pavement service life. [1][2]

Monitoring and follow-up

After the joint sealing work is finished, the contractor and inspector must continue to observe the joint throughout the curing period until a permanent seal is placed, confirming that the oversized backer rod stays correctly positioned. In the presence of the Representative, a QC wipe test prescribed by PTM No. 643 is performed immediately before the sealant is applied to verify that both faces of the joint are clean, dry and free of debris; for the first three saw‑cut joints two locations are tested each day, and thereafter testing continues at two locations on every joint and subsequently at intervals of every twenty joints. Once the sealant has cured, the inspector checks that the material completely fills any surface voids and then runs a 12‑foot straightedge across the joint and any areas identified by the Representative as potentially deficient; any irregularity greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch is deemed defective and must be corrected before acceptance. A visual‑tactile concrete‑pavement joint wipe test, as outlined in the quality‑control plan, is also performed to confirm that the sealant remains intact and uncontaminated. The QC plan specifies regular sampling and testing frequencies, and any failure observed during these wipe tests or straightedge checks triggers the predefined corrective action points. [2]
